### Factual and historical claims

- The wars of the twentieth century largely wiped away the past and transformed inequality. Wealth inequality is now close to regaining or surpassing its historical highs (p. 342).
- The 2008 crisis was less severe than 1929–1935. Output fell no more than 5 points in 2009, against a quarter in 1929–1935, because governments and central banks prevented financial collapse (p. 343).
- Pragmatic monetary policy did not address the structural causes: lack of financial transparency and rising inequality. 2008 was "the first crisis of the globalized patrimonial capitalism" (p. 344).
- Roosevelt raised the top federal income tax rate from 25 percent under Hoover to over 80 percent. The Obama-era debate was about moving from about 35 to about 40 percent (p. 344).
- Taxes took less than 10 percent of national income in the US, Britain, France and Sweden before 1914 (p. 345).
- From 1920 to 1980 the tax share rose by a factor of at least 3 or 4 (more than 5 in the Nordic countries). It stabilised in 1980–2010 at just over 30 percent in the US, about 40 percent in Britain, 45 in Germany, 50 in France and nearly 55 in Sweden (p. 346).
- So in tax and spending terms the state has "never" been larger, and talk of a "return of the state" is misleading. Deregulation and privatisation did reduce the state's role on other dimensions (p. 346).
- Social functions consume a quarter to a third of national income, split about evenly between education and health on one side and replacement incomes and transfers on the other (p. 347).
- Education and health cost 10–15 percent of national income. Public spending covers about three-quarters of their cost in Europe and half in the US (p. 347).
- Pensions cost 12–13 percent of national income in continental Europe and 6–7 percent in the US and UK. Public pensions are the main income source for at least two-thirds of retirees (pp. 347-348).
- Public pensions eradicated old-age poverty, which was "endemic as recently as the 1950s". They are the "third social revolution", after education and health (p. 348).
- Unemployment insurance costs 1–2 percent of national income and income support less than 1 percent. Income support is the most contested despite its small size, and stigma and complexity deter take-up (p. 348).
- The growth of the fiscal state "basically reflects the constitution of a social state" (p. 348).
- In the nineteenth century, the regimes born of the US and French Revolutions concentrated mainly on protecting property rights (p. 350).
- Broad consensus, especially in Europe, supports the social state. No major force proposes returning to 10–20 percent of national income in tax, and none proposes continuing 1930–1980 growth rates, which would mean 70–80 percent by 2050–2060 (p. 350).
- Education and health account for about 20 percent of employment and GDP in developed economies, more than all of industry (p. 351).
- Despite mass education, earned income inequality did not fall, and intergenerational mobility shows no long-run rise and may have fallen recently (pp. 352-353).
- Mobility is highest in the Nordic countries and lowest in the US. The US correlation coefficient is about two-thirds higher than Sweden's, with France, Germany and Britain in between (p. 353).
- In the US, the share of college degrees earned by children of bottom-half parents stayed at 10–20 percent in 1970–2010, while for top-quartile children it rose from 40 to 80 percent (p. 353).
- Harvard parents' average income is about $450,000, the average of the top 2 percent. Sciences Po parents' is about 90,000 euros, roughly the top 10 percent (p. 354).
- Most continental European tuition is under 500 euros. Quebec's planned rise from $2,000 to nearly $4,000 was reversed after a 2012 student strike (p. 354).
- French grandes écoles spend more public money on more advantaged students (Bourdieu and Passeron) (p. 354).
- PAYGO's return was close to 5 percent in postwar continental Europe and is about 1.5 percent now. r is expected at 4–5 percent (p. 356).
- Wage growth is "5–10 times less volatile" than the return on capital (p. 357). Note 45: interwar savers ruined by markets were the reason PAYGO replaced funded schemes, such as France's laws of 1910 and 1928 (p. 487).
- In 1970–1980 poor countries (Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia) taxed 10–15 percent of national income and intermediate ones 15–20 percent. Poor-country tax levels then fell, to a little over 10 percent in the 1990s, while rich-country levels rose (pp. 358-359).
- The fall in poor countries' revenue in 1980–1990 was largely due to lower customs duties, which had been about 5 percent of national income (Cagé and Gadenne) (p. 359).
- China has a broad income tax raising substantial revenue. India has had more difficulty (p. 360).

### Normative claims and principles

- The ideal policy is a progressive global tax on capital, which is "no doubt a utopian ideal". A regional, especially European, version is the fallback (p. 342).
- Confiscatory top income tax rates "deserve" to be revived, especially in the US (p. 344). A capital tax suits the twenty-first century better than the income tax (p. 344).
- Both the antimarket and the antistate camps are "partly correct": new instruments are needed to control finance, and tax and transfer systems need modernising (p. 345).
- Citizens will not accept new supranational tools unless existing ones are seen to work (p. 345).
- Modern redistribution rests on rights and equal access to fundamental goods, not on explicit transfers from rich to poor (pp. 348-349).
- Article 1 of the 1789 Declaration puts the burden of proof on inequality. "Common utility" can reasonably be read as allowing inequalities only if they serve all, especially the most disadvantaged, which is close to Rawls and Sen (p. 349).
- Abstract principles command a "partly artificial" consensus. Real disagreements about means, the extent of rights and the line between luck and effort can be settled only by "democratic deliberation and political confrontation" (pp. 349-350).
- "Taxation is neither good nor bad in itself" (p. 350). A much larger tax share is neither realistic nor desirable for the foreseeable future. Low growth makes durable tax increases hard, private consumption needs are legitimate, and large public sectors face organisational problems (pp. 350-352).
- The agenda is modernising and consolidating the social state, not dismantling or expanding it (p. 352).
- Tuition fees create "an unacceptable inequality of access". Publicly financed incentives to autonomous universities, on the Nordic model, are preferable to income-related fees or income-contingent loans (p. 355).
- Transparency about selection and parental income should come first. Meritocratic discourse often justifies existing inequalities (p. 355).
- PAYGO should remain part of the ideal social state everywhere. Moving to a funded system is "totally unthinkable" in an extreme form, because of the transition generation and volatility (pp. 356-357).
- Retirement ages should rise in a way that distinguishes intellectual from arduous work (p. 357). A unified scheme of individual accounts should replace fragmented regimes (p. 358). People of modest means should be encouraged to save (p. 358).
- Building a fiscal and social state is part of state-building and development. The rich countries and international organisations share blame for poor countries' falling tax capacity (p. 359).
