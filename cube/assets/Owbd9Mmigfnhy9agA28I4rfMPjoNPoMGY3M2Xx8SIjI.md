**Framing**

- Taxation is generally preferable to debt "in terms of justice and efficiency" because debt must be repaid to those with the means to lend; better to tax the wealthy than borrow from them (p. 399).
- Rich-country public debt averages about one year of national income (90% of GDP), the highest since 1945; emerging-economy debt is about 30% of GDP (p. 400).
- Hence debt is a question of distribution between public and private actors; "The rich world is rich, but the governments of the rich world are poor" (p. 400). Europe has the most private wealth and the hardest debt problem, "a strange paradox" (p. 400).

**Reducing the debt**

- Three methods, combinable: capital tax, inflation, austerity. Exceptional capital tax best; inflation useful and historically the usual route; prolonged austerity worst, and Europe's current course (p. 400).
- European national wealth is about six years of national income, mostly private; public assets roughly equal public debt; Europe's net foreign position is near balance, so European households own Europe's public debt (pp. 400-401).
- Selling all public assets would roughly pay off the debt, but should be "dismissed out of hand" because the social state must own its schools, hospitals and so on; still, interest on debt weighs on the exchequer much like rent (p. 401).
- A flat 15% tax on private wealth would yield nearly a year of national income and wipe out the debt (p. 401).
- The capital tax is equivalent to repudiation except that (1) its incidence is predictable while a haircut's is not and can trigger bank panic, and (2) it can be graduated by fortune size (pp. 401-402).
- Haircuts do not penalise those who took the risks, since assets are constantly traded and the largest holders can restructure in time (p. 402).
- A progressive exceptional tax generalises the logic of the EU's €100,000 deposit guarantee and must cover all assets, including unlisted shares, to reach the wealthiest (p. 402).
- Realistic target: cut debt by 20% of GDP (90% to 70%) with one-time rates of 0/10/20% (ten times the annual 0/1/2% schedule), or with the annual schedule for ten years; France's 1945 exceptional tax ran from 0 to 25% (p. 403).
- Inflation of 5% rather than 2% cuts the real debt/GDP ratio by more than 15% (p. 403). Historically most large public debts, particularly Europe's in the twentieth century, were reduced this way: France 13% and Germany 17% average inflation 1913–1950 (pp. 403-404).
- Other central banks (Fed, Bank of Japan, Bank of England) are trying to raise inflation targets; the ECB is "by far the most averse" (p. 404).
- Without a capital tax or inflation, with zero inflation, 2% growth and a 1% deficit, it takes 20 years to cut debt/GDP by 20 points; worse conditions mean 30–40 years (p. 404).
- Nineteenth-century Britain ran primary surpluses of 2–3% of GDP for a century (1815–1914) to pay down Napoleonic war debt and spent more on debt interest than on education; Piketty speculates this may explain later British decline (p. 404).
- Starting from about 100% rather than 200% of GDP, and with 2% target inflation, European austerity might last "only" ten or twenty years (p. 405).
- Inflation is "at best a very imperfect substitute" for a capital tax: hard to control (France over 50% a year 1945–1948; German prices multiplied by 100 million in 1923), and its effect disappears once expected (p. 405).
- Concession: idealised inflation taxes idle cash and spares real investors and debtors, a "tax on idle capital" with "some truth" to it (pp. 405-406); but it does not stop large diversified portfolios earning high returns (p. 406).
- The French view that inflation could soak "German rentiers" is "naïve and preposterous"; a great inflation would hurt people of modest means while sparing real estate and stock fortunes (p. 406). A little more inflation is preferable to a little more austerity (p. 406).

**Central banks**

- Under the gold standard the price level depended on the hazards of gold and silver discoveries; a return is "highly unlikely" (p. 406).
- With fiat money, central bank money creation is potentially unlimited and so must be strictly regulated (pp. 406-407).
- The Depression trauma produced a universal consensus that central banks must act as lenders of last resort (p. 407).
- Friedman and Schwartz's *Monetary History* (1963) blamed the Fed for the Depression and drew the political conclusion that a well-run Fed, not a welfare state, was all capitalism needed; this helped create the climate for the "conservative revolution of 1979–1980" (pp. 407-408).
- Piketty: a well-run central bank and a social state with progressive taxation are "complements rather than substitutes" (p. 408).
- Central banks "do not create wealth as such; they redistribute it": their operations are loans with matching assets and liabilities (p. 408). Their dynamic effect on wealth can be positive or negative (pp. 408-409).
- Central banks can prevent failures but cannot make firms invest, households consume, or set the inflation rate (p. 409).
- Their strength is speed; their weakness is limited ability to target, so balance sheets should be limited. Since 2008 they roughly doubled (Fed and BoE from 10 to over 20% of GDP; ECB from 15 to 30%), still modest against private wealth of 500–600% of GDP (p. 410).
- Central banks lack "democratic legitimacy" to buy firms, finance energy transition or run the economy; industrial and educational policy belong to states. "The problem is not one of technical impossibility but of democratic governance" (pp. 410-411).

**Cyprus**

- Bank regulation and capital taxation are complementary (p. 411). Neither the Troika nor member states have a financial cadaster (p. 412).
- Greece cannot tax its rich alone because they move money abroad, often within Europe, and has had to sell public assets at fire-sale prices (p. 412).
- Cyprus (March 2013): a banking sector swollen by foreign, notably Russian, deposits; no published statistics on depositors (p. 412).
- The Troika's proposed deposit levy (6.75% up to €100,000, 9.9% above) had illusory progressivity and an undefined base limited to deposits, so would likely have been "extremely regressive" (p. 413). It was rejected by Cypriots; the replacement exempted deposits under €100,000 on obscure bank-by-bank terms (p. 413).
- Lesson: a progressive capital tax can work as an exceptional levy, potentially at high rates, in banking crises; what is "deeply shocking" is that authorities did not equip themselves to share the burden progressively (p. 413). The excuse was missing data; the obstacles may be "purely ideological" (p. 414).

**The euro and European unification**

- Europe created "for the first time in human history on such a vast scale" a currency without a state (p. 414). The step-by-step story is partly true, but refusing to specify the endpoint may derail the process (p. 414).
- The design reflects the late-1980s belief, after 1970s stagflation, that central banks should be independent and target only inflation; 2008 shattered that view (p. 414).
- Tight constraints on central banks are legitimate, but their independence should have precise limits; the Fed, BoJ and BoE are public bodies working "hand in hand" with elected governments (pp. 414-415).
- The ECB's statutes put low inflation above growth and employment and bar it from buying newly issued government debt; it must juggle seventeen national debts (p. 415).
- Rates converged 2002–2007 and diverged after 2008; southern countries lost devaluation and gained no guarantee of low, predictable borrowing rates (pp. 415-416).
- "The only way" out: pool Eurozone debts under a budgetary parliament drawn from national parliaments (p. 416), with a European finance minister; secretive councils of heads of state "cannot do the work of this budgetary body" (p. 417). France has been "mostly absent" from the debate (p. 417).
- Other joint tools: the progressive capital tax and, "even more obvious", a corporate tax based on a single European profit declaration with revenue apportioned by sales or wages, ending profit-shifting to low-tax subsidiaries (pp. 417-418).
- Extend the "residence of the capital asset" principle to financial assets (p. 418).
- These proposals are no more utopian than a stateless currency; relinquished monetary sovereignty must be matched by restored fiscal sovereignty at the European level (p. 418), so as to construct "a continental political authority capable of reasserting control over patrimonial capitalism" (pp. 418-419).
- Without political union, tax competition continues and pushes toward consumption taxes, a nineteenth-century system with no progressivity; the financial transactions tax shows progress is possible (p. 419).

**Public capital, the golden rule, and climate**

- The desirable level of public debt cannot be fixed with certainty; "only democratic deliberation can decide", and only alongside the question of desirable public and total national capital (p. 419).
- The golden rule (r = g) gives a theoretical ceiling on accumulation. Taken literally it implies β far above historical levels: perhaps 10–15 years of national income for r to fall to 1–1.5%, 20–30 years for it to fall to 0.2% (p. 420).
- At r = g, α = s; as long as r > g, α > s (p. 420). "The inequality r > g is the basis of a society of rentiers" (p. 421).
- Taxing rentiers is a "much simpler and more effective" way to end their reign than accumulating capital worth dozens of years of income (p. 421).
- The optimal capital stock depends on the sources of growth and on how generations are weighed; one can reach "any desired conclusion", so the golden rule "is of limited practical utility" (pp. 421-422).
- Maastricht's 3%/60% rules have never been fully justified and follow from creating a currency without pooled debt; a budgetary parliament would make them unnecessary (p. 422).
- No a priori constitutional debt limits: crises need emergency budgets, judges interpret fiscal law conservatively, and EU law tends to rank free movement of capital above member states' right to tax (pp. 422-423). A supermajority rule would be acceptable as a confidence-building step (p. 422).
- Piketty has "no particular liking for public debt", which often redistributes from poor to rich (pp. 422-423).
- European national wealth "has never been so high"; the real shame is its unequal distribution and that interest on the debt exceeds investment in higher education (p. 423). Debt should be cut "as quickly as possible", ideally by a progressive one-time capital tax, failing that by inflation, decided by a sovereign parliament (p. 423).
- Climate change is the world's principal long-term worry (p. 424). Stern's low discount rate is "more reasonable" than Nordhaus's (p. 424), but the discount-rate debate sidesteps the practical issue of what to invest in, who finances it, and who owns the resulting patents (pp. 424-425).
- Public debt is "not our major worry"; education and natural capital are (pp. 424-425). No one knows yet what role governments will play (p. 425).

**Economic democracy**

- New forms of property and democratic control of capital will be among the most important issues ahead; the public/private line is less clear than assumed since 1989 (p. 425).
- Transparency of corporate (and government) accounts, not of individual incomes, is what democratic participation needs; Lonmin's accounts do not show the profit/wage split at Marikana (pp. 425-426).
- Transparency without a right to intervene, including worker board seats, is "of little use"; institutions of democracy and capitalism must be "reinvented again and again" (p. 426).
