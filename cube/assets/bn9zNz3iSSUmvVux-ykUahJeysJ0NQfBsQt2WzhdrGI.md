**Conclusion**

- The book's purpose was not to sketch a detailed program for a desirable future society (p. 234).
- Only on international affairs did it go a little beyond its essentially critical task, because a framework for long-term growth may soon have to be created there (pp. 234-235).
- Whatever is done after the war is only the start of a long and arduous process (p. 235).
- A detailed blueprint of a desirable internal order would probably be of little use at this stage, and it is doubtful anyone is competent to furnish one (p. 235).
- What matters now is agreement on certain principles and freedom from the errors of the recent past (p. 235).
- It is more important to clear away obstacles and release the creative energy of individuals than to devise more machinery for guiding and directing them (p. 235).
- The first need is to drop the belief that recent policy was all either wise or inevitable; much of it was very foolish (p. 235).
- Courage to make a new start, even at the cost of stepping back first, belongs to those who reject inevitable tendencies, not to preachers of a New Order (p. 235).
- Those loudest for the New Order are the most under the sway of the ideas that produced the war (p. 235).
- The young are right to distrust the ideas ruling their elders but wrong to think those are still nineteenth-century liberal ideas, which they hardly know (p. 235).
- There is neither the wish nor the power to return to the reality of the nineteenth century, but there is the opportunity to realise its ideals (p. 235).
- The twentieth century, not the nineteenth, made a mess of things (p. 235).
- If the first attempt to create a world of free men failed, it must be tried again; freedom for the individual is the only truly progressive policy (pp. 235-236).

**Bibliographical Note**

- A few chapters can discuss only some aspects of a long-unfashionable view, and that is hardly enough common ground for readers formed by the dominant views of the last twenty years (p. 236).
- The author's views are not singular; a steadily growing number of writers in many countries reached similar conclusions independently (p. 236).
- The list includes several works that supplement this essay's critical character with a fuller discussion of a desirable future society (p. 236).
- The earliest and most important is Mises's work, first published in 1922 (p. 236).
- Important German and Italian works exist that cannot safely be named, out of consideration for their authors (p. 237).
- Three books (Ashton, Foerster, Kantorowicz) are singled out as best for understanding the ideas ruling the enemy, plus Schnabel's history of nineteenth-century Germany (p. 237).
- The best guides may still be the political philosophers of the liberal age: Tocqueville, Acton, Constant, Burke and The Federalist (p. 237).
- The present generation takes liberty for granted, does not see where the danger comes from, and lacks the courage to free itself from the doctrines that endanger it (p. 237).
