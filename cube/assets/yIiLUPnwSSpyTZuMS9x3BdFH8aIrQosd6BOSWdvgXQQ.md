**Status of the conclusions**

- The book presents the current state of historical knowledge on the distribution of wealth and income since the eighteenth century and draws lessons for the century ahead (p. 426).
- Its sources are "more extensive than any previous author has assembled" but "imperfect and incomplete"; all conclusions are "by nature tenuous and deserve to be questioned and debated" (p. 426).
- Social science should not try to produce "mathematical certainties" that substitute for open democratic debate (p. 426).

**The restated argument**

- A market economy based on private property, left to itself, contains powerful forces of convergence and powerful forces of divergence; the latter threaten democratic societies and social justice (p. 426).
- The principal destabilising force is that r can exceed g "significantly" and "for long periods of time" (pp. 426-427).
- r > g means past wealth grows faster than output and wages; the entrepreneur tends to become a rentier; "The past devours the future" (p. 427).
- The consequences are "potentially terrifying", especially since returns rise with the size of the initial stake and the divergence is occurring globally (p. 427).
- There is no simple solution: education, knowledge and clean technology can encourage growth, but will not raise it to 4–5%; only catching-up economies grow that fast, and frontier growth will likely not exceed 1–1.5% "no matter what economic policies are adopted" (p. 427).
- With a 4–5% return, r > g is likely to become the norm again, as it was until the eve of World War I; the two world wars created "the illusion" that it had been overcome (p. 427).
- Taxing capital income heavily and indiscriminately enough to push r below g would risk "killing the motor of accumulation" and further lowering growth (p. 427).
- The right solution is a progressive annual tax on capital (p. 427).
- Immense wealth inequalities have "little to do with the entrepreneurial spirit" and do not promote growth, nor are they of "common utility" (p. 428).
- The capital tax needs international cooperation and regional political integration, beyond the reach of the nation-states where earlier social compromises were made (p. 428).
- Pure and perfect competition cannot alter r > g, which is "not the consequence of any market “imperfection.”" (p. 428).
- Despite the risk that European integration erodes existing social states, there is no genuine alternative: "we must bet everything on democracy—and in Europe, democracy on a European scale" (p. 428). The US and China have more options; small European countries withdrawing nationally would face worse frustration (p. 428).

**Political and historical economics**

- Economics is a subdiscipline of the social sciences; the term "economic science" is arrogant; "political economy" conveys its normative purpose (p. 428).
- Political economy asks what policies and institutions bring us closer to an ideal society, and this aspiration to study good and evil is "necessary, indeed indispensable" even if often unfulfilled (pp. 428-429).
- Social scientists, like all citizens, should take stands on specific institutions and policies, not invoke abstract principles; the idea that scholars deal in means and citizens in ends is "dangerous" (p. 429).
- Economists' "immoderate use of mathematical models" is often an excuse "for occupying the terrain and masking the vacuity of the content" (p. 429).
- Controlled experiments are useful in moderation and have turned economists toward concrete questions, but they succumb to "a certain scientistic illusion" and neglect history (p. 429).
- Historical causality is hard to prove, but the lessons of history, especially the twentieth century, are of "inestimable, irreplaceable value" no controlled experiment can equal (pp. 429-430).
- Economists should be pragmatic in method and work with other disciplines; other social scientists should not flee numbers or rest on "every statistic is a social construct", which is true but insufficient (p. 430).
- Furet's career illustrates both why serial history died (aridity, data-processing limits, the anti-Marxist turn of the 1970s) and the loss involved (pp. 430-431).
- Politics and ideas exist independently of economic change, yet prices, wages, incomes and fortunes shape political attitudes, which in turn shape institutions and economic change; an approach that is economic, political, social and cultural at once is "indispensable" (pp. 430-431).
- The 1917–1989 clash of communism and capitalism "sterilized rather than stimulated" research on capital and inequality (p. 431).
- Serial history petered out before reaching the twentieth century, where politics is visibly "ubiquitous" in inequality and capital/income curves; studying that century forces one to study the state, taxes and debt concretely and to abandon infrastructure/superstructure notions (p. 431).
- Not all social science needs statistical series, and data collection is not always imaginative (p. 431); but all citizens should take a serious interest in money, its measurement and history, because those who have a lot of it always defend their interests (pp. 431-432).
