| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vautrin's lesson | pp. 171-173 | The ex-convict's advice to Rastignac in *Père Goriot* (1835): marrying a million-franc heiress yields 50,000 francs a year at 5 percent at age twenty, ten times a royal prosecutor's pay and as much as the best Parisian lawyers earn at fifty (pp. 172-173). Shorthand for a society in which inherited wealth beats work and study (p. 173). |
| Three components of income inequality | p. 171 | Inequality of labor income; inequality of capital ownership and capital income; and "the interaction between these two terms" (p. 171), i.e. the correlation between labor and capital income (p. 175). |
| Income from capital | p. 175 | All income from owning capital, "independent of any labor and regardless of its legal classification", here including capital gains (p. 175). (Contrast ch06's average return, which neglects capital gains, ch06 p. 151.) |
| Correlation between labor and capital income | p. 175 | The third determinant of total inequality; often low or negative where owners need not work (p. 175), negative in traditional societies, positive but below one in modern ones (pp. 182-183). |
| Decile, centile, thousandth | pp. 178, 180 | Statistical groups defined the same way in every society, so allowing comparison across time and space (p. 180). Defined on the adult population, at the individual level where possible; French and US historical income data are household-level (endnote 5, p. 454). |
| Lower / middle / upper class | p. 179 | Bottom 50 percent / middle 40 percent / top 10 percent. Declared "arbitrary and open to challenge" and said to "play virtually no role in the analysis" (p. 179). Everyone in the middle 40 is above the median by construction (p. 180). |
| Dominant class / wealthy class | pp. 180-181 | Top 1 percent ("dominant class") and the next 9 percent ("wealthy class" or "well-to-do"), labels chosen "for the sake of concreteness" (pp. 180-181). |
| Primary distribution | p. 183 | Distribution before taxes and transfers. All of Tables 7.1–7.3 are primary distributions (p. 183); replacement incomes (pensions, unemployment insurance) are counted as labor income, "deferred wages" (endnote 9, p. 455). |
| Patrimonial (propertied) middle class | p. 188 | The middle 40 percent of the wealth distribution holding a significant share of wealth (one-quarter to one-third) mostly through owner-occupied housing; absent in 1900–1910, when the middle 40 held 5–10 percent (pp. 188-189). |
| Hyperpatrimonial society / society of rentiers | p. 191 | Very high total-income inequality driven by inherited wealth, with the top decile owning about 90 percent and the top centile 50 percent of wealth: Ancien Régime France, Belle Époque Europe (p. 191). |
| Hypermeritocratic society / society of superstars or supermanagers | p. 191 | Very high total-income inequality driven by top labor incomes; "largely created by the United States over the past few decades" (p. 191). "Hypermeritocratic" is how winners describe it; Piketty says he is not yet judging whether the label fits (p. 191). |
| Gini coefficient | p. 192 | Synthetic index from 0 (complete equality) to 1 (absolute inequality) (p. 192). Typical ranges: labor income 0.2–0.4, capital 0.6–0.9, total income 0.3–0.5 (p. 192). |
| Synthetic index | p. 175 | A single number (Gini, Theil) summarising a whole distribution; mixes labor and capital inequality and different parts of the distribution (pp. 175-176, 192-193). |
| Interdecile ratio (P90/P10) | p. 193 | Ratio of the ninetieth to the tenth percentile threshold (p. 193); ignores everything above P90 (p. 194) and is unstable at the bottom for measurement reasons (pp. 194-195). |
| Distribution table | p. 193 | Shares of total income or wealth held by the bottom 50, middle 40, top 10 and top 1 percent, with corresponding money levels. Piketty's preferred instrument (pp. 193, 195). |
| Social tables / political arithmetic | p. 195 | Seventeenth–nineteenth-century tables (Gregory King 1688; Expilly, Isnard, Peuchet, Colquhoun, Blodget) giving numbers and incomes of social groups (p. 195). Piketty's tables are their descendants but use deciles rather than the period's own categories (p. 196). |
