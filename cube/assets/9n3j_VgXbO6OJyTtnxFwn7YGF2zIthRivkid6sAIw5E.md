**A. There has been no "return of the state" because it never left (pp. 344-347)**

- **Premises**: (p. 344) Today's crisis differs from 1929 because the state is already far larger. (p. 346) Taxes went from under 10 percent to 30–55 percent of national income and stabilised. (p. 346) No downward trend is visible in taxes or outlays.
- **Qualification**: (p. 346) The state also acts by rules and ownership, and on those dimensions (financial deregulation, privatisation) its role shrank after 1980.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 346-347) "Return of the state" is a misleading frame. "There will be no second leap".
- **Note**: the conclusion is drawn on the tax/spending dimension alone, which he admits is partial. His later programme (capital tax, financial transparency) is largely about the rule and ownership dimensions, where the state did retreat.

**B. The fiscal state is the social state (pp. 347-348)**

- **Premises**: (p. 347) Regalian functions cost a little under a tenth of national income today, about what they cost in total before 1914 (p. 345). (pp. 347-348) Education and health (10–15 percent) plus replacement and transfers (10–15, up to 20 percent) total 25–35 percent.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 348) Social spending accounts for "nearly all" the twentieth-century increase in revenue.

**C. From the 1789 Declaration to a Rawlsian principle (pp. 348-350)**

- **Premises**: (p. 349) The Declaration of Independence asserts an equal right to the pursuit of happiness. (p. 349) Article 1 of 1789 asserts equal rights and allows social distinctions only "on common utility". (p. 349) The second sentence reverses the burden of proof onto inequality. (p. 349) The drafters meant the abolition of hereditary orders and privileges, but the phrase "can" be read more broadly.
- **Interpretive step**: (p. 349) "One reasonable interpretation": inequalities are acceptable only if in the interest of all, "in particular of the most disadvantaged". Hence rights and advantages should be extended to everyone as far as it serves those with the fewest rights and opportunities.
- **Supporting moves**: (n. 20, p. 484) The drafters were not utilitarians, and the "concave utility" justification of redistribution "bears little apparent relation to the way most people think". Rights are more pertinent. (n. 21, pp. 484-485) Disadvantage is defined by factors beyond one's control, so reducing inequalities of family endowment and luck is just. (n. 22, p. 485) Rawls quoted.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 349) This is "similar in intent" to Rawls's difference principle and close to Sen. There is a "certain (partly artificial) consensus" on abstract principles.
- **Status (my observation)**: this is a normative reconstruction, not a historical claim. Piketty marks it as an interpretation ("One can interpret the phrase more broadly"). Moving from the drafters' anti-privilege meaning to a maximin principle is a large step: abolishing legal orders is compatible with any distribution produced by equal legal rules. The step gives a French revolutionary pedigree to what is essentially Rawls, and it does the work of making the capital tax look like an application of an old consensus. Note too that note 21 adds luck-egalitarianism (factors beyond control), which is not in Rawls's difference principle as quoted in n. 22.

**D. Principles do not settle policy; democracy does (pp. 349-350)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 349-350) Disagreements concern means, the extent of affordable rights, and the boundary between luck and effort. (p. 350) These "will never be answered by abstract principles or mathematical formulas".
- **Conclusion**: (p. 350) Democratic institutions and the relative power and persuasiveness of social groups decide.
- **Tension**: this sits uneasily with the confident policy conclusions elsewhere (the capital tax as "the ideal policy", p. 342; confiscatory rates, p. 344). Piketty presents these as following from his historical analysis, not as one side's position in a democratic contest.

**E. Why the social state should not keep growing (pp. 350-352)**

- **Premises**: (p. 350) In theory, a country could tax two-thirds or three-quarters of national income, since taxation is neither good nor bad in itself. (pp. 350-351) Postwar expansion was eased by 5 percent growth, while with growth of just over 1 percent per capita durable rises in the average tax rate are hard to imagine. (p. 351) People legitimately want private goods, and there is "no obvious reason" all needs should be tax-financed. (p. 351) Beyond a certain size the public sector faces organisational problems. New participatory forms might solve them, but that is speculative.
- **Conclusion**: A drastic increase is "neither realistic nor desirable, at least for the foreseeable future" (p. 350). (p. 352) The task is to improve the existing public sector, about half of national income.
- **Note**: the first reason is a political-economy claim about consent under low growth, supported by the uniform stabilisation since 1980 ("no accident", p. 351). The second is a mixed normative and organisational claim. Neither rules out more progressive redistribution at a constant total (p. 351, explicitly allowed).

**F. Education does not deliver mobility (pp. 352-355)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 352-353) Educational upgrading shifted all qualification levels upward without reducing wage inequality (Part Three). (p. 353) Intergenerational correlations show no long-run trend to more mobility, though the data are "highly imperfect". (p. 353) US mobility is lowest among the rich countries. (pp. 353-354) US elite access tracks parental income through tuition and donations. Harvard parents average top-2-percent incomes. (p. 354) In France, cultural selection and grandes écoles funding do similar work.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 355) Real equality of opportunity in higher education has no easy solution. Meritocratic rhetoric often justifies existing inequality. The Boutmy quotation shows elites inventing meritocracy to defend their position against universal suffrage (p. 355).
- **Inference to check**: (p. 353) "One possible explanation" of low US mobility is high tuition. This is offered as a possibility, but by the end of the paragraph parents' income "has become an almost perfect predictor of university access" (p. 353). The underlying data are degree completion by parental income quartile (Duncan and Murnane, n. 30, p. 486). A 10–20 percent against 80 percent gap is strong, but "almost perfect predictor" overstates what quartile shares show.

**G. Keep PAYGO despite r > g (pp. 355-357)**

- **Premises**: (p. 356) PAYGO's return equals the growth rate g. Under r > g a funded system seems to pay more. (p. 356) But the transition leaves one generation with nothing, because it paid for its predecessors and would see its successors' contributions invested instead. (p. 357) r > g holds on average, not for each investment. Returns are volatile, and betting a generation's necessities on markets is irrational. (p. 357) Wage growth is 5–10 times less volatile than capital returns.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 357) PAYGO "will therefore continue to be part of the ideal social state of the future everywhere". (p. 357) Parameters such as retirement age must adapt to longer lives and low growth.
- **Note**: the transition argument is a classic double-payment problem. The volatility argument depends on the risk-return tradeoff that ch12 argued is weak for the largest portfolios (p. 328 in ch12). Piketty's position stays consistent because the relevant investor here is a generation of small savers. Note 47 links back to ch12: modest savers struggle even to achieve the average return (p. 487).

**H. Poor countries need fiscal capacity, and the rich countries undermined it (pp. 358-360)**

- **Premises**: (p. 359) With 10–15 percent of national income in tax, a state can do little beyond regalian functions. (p. 359) Underpaying public servants produces a vicious circle of poor services and low trust. (p. 359) Since 1980, "ultraliberal" pressure cut public sectors, and trade liberalisation removed customs revenue (about 5 percent of national income) before replacement taxes existed. (p. 359) Today's rich countries cut tariffs at their own pace with alternatives in mind.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 359) Part of the blame lies with the rich countries and international organisations, who use the developing world as "a field of experimentation" (pp. 359-360).
- **Evidence status**: one working paper (Cagé and Gadenne 2012, n. 48, pp. 487-488) supports the customs-revenue claim. The broader "ultraliberal wave" causal claim is asserted.
