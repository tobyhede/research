| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Factorial vs individual distribution | p. 31 | Factorial: the split of income between labour and capital treated as homogeneous "factors of production". Individual: inequality of labour and capital income between persons. Parts One–Two study the first; Part Three the second (p. 31). Endnote 3 equates them with "functional" and "personal" distribution (p. 435). |
| Capital-labor split | p. 34 | The share of income going to capital and to labour respectively (p. 34). Contrasted with the capital/income ratio, which Piketty says is the more fruitful object (p. 34). |
| National income | p. 34 | "the sum of all income available to the residents of a given country in a given year, regardless of the legal classification of that income" (p. 34). Equals GDP minus depreciation plus net income from abroad (pp. 34-36). Endnote 7: also called net national product (p. 436). |
| GDP / domestic output | pp. 34-35 | GDP is goods and services produced within the borders in a year. Subtracting depreciation (about 10% of GDP) gives net domestic product, which Piketty calls "domestic output" (pp. 34-35). |
| Depreciation | pp. 34-35 | Wear and tear on buildings, infrastructure, machinery etc.; "does not correspond to anyone’s income" (p. 35). |
| Net income from abroad | p. 35 | Income received from abroad minus income paid to foreigners; mostly capital income, plus some labour income and transfers (p. 35; endnote 7, p. 436). |
| Capital (= wealth) | pp. 36-37 | "the sum total of nonhuman assets that can be owned and exchanged on some market" (pp. 36-37): real property including housing, plus financial and professional capital. Used interchangeably with "wealth" (p. 37). Includes land and natural resources (p. 38), gold (p. 38), residential housing (p. 38) and immaterial capital via market capitalisation (p. 39). Valued at market prices (p. 38). |
| Human capital | p. 36 | Labour power, skills, training and abilities. Excluded from "capital" because it cannot be owned by another or permanently traded, except in slave societies (pp. 36-37). |
| Private / public capital | pp. 37, 39 | Assets minus liabilities of private individuals vs of government entities (p. 39). Public wealth today is "insignificant (or even negative" in most developed countries (p. 39). |
| National wealth / national capital | p. 38 | "the total market value of everything owned by the residents and government of a given country at a given point in time, provided that it can be traded on some market" (p. 38); nonfinancial plus financial assets minus financial liabilities (pp. 38-39). National wealth = private wealth + public wealth (p. 39). |
| Domestic capital / net foreign capital | pp. 39-40 | National capital = domestic capital (located within the borders) + net foreign assets (assets owned abroad minus domestic assets owned by foreigners) (pp. 39-40). Globally net foreign positions sum to zero (p. 40). |
| β (capital/income ratio) | p. 40 | Capital stock divided by annual national income; a stock divided by a flow, expressed in years (p. 40). 5–6 in rich countries around 2010 (p. 40). |
| α (capital share) | p. 41 | Share of income from capital in national income (p. 41). About 30% in rich countries around 2010 (p. 42). |
| r (rate of return on capital) | pp. 41-42 | Yield on capital over a year "regardless of its legal form (profits, rents, dividends, interest, royalties, capital gains, etc.)", as a percentage of capital's value (p. 42). Broader than the "rate of profit" and much broader than the "rate of interest" (p. 42). |
| First fundamental law of capitalism | p. 41 | α = r × β; "a pure accounting identity" valid for all societies by definition (p. 42). |
| Second fundamental law of capitalism | p. 44 | Previewed only: the higher the savings rate and the lower the growth rate, the higher β (p. 44). |
| Purchasing power parity (PPP) | p. 50 | Conversion rate that equalises the actual purchasing power of incomes across countries, from the International Comparison Program (p. 50). $1.20 per euro in 2012 vs a market rate of $1.30 (p. 50). |
| Marginal productivity of capital | p. 53 | "the additional output due to adding one new unit of capital “at the margin”" (p. 53). The basis of the classical argument that capital flows from rich to poor countries promote convergence (pp. 53-54). |
| Diffusion of knowledge | p. 55 | Restated as "the principal mechanism for convergence at the international as well as the domestic level" (p. 55); depends on finance, education and legitimate, efficient government (p. 55). |
