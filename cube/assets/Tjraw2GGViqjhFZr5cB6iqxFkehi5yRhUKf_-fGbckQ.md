- **Depends on ch10**: r > g, the inheritance society (ch10 p. 254), and the 1914–1945 shocks are taken from ch10; ch11 delivers ch10's promise that most nineteenth-century wealth was inherited (ch10 p. 255).
- **Depends on ch05 and ch03**: β's U-curve and the fall of private wealth from seven to 2–2.5 years of income (p. 288, citing Figure 3.6).
- **Depends on ch07-ch09**: Vautrin's lecture (ch07), the stable top-centile wage share of 6–7 percent (p. 296), and the claim that supermanager pay has "very little factual basis" (p. 304, from ch09).
- **Feeds ch12**: unequal returns by wealth (p. 294, note 26; p. 297 "as much as a third") would raise inheritance's share; ch11 holds the return equal.
- **Feeds ch13-ch15**: pensions as "the fortunes of those who have no fortune" (note 12, p. 470); education and mobility (note 53, p. 475); labour taxed at about 30 percent against an effective inheritance tax under 5 percent (note 50, pp. 474-475), the basis for ch14-ch15's tax proposals.
- **Definitional shift on "rent" (my observation)**: Piketty's rent is all pure ownership income (p. 308); the economists' rent is above-competitive return. His claim that rent "has absolutely nothing to do with the problem of imperfect competition" (p. 308) is true under his definition. The disagreement with Draghi is partly verbal; the substantive claim is that ordinary competitive returns, compounded with r > g, are enough to produce rentier dominance.
- **Merit as standard and as ideology (my observation)**: the meritocratic hope is a democratic necessity (p. 307), yet "meritocratic extremism" is dangerous (p. 304), and the nineteenth-century elites are praised for not pretending to merit (p. 303). The coherent reading is that the standard is "common utility" (p. 307), with merit-based justifications acceptable only when they are factually true. Synthesis should state that this is a reading, since Piketty does not spell out his theory of justice here.
- **Entrepreneurs and rentiers (my observation)**: p. 287 concedes many Belle Époque fortunes were made by entrepreneurs; the charge is that r > g turns them into rentiers over time. So the inequality Piketty calls arbitrary is not the origin of fortunes but their compounding and transmission. This matters for any comparison with authors who defend the initial earnings.
- **"Inevitable" versus "it could be otherwise"**: compare "all but inevitable" and "in strict logic, it could be otherwise" (p. 274) with "almost inevitable (at least in the most plausible dynamic models)" (p. 298). The hedges are consistent; the strength comes from models not printed in the book.
- **Tension with ch10 on causes of non-recovery**: ch10 gives taxation as the main reason concentration has not returned (ch10 p. 272); ch11 explains the low postwar inheritance flow by rejuvenation of wealth and high growth (pp. 288, 290-291), with taxation mentioned mainly in the 2010–2100 scenarios (p. 289) and note 50. The two accounts are complementary (stock concentration versus flow volume) but synthesis should keep them distinct.
- **Two kinds of inheritance inequality**: volume (inheritance as a share of resources, now back near nineteenth-century levels, p. 304) versus concentration (top-centile share, far below, p. 304). The petits-rentiers argument (p. 306) is about the first; the democracy argument (p. 307) mostly about a possible return of the second.
