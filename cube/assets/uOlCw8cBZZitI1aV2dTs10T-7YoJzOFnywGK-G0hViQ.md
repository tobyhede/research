**1944 preface**

- Personal testimony only: his own motives, his earlier socialist views, the costs of publication (p. 38). The claim that most economists were silenced and that opinion was led by cranks is asserted without names or figures (p. 38).

**1956 foreword**

- Autobiographical testimony about origins, audience and his contact with the Continent (p. 39).
- Reception claims rest on his own impression; he names Wootton's Freedom under Planning as the representative left criticism and cites one reviewer who thought it confirmed him (note 5, pp. 49-50), and Finer's Road to Reaction as the specimen of abuse (note 9, p. 51).
- Publication history: rejection by three houses, attributed in his note 7 to political prejudice on the strength of William Miller's statement as reported by Couch (p. 40; note 7, p. 50). Caldwell's bracketed addition gives the background and shows the source is a hostile letter, which fits one firm, not necessarily all three (p. 50).
- One historical datum for American enthusiasm for planning: the 1934 National Planning Board's attention to planning in Germany, Italy, Russia and Japan (p. 41). He declines to name individuals (p. 41).
- A quoted critic (identified by Caldwell as Alvin Hansen) for the counter-claim that the four countries reached totalitarianism by different roads (p. 42; note 12, p. 51).
- Official document: two passages of the Economic Survey for 1947, set against the later Control of Engagement Order (pp. 45-46; Caldwell's notes 18-20, p. 52). Hayek concedes the power was never used and argues that the existence of the power is what matters (pp. 45-46).
- Sociological survey: long extract from L. J. Barnes's 1945 report on youth in an English county (pp. 46-47; note 21, p. 52). One report, one county, pre-Labour, wartime regulation. Hayek's wording outruns it: the change in national character can hardly be mistaken, yet the changes are not easily demonstrated but clearly felt if one lives in the country (p. 46).
- Authority by quotation: Tocqueville on the new servitude (p. 47); Ivor Thomas, a Labour defector (p. 48; note 24, p. 52); The Economist on Crichel Down and Hewart's New Despotism (p. 48; note 25, pp. 52-53); the jurist G. W. Keeton that Britain lives on the edge of dictatorship (p. 49; note 26, p. 53). These are opinions of like-minded observers. He presents them as observers less suspect of preconceived opinions (p. 48), but Thomas had left Labour and later joined the Conservatives (Caldwell, p. 52).
- Comparative assertion without data: the German Social Democrats of the 1920s never approached totalitarian planning as closely as British Labour (p. 48).
- Referral: Jewkes's Ordeal by Planning for the detailed case (p. 49). The foreword itself does not examine British economic policy in detail and says so (p. 48).
- Confident language outrunning evidence: that it is doubtful whether the Rule of Law still prevails in Britain (p. 48); that the century of socialism probably ended around 1948 (p. 42); that the main conclusions are widely accepted (p. 42).

**1976 preface**

- Autobiographical testimony about dates, origin and his own discomfort (pp. 53-54). The unnamed philosopher anecdote (identified by Caldwell as Carnap) illustrates hostile reaction by people who had not read the book (p. 54; note 2, p. 56).
- One comparative example for the terminological point: Sweden against Great Britain and Austria (p. 55). No evidence is given for the claim that the welfare-state route tends to the same ultimate outcome (p. 55), nor for the claim that socialist influence and trust in totalitarian rulers have markedly increased (pp. 55-56).
- Kelsen and Laski are cited, without reference, as having held that the Rule of Law would prevail under a constitutionally empowered Hitler (p. 55).
- A concession about evidence: the book may contain much he could not have convincingly demonstrated when he wrote it (p. 56).
