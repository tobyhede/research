| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| genuine socialist movement | p. 238 | Hayek 1933: National Socialism is socialism in substance, the final fruit of German anti-liberal tendencies since the later Bismarck era, not a reaction toward 1914. |
| anti-liberal | p. 238 | Hayek 1933: the common trend of German intellectual life that led to socialism of the chair, then Marxism, then Nazism; what the movement is more than anything else (p. 239). |
| inherent logic of collectivism | p. 240 | Hayek 1933: collective action beyond certain limits requires coercing all into accepting the rulers' view of the common interest, so it cannot be confined to the economic sphere. |
| middle class socialism | p. 240 | Hayek 1933: Nazism's variant, favouring artisans and shopkeepers and nominally keeping private property while emptying it by control. |
| the worst | p. 241 | Knight 1943, quoting the book: the kind of individuals under whose control a planned order will inevitably fall. |
| essentially negative | p. 242 | Knight 1943: the book criticises but hardly considers alternatives or the necessary range of government activity. |
| planning in the good sense | p. 243 | Marschak 1943, citing the book's phrase: the permissible kind, which he says Hayek does not concretely mark off from undesirable planning proper. |
| shibboleths | p. 243 | Chamberlain 1944: the slogans full employment, planning, social security, freedom from want (p. 244). |
| rule of law | p. 245 | Chamberlain 1944: the alternative to planning; the individual must know in advance how the rules will work. Marschak glosses it as the avoidance of ad hoc administrative decisions (p. 243). |
| collectivism / individualism | p. 248 | Friedman 1971: the two poles of the battle; collectivism covers central planning and also the newer transfer and regulatory state. |
| liberal | p. 249 | Friedman 1971: as Hayek uses it, the nineteenth-century sense of limited government and free markets, not the American sense. |
| check to collectivism | p. 250 | Friedman 1971: the unexpected halt after 1945 to the growth of state production and planning in Britain, France and the United States. |
| control of engagements order | p. 250 | Friedman 1971: the British power to assign people to occupations; for him the point at which planning was sacrificed to liberty. |
