Concessions and qualifications recorded:
- The growth of monopoly and the shrinking field of competition are 'not disputed' as historical fact (p. 88).
- 'We cannot here investigate this question in detail and must be content to accept the best evidence available' (p. 89).
- Economies of size do exist in some fields ('where they exist', in the monograph Hayek quotes) (p. 89).
- Britain in the 1920s suffered from 'incompatible policies' on wages and money (p. 90).
- For England the view that policy merely follows facts has 'a certain appearance of truth' (p. 91).
- That complexity creates problems needing central planning is true 'in a sense': the problems of a modern town, of close contiguity in space, and of public utilities are not adequately solved by competition (p. 91). State action accepted: town planning and public-utility regulation or provision.
- The monopoly-for-new-technique argument is 'not necessarily fraudulent' and the stock reply does not dispose of all cases (p. 93).
- Compulsory standardisation or prohibition of variety might raise abundance more than enough to compensate for restricted choice; an invention might some day be usable only if all adopt it at once (p. 93).
- Preserving freedom may cost 'a possible immediate gain', and the price of variety 'may sometimes be high' in the short run (pp. 93-94).
- It cannot be asserted that the sacrifice will be rewarded in all instances; compulsion 'in a particular instance' may do no harm (p. 94).
- Inventions have given us tremendous power (p. 94).
- Almost every technical ideal of the experts could be realised quickly if made the sole aim (p. 94).
- A planned society 'would certainly further some objectives more than is the case at present'; there are 'good things which the people of these countries owe entirely to planning' (p. 95).
- The motor roads do not represent a kind of planning impossible in a liberal society (p. 95): state road-building is again accepted.
- Single-minded idealists have 'great pragmatic value' in a free society and deserve 'just admiration' (p. 96).
- The market's checks on individual effort are 'impersonal, and often unintelligible' (p. 96): an admission that market outcomes cannot always be explained or justified to those affected.
