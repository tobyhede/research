- Epigraph from Hölderlin on the state made a hell by the attempt to make it heaven (pp. 73-74). Kind: motto; editor supplies source and German text (p. 78).
- Saint-Simon's prediction that those who disobey his planning boards would be 'treated as cattle' (p. 74). Kind: quotation from an opponent, uncited by Hayek. **Editor's correction**: the translated source reads that such a man will be 'regarded and treated by others as an animal', and the sentence was apparently deleted by disciples from some later versions (p. 78). Hayek's wording is a loose rendering.
- 'Terminate the revolution' and coercive 'spiritual power' (p. 74). Kind: quoted phrases, unattributed and uncited.
- Tocqueville's 1848 speech contrasting democracy and socialism (p. 74). Kind: quotation from an authority, cited by Hayek (p. 78). The editor prints the French original (p. 78). My observation from that original: Hayek's English gives 'individual freedom' for indépendance individuelle and drops 'un instrument' from the series agent, instrument, chiffre; the sense is preserved but the translation is not exact. Context worth keeping: the speech was on the right to work.
- 'Despotism of physical want', 'restraints of the economic system', 'not worth having' (pp. 74-75). Kind: phrases in quotation marks attributed to socialists generally; no source given.
- Paul Janet on the Saint-Simonian theory of liberty, and John Dewey's 'liberty is the effective power to do specific things' in Hayek's note 4 (pp. 78-79). Kind: quotation from an opponent; the one piece of evidence that a named socialist or left thinker actually defines freedom as power.
- Max Eastman on Stalinism as worse than fascism and as socialism's unforeseen political accompaniment (pp. 75-76). Kind: testimony of a disillusioned sympathiser. **Editor's correction**: Hayek listed all three passages as p. 82; they are on pp. 82, 82 and 154 (p. 79). Editor adds that Eastman later condensed this book for Reader's Digest (p. 79).
- W. H. Chamberlin on socialism as the road to dictatorship (p. 76). Kind: testimony; cited (p. 79). Note Chamberlin's own hedge, 'in the beginning at least' (p. 76).
- F. A. Voigt: Marxism 'is' Fascism and National Socialism (p. 76). Kind: testimony. **Editor's correction**: the editor could not locate the quoted passage at the page Hayek cites, and offers a similar passage from p. 35 of Voigt's book (p. 79). The quotation is therefore unverified.
- Walter Lippmann on the nemesis of the planned society (p. 76). Kind: testimony; cited (p. 79).
- Peter Drucker on Russia and Germany travelling the same road (p. 76). Kind: testimony. **Editor's correction**: Hayek gave p. 230; the passage is on pp. 245-246 (p. 79). Drucker's passage itself denies that communism and fascism are essentially the same and calls fascism the stage after communism has proved an illusion (p. 76), which is a different thesis from Voigt's identity claim; Hayek says Drucker puts it 'perhaps more justly' (p. 76).
- Leaders who began as socialists: Mussolini, Laval, Quisling (pp. 76-77); Michels cited for the Italian cases in Hayek's note 10 (p. 80). Kind: historical examples.
- Ease of conversion between communist and Nazi, 'generally known in Germany'; students returning unsure which they were (p. 77). Kind: anecdote and appeal to common knowledge.
- Eduard Heimann, a religious socialist, on liberalism as the doctrine most hated by Hitler (p. 77). Kind: admission by an opponent; cited (p. 80). Heimann calls the grain of truth in Hitler's socialist claim 'infinitesimal' (p. 77).
- Hitler's February 1941 statement that National Socialism and Marxism are basically the same in Hayek's note 12 (p. 80). Kind: quotation from the enemy; Hayek hedges with 'whatever may have been his reasons' (p. 80).
- Where language outruns evidence: 'There can be no doubt' (p. 75), 'Unquestionably' (p. 75), 'Everyone who has watched' (p. 76), 'it was socialism that had killed it' (p. 77) and 'not only unachievable' (p. 78) are all asserted on testimony and anecdote. The chapter's evidence is almost entirely quotation: it shows that some informed people believed in the connection, not that the connection holds. Hayek concedes as much at p. 78. Two of the five witness quotations carry wrong page references and one cannot be found.

Concessions and qualifications recorded:
- Circumstances limit the range of choice 'for some very much more than for others' (p. 75): the inequality the socialists point to is real.
- The socialist belief in freedom is 'genuine and sincere' (p. 75); most socialists 'believe profoundly in the liberal ideal of freedom' and would recoil from its destruction (pp. 77-78).
- Socialist promises of greater wealth were 'often' irresponsible, but Hayek grants that the core demand was equality of range of choice, not abundance (p. 75).
- Communists and Nazis or Fascists did clash with each other more than with other parties before 1933 and 1922 (p. 77).
- The conclusions of this chapter 'will only confirm the apprehensions which others have already expressed'; the reasons are deferred (p. 78).
- No state activity is discussed in this chapter.
