Listed separately because these are easily overstated or understated. Each is his position with his own conditions.

1. **Guaranteed minimum** of food, shelter and clothing sufficient to preserve health and the capacity to work, assured to everybody (p. 144). Conditions and caveats: (a) premised on a society that has reached our general level of wealth (p. 144); (b) the precise standard raises difficult questions (p. 144); (c) he flags as an important open question whether those who rely on the community should indefinitely enjoy all the same liberties as the rest (p. 144); (d) incautious handling could cause serious, perhaps dangerous, political problems (p. 144); (e) Hayek's own footnote adds that serious international problems arise if mere citizenship confers a right to a higher standard of living than elsewhere (p. 153, note 3); (f) it must be provided outside the market (pp. 144, 152). He notes this security had long been achieved for a considerable part of the English population (p. 144). Note the level: subsistence adequate to health and working capacity, not a share in general prosperity.
2. **Social insurance** for common hazards against which few can make adequate provision. The condition is that the risk be genuinely insurable: as with sickness and accident, neither the desire to avoid the calamity nor the effort to overcome it is as a rule weakened by assistance. On that condition the case for the state's helping to organize a comprehensive system is "very strong" (pp. 144-145). Caveats: the wording is that the state helps to organize, not that it must be sole provider; details will divide friends and opponents of competition; measures that make competition ineffective can be smuggled in under the name of social insurance (p. 145). Unemployment and old age are not named in the list of examples. (My observation: whether unemployment insurance passes his moral-hazard test is left unaddressed.)
3. **Disaster relief** for victims of acts of God such as earthquakes and floods; wherever communal action can mitigate disasters the individual can neither guard against nor provide for, it should "undoubtedly" be taken (p. 145).
4. **Combating general fluctuations and mass unemployment**, which he calls one of the gravest and most pressing problems of the time. It requires much planning in the good sense but not, or at least need not require, the planning that replaces the market (p. 145). Preferred route: monetary policy, compatible even with nineteenth-century liberalism (p. 145). Public works on a very large scale are acknowledged as the view of others, not ruled out, but said to risk much more serious restriction of the competitive sphere and growing dependence of all activity on government expenditure, so that in experimenting we must watch our step; not in his opinion the most promising way (p. 145). The editor identifies this as what would later be called Keynesian demand management (p. 153, note 4).
5. **Absolute security for a few special offices** such as judges, where complete independence is of paramount importance (p. 144).
6. **Voluntary labor service on military lines** may be the best form in which the state can provide the certainty of an opportunity for work and a minimum income for all; sections of a free society may be organized on the military principle for those who prefer it. Condition: it must be voluntary and not extended to those unwilling to give up their freedom (p. 149).
7. **Mitigation of transitional hardship**: Hayek's own footnote commends W. H. Hutt's Plan for Reconstruction (1943) for suggestions on how the hardships of those displaced by change might be mitigated within a liberal society (p. 153, note 5).
8. **Stated goals of policy**: adequate security against severe privation and reduction of the avoidable causes of misdirected effort and consequent disappointment will have to be among the main goals of policy (p. 152). Some security is essential to preserving freedom, because most men will bear freedom's risk only if it is not too great (p. 152).
9. **Moral concession**: undeserved losses offend our sense of justice; the hardships have no moral justification yet are inseparable from the competitive system (pp. 145-146). He does not claim market outcomes are deserved.

What he refuses: protection of particular incomes, standards of life, fair prices or professional incomes; stabilization of particular prices or wages; restrictions on entry (pp. 149-150).
