- **Depends on ch01 and ch05**: α = r × β (ch01) and β = s/g (ch05) are the two laws this chapter joins. The forecast of rising α inherits every uncertainty in ch05's forecast of β (s ≈ 10 percent, g ≈ 1–1.5 percent globally).
- **Market value vs volume (my observation)**: β throughout is capital at market value, and the chapter itself says the relative price of capital fell 1910–1950 and rose 1950–2010 (p. 151). The elasticity criterion (p. 157) is a statement about volumes of capital along a production function. Where β rises because asset prices rise, the co-movement of α and β in Figure 6.5 is not a movement along a production function and says nothing direct about σ. ch03 and ch05 on the relative price of capital should be read against this.
- **Housing carries much of the rise (my observation)**: in France the capital share rose from 15 to 27 percent of national income 1982–2010 (Figure 6.8), while housing rent alone rose from 2 percent (1948) to 10 percent (2010) (Figure 6.7), and Piketty says rent's rise is why α kept climbing after the profit share stabilised (p. 164). An argument from robots and many new uses of capital (p. 160) sits awkwardly with a rise driven largely by the rental value of dwellings.
- **Endpoints vs the U (my observation)**: Europe's β is now "scarcely less" than in the eighteenth–nineteenth centuries (p. 169), yet α is 25–30 rather than 35–40 percent and the pure return 3–4 rather than 4–5 percent (pp. 147, 161). Comparing endpoints, similar β has come with lower r and lower α, which Piketty attributes to a shift in technology toward labor (p. 161), not to σ. So σ > 1 is identified only from the movements within the U: the mid-century trough and the post-1970 rise. The trough was caused by war destruction and asset-price collapse (ch03–ch05), and mid-century returns near 10 percent are said to include unpaid entrepreneurial labor (pp. 146-147). Those are the periods least like a movement along a stable production function.
- **Net vs gross (my observation, from outside the book)**: national income and capital are net of depreciation throughout the book (ch01). The empirical literature on the elasticity of substitution mostly estimates it on gross output and often finds it below one. A net elasticity above one requires less than a gross elasticity above one, but the book does not state which concept its 1.3–1.6 refers to, and the method is in the appendix only (endnote 24, p. 453). For the synthesis, the claim should be recorded as resting on an estimate not shown in the book.
- **Bargaining power as a rival explanation**: conceded on pp. 153 and 160. If institutions rather than technology drive the post-1970 rise, the policy implication shifts toward ch09's institutional account of labor income and ch13–ch15's tax and regulation agenda, which Piketty favours anyway. The concession does not hurt the policy conclusion, but it weakens the claim that the rise is a law-like consequence of β.
- **Pure return vs r > g**: the stable 4–5 percent pure return here (p. 147) is the r in fm01's r > g and ch10's concentration mechanism. Here it is pretax and averaged over all assets; ch10 and ch12 need a post-tax return by size of fortune. This chapter already says returns rise with fortune size (pp. 143-144, 147; endnote 6 points to ch12) and that the average after-tax return "has decreased much more" than the pretax one (p. 149). Those two adjustments pull in opposite directions for the r > g argument; check how ch10 and ch12 handle them.
- **Inflation**: "redistributing wealth among those who have it" (p. 152), with large fortunes best protected. Feeds ch12 (endnote 13, p. 452) and ch15–ch16's rejection of inflation as a substitute for a capital tax.
- **Human capital**: the "Is Human Capital Illusory?" section (pp. 161-162) defers to Part Three; ch07's patrimonial middle class and ch08–ch09's supermanagers take up the question at the individual level. Endnote 36 (p. 454) flags Modigliani's life-cycle vs Kaldor's inheritance views, picked up in ch07 (p. 177) and ch11.
- **Marx**: the reading of Marx as g ≈ 0 (p. 164) recurs in ch17's conclusion; whether that is a fair reading of the falling-rate-of-profit argument (which in Marx turns on the organic composition of capital, not on g) is not argued here.
- **Political economy of statistics**: the Cold War politicization of the share debate (pp. 158-159) anticipates ch07's claim that measurement "is never neutral" (p. 196).
