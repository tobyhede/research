**A. Economophobia, not the end of economic man**

- **Premises**: (p. 208) Current demands are almost all economic. (p. 208) Beliefs are governed by (false) economic doctrines. (p. 208) What has changed is refusal to accept that aims conflict or that anything must be given up.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 208) the "End of Economic Man" misdescribes both the past and the trend; the real phenomenon is revolt against economic necessity.

**B. The epistemic argument for submitting to impersonal forces**

- **Premises**: (pp. 208-209) In a complex society the causes of any individual's change of fortune involve more circumstances than any single mind can grasp. (p. 209) Coordination must therefore take account of facts no individual can survey. (p. 209) Civilization grew through submission to market forces that did this. (p. 209) Refusing to submit to what one cannot understand or attribute to a conscious will is an incomplete rationalism.
  **Unstated premises**: (i) the market's outcomes are the only available mechanism that makes use of dispersed facts (the knowledge argument, stated more fully in ch. 4 and elsewhere in Hayek); (ii) the two alternatives are exhaustive, so partial correction of market outcomes is either harmless or a step toward the second; (iii) the "forces" are not themselves the product of alterable legal and institutional choices in any way that matters to the argument.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 209) the only alternative to submission to the market is submission to the arbitrary power of other men, which will be more painful.

**C. No single purpose in peace**

- **Premises**: (p. 210) Freedom is incompatible with permanent subordination of society to one purpose. (p. 210) War is an exception because the sacrifice is temporary and preserves freedom in the long run.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 210) wartime methods cannot be carried into peace; even the conquest of unemployment must not be pursued "at any price".

**D. Post-war wages and the limits of monetary policy**

- **Premises**: (pp. 210-211) War has moved large numbers into trades that cannot employ them afterwards at the same relative wages. (p. 211) If unions prevent relative wage reductions, the options are compulsory transfer or unemployment. (p. 211) Monetary expansion can adjust relative wages only by raising all others, i.e. by a large general inflation that reduces the real wages concerned covertly. (pp. 211-212) A policy of always securing the monetary maximum of employment requires progressive inflation and blocks reallocation, lowering productivity.
  **Unstated premises**: unemployment of the relevant kind is mainly a problem of relative wages and misallocation between industries, not of deficient aggregate demand (Hayek's position against Keynes, assumed, not argued); money wages are rigid downward only because of union resistance.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 211-212) "full employment" pursued by monetary means is self-defeating; if neither unemployment nor coercion is accepted, only "desperate expedients" remain.

**E. Growth as a political necessity**

- **Premises**: (p. 213) Everyone is convinced material conditions must improve. (p. 213) Democracy will not survive a substantial peacetime fall in living standards or long stagnation. (p. 212) Dispossession of a middle class was decisive for Continental totalitarianism. (p. 212) Growth requires rapid adaptation unobstructed by group claims.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 212-213) economic considerations cannot be damned; redistribution in place of growth is dangerous; protected group standards must give way, with a uniform minimum as the only guaranteed security.

**F. Collectivism is destructive of morals**

- **Premises**: (p. 213) Moral value attaches only to free decisions involving sacrifice of one's own interests. (pp. 213-214) Compelled good conduct earns no praise; unselfishness at others' expense is not a virtue. (p. 214) Collectivism's main promise is relief from responsibility. (p. 215) Collective standards derive from individual ones.
  **Unstated premises**: (i) the scope of free moral decision shrinks in proportion as economic decisions are collectivized, i.e. economic freedom is the chief arena of moral choice (asserted as "the sphere where material circumstances force a choice upon us", p. 214); (ii) political participation and support for collective provision are not themselves exercises of moral responsibility (asserted for elections, p. 215).
  **Conclusion**: collectivism "cannot but be antimoral in its effect, however lofty the ideals to which it owes its birth" (p. 214); the moral sense has been blunted (p. 216).

**G. Propaganda and the Germans**

- **Premises**: (p. 217) Germans know what the traditional values of democratic life are. (p. 217) Collectivist ideas are German ideas of a generation ago. (p. 218) Anti-Nazi Germans want protection from the state, not large-scale organization.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 217-218) the democracies can win over the "decent elements" only by defending their own liberal tradition, not by offering a half-way version of the German path.
