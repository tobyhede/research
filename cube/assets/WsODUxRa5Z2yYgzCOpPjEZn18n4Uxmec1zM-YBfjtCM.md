- **Depends on ch03**: the European comparison, the market-value rule (ch03, p. 86) and the treatment of debt as an internal claim (ch03, p. 83) are carried over.
- **Anticipates ch05**: Europe's higher β "automatically" follows from lower demographic growth (p. 111), and the asset-price rebound is said to explain a "significant part" of the post-1970 recovery, though less than volume effects (p. 108). Both are claims whose mechanism (β = s / g) is introduced only in ch05.
- **Feeds ch05 and ch06 on prices**: the land argument (pp. 108-109) and Rhenish valuation (pp. 104-105) both show that the value of capital depends on prices that respond to abundance and institutions. ch05 (p. 135) generalises: the price of capital "is always in part a social and political construct". This sits uneasily with using market-valued β as the core state variable of the book's laws (my observation).
- **Internal inconsistency in dates (my observation)**: the text gives German inflation of nearly 17 percent "between 1930 and 1950" with prices multiplied by 300 (p. 103), but note 2 gives the period as 1913–1950 (p. 444). Arithmetic favours the note: 17 percent a year for 37 years multiplies prices by about 330, while for 20 years it gives only about 23.
- **Tension on "America had little capital" (my observation)**: the headline claim of a low New World β (p. 108) holds only if slaves are excluded. With slaves, US wealth was "relatively stable" at about four and a half years (p. 115), and southern capital exceeded six years (p. 115). The egalitarian, low-capital America is the North. Synthesis should not cite the 1770–1810 US figure of "three years" without this qualification.
- **Tension with ch03 on Ricardian conditions**: note 8 (p. 445) says 1914–1945 deficits reduced national capital because private saving had already been shocked, unlike Ricardo's Britain. This confirms that ch03's "debt as internal claim" is neutral only conditionally.
- **Link to ch06 and Part Three**: the slave example uses α = 0.6 for labour, r = 5 percent and implies the r on slave capital was 7–8 percent (p. 117), so it presupposes a theory of returns not yet given. ch06 takes up how r is determined.
- **Link to ch13**: the US welfare state's "nondevelopment" is linked to racial inequality (p. 116), a claim not developed here.
