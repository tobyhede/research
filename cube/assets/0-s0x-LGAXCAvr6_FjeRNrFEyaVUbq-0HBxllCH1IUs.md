| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| National Socialism / Naziism | p. 178 | Not an irrational revolt but a doctrine with a long intellectual pedigree: collectivism freed from all traces of an individualist tradition (p. 178). |
| Socialism | p. 179 | Used broadly for anticapitalist, organization-centred doctrine, of Left or Right. Marxism is treated as socialism plus liberal elements (internationalism, democracy) which obstruct socialism's realization (pp. 178-179). Following Plenge, organization is taken as the essence of socialism, traced to early nineteenth-century France (pp. 180-181). |
| Socialists of the Left / of the Right; radical / conservative socialism | p. 179 | Left: Marxist social democracy. Right: Prussian-conservative, nationalist anticapitalism. The chapter's thesis is their fusion (p. 179). "Conservative Socialism" and "Religious Socialism" are slogans of the writers who prepared the atmosphere for National Socialism (p. 186). |
| Liberalism | p. 178 | The individualist tradition identified by its German enemies with England, the ideas of 1789, parliamentarism, the commercial spirit; the common archenemy of both socialisms (pp. 180, 183-186). "Liberal in the old sense" (p. 186). Defined in this chapter almost entirely through its opponents' descriptions. |
| Ideas of 1789 vs. Ideas of 1914 | p. 180 | Plenge's antithesis: the ideal of freedom versus the ideal of organization (p. 180). Sombart had already called the ideas of 1789 commercial ideals (p. 180). |
| Organization | p. 180 | The deliberate central ordering of social and economic life; for Plenge, Ostwald and the engineers the essence of socialism and Germany's discovery (pp. 180-182). Links to "planning" in the rest of the book (the equation is made explicitly at p. 190 in ch13). |
| Volksgemeinschaft | p. 180 | The German idea of the state as a people's community in which the individual has no rights but only duties (p. 180); Plenge's people's community of national socialism, born of the 1914 war economy (p. 181). Editor: people's community, later with racial implication under the Nazis (p. 187, note 8). |
| Commercial vs. heroic (Händler und Helden) | p. 179 | Sombart's opposition of English commercial civilization, which seeks individual happiness, to German heroic culture, which subordinates the individual to the higher life of people and state (pp. 179-180). The hostile mirror image of Hayek's own commercial/military distinction (ch09 p. 148; ch10 p. 163). |
| Prussianism | p. 184 | Spengler: the instinct that power belongs to the whole, everyone given his place, command and obedience; identical with authoritarian socialism (pp. 184-185). |
| Beamtenstaat | p. 185 | Spengler's state of the future in which everybody is a state official with pay fixed by the state (p. 185). Reused from ch09 (p. 152), where Hayek applied it descriptively to pre-Nazi Germany. |
| Edelnazis | p. 186 | The Tat circle around Ferdinand Fried; chief intellectual exponents of the fused tradition in the late 1920s (p. 186). |
| War hysteria of 1914 | p. 179 | The never-cured wartime mentality that Hayek dates as the beginning of the modern development producing National Socialism (p. 179). |
