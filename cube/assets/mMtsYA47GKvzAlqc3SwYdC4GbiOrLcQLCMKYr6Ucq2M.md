| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Nazi-Socialism" memo | p. 7 | Hayek's memorandum to Beveridge, dated by Caldwell to May or early June 1933, arguing that National Socialism is a genuine socialist movement; the seed of the book (Caldwell's description of Hayek's document). |
| "Freedom and the Economic System" | p. 8 | Hayek's April 1938 magazine article and its expanded 1939 pamphlet version; the intermediate stages between the memo and the book (Caldwell). |
| Abuse of Reason project | p. 10 | Hayek's unfinished large intellectual history "from Saint-Simon to Hitler", in two parts called Hubris and Nemesis; The Road to Serfdom grew out of its last part (Caldwell, drawing on Hayek's letters) (pp. 10-11). |
| Planning | p. 9 | In Caldwell's account of the 1930s, a word used across the political spectrum with no agreed content; Robbins (quoted) calls it a panacea of ambiguous meaning (p. 10). Caldwell distinguishes central planning from the liberal planning of a general system of rules, which is Hayek's 1939 distinction (p. 8). |
| Market socialism (also "competitive socialism", "pseudo-competition") | p. 9 | Lange's and Durbin's scheme: markets for consumer goods and labour, public ownership of the means of production, a Central Planning Board setting prices by trial and error (Caldwell's summary) (p. 21). "Pseudo-competition" is Hayek's 1935 label (p. 9). |
| War economy | p. 12 | Neurath's doctrine that wartime central planning is a model for running a peacetime economy (Caldwell's report) (p. 12). |
| Inevitability thesis / slippery slope | p. 23 | The reading, which Caldwell rejects, that Hayek said a little planning dooms a society to totalitarianism (Caldwell's label for the critics' reading). |
| Logical argument (vs historical argument) | p. 25 | Caldwell's own classification: Hayek's claim is that full-scale central planning cannot be combined with the freedoms liberal socialists value; it is not a prediction about where history must go. |
| Welfare state | p. 26 | For Caldwell, a thing distinct from socialism (full nationalization plus central planning); Hayek treated its dangers separately and later (p. 26). |
| Tract for the times | p. 26 | Hayek's own phrase, as reported by Caldwell, for the book's occasional character. |
| Definitive Edition | p. 3 | Subtitle proposed by the University of Chicago Press; the text is the reset 1944 American edition with silent corrections (Caldwell, Editorial Foreword). |
