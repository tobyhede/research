- Is the dichotomy on p. 209 (market forces or arbitrary power of men) defensible given Hayek's own acceptance of a minimum income, retraining, regulated monopoly (ch. 13) and gradual decontrol? What principle marks permitted correction from the beginning of "mastering the forces of society"?
- Does Hayek's account of unemployment (relative wages, misallocation) survive comparison with the demand-deficiency account he does not address? Note he grants a short-run monetary maximum exists (p. 211) but says it is unsustainable.
- Is the claim that democracy cannot survive stagnation or falling standards (p. 213) consistent with asking people to accept lower relative positions and restrained consumption (pp. 211-212)? He seems to need both acceptance of individual loss and aggregate growth.
- Is the definition of morals (pp. 213-214) too narrow? It excludes by definition any moral worth in collectively organized provision and treats voting as morally empty (p. 215). Synthesis should compare with opponents' view that institutions can embody moral commitments.
- What is the evidence for moral decline over fifty years (pp. 213-216)? None is given beyond examples without instances. Is the claim falsifiable?
- "Inevitable and undeniable" (p. 214) versus the book's repeated rejection of inevitability: is this conceptual necessity (from the definition) or prediction?
- How does the national-character argument (Anglo-Saxon virtues, pp. 216-217) square with Hayek's rejection in ch. 12 of explanations of Nazism from German national character? He appears to hold that institutions and ideas form character (p. 216), which would reconcile them; check ch. 12.
- Who are the "supposed liberals" who countenance population transfers and condone atrocities (pp. 215-216)? Unnamed; the reference to "powers with whose professed principles they sympathize" presumably means the Soviet Union, but Hayek does not say so.
