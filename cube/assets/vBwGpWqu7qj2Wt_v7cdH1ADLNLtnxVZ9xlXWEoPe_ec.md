- How reliable are eighteenth-century balance sheets built from King, Petty, Vauban and Boisguillebert (note 1, p. 443)? These are contemporary conjectural estimates, not records; the U-curve's left arm rests on them. Check the online appendix or later critiques before treating 1700–1800 levels as data.
- Is the composition change (land to housing and business capital) more than relabelling? Note 1 says half of eighteenth-century "other domestic capital" was farm buildings and livestock, and ch05 says farmland value was mostly accumulated improvements (p. 141). The categories are partly an accounting convention.
- The chapter's per-capita examples use six years for 1700 and 1910 while the series show seven (notes 8-9, p. 443). Minor, but any synthesis quoting the 120,000 euros of land in 1700 should use the note's 150,000.
- Who actually held public debt in nineteenth-century Britain and France? The distributional claim (pp. 93, 98) rests on literary evidence and on later wealth-concentration data; it needs the ch10 estate series to be tested.
- The rate on nineteenth-century bonds (4–5 percent) is quoted as exceeding growth (p. 94). Did British bondholders' heirs actually accumulate, or consume the interest? The answer depends on saving out of capital income, which ch10 and ch11 model.
- "Without really understanding why" (p. 101) is a strong interpretive claim about French politics with no evidence offered in the chapter.
