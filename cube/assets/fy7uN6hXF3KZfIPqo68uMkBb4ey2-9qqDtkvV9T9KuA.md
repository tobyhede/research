| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| β (capital/income ratio) | p. 119 | Market value of capital divided by annual *national* income. In this book an unqualified "capital/income ratio" always means national income in the denominator (p. 130). Applies to national capital or to private capital depending on context; the 1970–2010 analysis uses private capital (p. 123). |
| s (savings rate) | p. 119 | Net new saving divided by national income: saving "net of capital depreciation, that is, truly new savings" (p. 128); see also note 2 (p. 446). Private saving includes household saving and firms' retained earnings (p. 127). |
| g (growth rate) | p. 120 | Growth rate of *total* national income, i.e. per capita growth plus population growth (p. 120). Note 3 (p. 446) gives the alternative notation β = s / (g + n). |
| Second fundamental law of capitalism (β = s / g) | p. 119 | The long-run equilibrium capital/income ratio toward which an economy tends if it saves s and grows at g permanently (pp. 119-120). Asymptotic, dynamic, "never perfectly realized in practice" (p. 121). |
| First fundamental law (α = r × β) | p. 121 | Recalled from ch01: capital's share of income α equals the average return r times β. "a pure accounting identity, valid at all times in all places" (p. 121), in contrast to the dynamic second law. |
| Accumulable capital vs pure natural resources | p. 121 | β = s / g covers only capital humans can accumulate. Pure natural resources, "whose value is independent of any human improvement and any past investment", can raise β without saving (p. 121). |
| Pure land | p. 141 | Land "prior to any human improvements" (p. 141); estimated at no more than half to one year of national income in both eighteenth-century farmland and twenty-first-century urban land (pp. 141-142). |
| Relative asset prices; volume vs price effects | pp. 121-122 | Asset prices relative to consumer prices. Volume effects come from new saving; price effects from capital gains or losses on existing assets. Short-run price effects often exceed volume effects (p. 122); the law needs relative prices to balance out over the long run (pp. 121-122). |
| Retained earnings | p. 127 | Profits reinvested by firms on behalf of their owners; up to half of private saving in some countries (p. 127). Counting them as saving turns most apparent long-run share-price gains into volume effects (p. 127). |
| Depreciation | p. 128 | Wear and tear on buildings and equipment, about 10–15 percent of national income, absorbing nearly half of gross saving of 25–30 percent (p. 128). Gross version of the law: β = s / (g + δ) (note 12, p. 447). |
| Durable goods / valuables | pp. 128-129 | Household durables (30–50 percent of national income) are excluded from wealth, following national-accounting standards; valuables (art, jewellery, gold, 5–10 percent) are included (pp. 128-129). |
| Disposable income | p. 129 | National income minus taxes and obligatory payments plus monetary transfers; about 70–80 percent of national income today, 90 percent before 1900 (pp. 129-130). Rejected as β's denominator because it omits transfers in kind (p. 130). |
| Foundations (moral persons) | p. 130 | Nonprofits financed mainly by private gifts or their own property, counted in private wealth; always under 10 percent and generally under 5 percent of private wealth (pp. 130-131). |
| Privatisation | p. 132 | Transfer of public wealth into private hands, including via public deficits financed by private bondholders; one of three factors behind capital's comeback (pp. 132-134). |
| Tobin's Q | p. 136 | Ratio of a firm's (or a country's firms') stock-market value to book value (p. 136). Book value is all balance-sheet assets minus debt (p. 135). |
| New patrimonial capitalism | p. 125 | The "strong comeback of private capital in the rich countries since 1970" (p. 125). |
| Financialisation (gross positions) | p. 139 | Growth of total financial assets and liabilities faster than net wealth: from four to five years of national income in the early 1970s to ten to fifteen (twenty in Britain) by 2010 (p. 139). |
