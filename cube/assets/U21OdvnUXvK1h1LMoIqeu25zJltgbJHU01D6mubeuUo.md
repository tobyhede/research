- **Depends on ch09**: the rejection of marginal productivity at the top (pp. 373-374) and the luck/talent finding (p. 376) are explicitly carried over from ch09. The top-rate argument is only as strong as ch09's critique.
- **Depends on ch11**: Rastignac's dilemma and the lifetime-resources criterion (p. 363) come from ch11; so does the claim that inheritance is gaining importance.
- **Depends on ch10**: the claim that progressive taxation of top incomes and estates "partly explains" why concentration never returned to Belle Époque levels (p. 362) is the policy side of ch10's account of why 1914–1945 broke concentration.
- **Feeds ch15**: three lines lead straight into the capital tax: (a) capital income escaping the income tax through competition (p. 362); (b) the knowledge-producing function of even a low tax (p. 369), which becomes the 0.1% "compulsory reporting" tax in ch15 (p. 382); (c) the deferral of small-country coordination to "the next chapter" (p. 377).
- **Contradicted in ch15 (my observation)**: the closing prophecy that the US "may be on the verge of becoming the Old Europe" (p. 378) is walked back in ch15, where immigration keeps the US "still a long way" from it in ch15 (p. 398), referring back to this chapter explicitly.
- **Hardening of the causal claim (my observation)**: p. 362 says the fall in progressivity "probably explains much" of the rise in top earned incomes; p. 376 says only dissuasive taxation "can do the job". The hedge disappears once the PSS results are introduced, though p. 376 itself acknowledges social norms as an independent factor.
- **Causation versus capture (my observation)**: Argument D treats tax cuts as the cause of rising top pay; p. 377 raises the possibility that the 1% captured the US political process. If capture is real, the correlation on p. 373 is partly reverse causation, which the chapter does not address.
- **War versus deliberation (my observation)**: the historical thesis is that war, not universal suffrage, created progressive taxation (p. 377); the policy thesis relies on "collective deliberation and democratic experimentation" (p. 376) and on "ideas and intellectual debate" (p. 377). Piketty acknowledges the gap ("natural optimism", p. 377) but does not resolve it. This is the book's general problem of agency for its proposals; compare ch16 and ch17.
- **"Liberal" confiscation (my observation)**: p. 370 calls confiscatory rates liberal because they work through incentives under democratic rules. The same move recurs in ch15, where the capital tax is "the liberal form of capital control" in ch15 (p. 397). Useful for a cross-book theme against Hayek (road-to-serfdom ch06 treats general tax rules aimed at distributive outcomes as the pressure point of the Rule of Law).
- **Revenue versus regulation**: confiscatory rates are explicitly not for revenue (p. 370), (p. 377); revenue comes from 50–60% rates lower down. The same split between regulating and financing reappears in ch15 (p. 381).
