The chapter's evidence is mostly the book's own national-accounts series reused, plus recent policy episodes. Little new data appears; the policy analysis leans on hypotheticals and illustrative arithmetic.

- **Structure of European national wealth**: about six years of national income, mostly private; public assets ≈ public debt ≈ one year; private wealth half real estate, half financial; net foreign position near balance (p. 400). Source: Part Two series, note 1 points to Table 3.1 (p. 501). Note 2: counting tax-haven assets, Europe's foreign position is "significantly positive" (Figure 12.6) (p. 501).
- **Wealth concentration**: top 10% own 60% of European wealth (p. 401), from ch10.
- **Debt levels**: rich-world average about 90% of GDP; emerging economies about 30% (p. 400). No source given in text.
- **Privatisation equivalence**: sale of all public assets would roughly pay the debt, "according to the national accounts" (p. 401; note 3 cites chs. 3–5 and the online appendix).
- **Simulations**: flat 15% levy ≈ nearly a year of national income (p. 401); 0/1/2% annual schedule ≈ 2% of European GDP (from ch15, p. 389); one-time 0/10/20% ≈ 20% of GDP (p. 403); other schedules in online Supplemental Table S15.1 (note 7, p. 501). The tenfold scaling assumes the tax base does not shrink under much higher rates; behavioural response is not discussed.
- **Historical precedent**: France's 1945 exceptional capital tax, progressive 0–25% (p. 403; note 8 refers to ch10).
- **Inflation arithmetic and history**: 5% vs 2% inflation cuts real debt/GDP >15% (p. 403); France 13% and Germany 17% average inflation 1913–1950 (p. 404); France >50% a year 1945–1948 (p. 405); German prices ×100 million in 1923 (p. 405). Note 10: much of Germany's postwar debt was cancelled by the Allies (citing Albrecht Ritschl, LSE 2012) (p. 502). Note 13: French response to 1945–1948 was the 1956 old-age minimum and PAYGO pensions (p. 502).
- **Austerity arithmetic**: 2% growth, 1% deficit, zero inflation → about 1 point a year off debt/GDP (p. 404; note 11, p. 502). Britain 1815–1914 primary surpluses 2–3% of GDP; debt interest exceeded education spending (p. 404; Chapter 3 cross-reference). European university spending under 1% of GDP (p. 405).
- **Central banks**: Friedman and Schwartz, *A Monetary History of the United States, 1857–1960* (1963) (p. 407; note 19, p. 503); balance sheets Fed and BoE from 10 to >20% of GDP, ECB 15 to 30% (p. 410); private wealth 500–600% of GDP (p. 410); note 22: gross financial assets and liabilities ten to twenty years of GDP; Bank of Japan near 40% of GDP in summer 2013; Banque de France 90–100% in 1944–1945 (pp. 503-504); 2012–2013 government borrowing at just over 1% (pp. 409-410); note 21: 6–7% for Italy and Spain, 15% for Greece in 2011–2012 (p. 503). Note 25: Banque de France governed by its 200 largest shareholders 1803–1936, nationalised 1945 (p. 504). Note 26: ECB's December 2009 collateral announcement on Greek bonds (p. 504).
- **Cyprus**: one million inhabitants, EU 2004, euro 2008; Russian deposits drawn by low taxes; proposed rates 6.75% and 9.9%; replacement exempting deposits under €100,000 (pp. 412-413). Sourced to "statements by officials of the Troika" (p. 412) and conversations with officials (p. 413; note 24, p. 504). Piketty stresses that no depositor statistics were published (p. 412), so the "Russian millionaires" characterisation (p. 413) is itself unverified.
- **Euro**: Europe nearly one-quarter of world GDP in 2013 (p. 414); Maastricht 1992, notes 1 January 2002 (p. 414); rates near-identical 2002–2007 (p. 415).
- **Corporate tax**: Ireland and Eastern European countries' low-rate strategies (p. 417); Google managers' "positive externality" defence (note 33, p. 505); Belgium's 2006 allowance for corporate equity (note 36, p. 506); FTT revenue unlikely above 0.5% of GDP (note 38, p. 506).
- **Golden rule**: Phelps 1961, "A Fable for Growthmen" (p. 419; note 40, p. 506), also Allais 1947, von Neumann 1945, Malinvaud 1953; Keynes's *General Theory* chs. 16 and 24 (note 44, p. 507). The β estimates for r = 1–1.5% (10–15 years) and r = 0.2% (20–30 years) are explicit guesses ("Perhaps", "maybe even more", p. 420).
- **Climate**: Stern Report (2006); damage "dozens of points of global GDP per year" in some scenarios; Stern 1–1.5% discount and ≥5% of GDP mitigation vs Nordhaus 4–4.5% (p. 424). Note 52 cites Guesnerie and Sterner on limited substitutability of natural capital (p. 509). Note 54: net public investment in rich countries about 1–1.5% of GDP (p. 509).
- **Transparency**: Lonmin/Marikana, thirty-four strikers killed August 2012 (p. 426; first told p. 31). Note 57: the 1790 "compendium of pensions" with 23,000 names (p. 510). Note 58: wages are aggregated with intermediate inputs in published accounts (p. 510). Note 59: Rancière (p. 510).
- **Bare assertion / language outrunning evidence**: privatisation advocated by "some very serious people" (p. 401), unnamed; setback to British education "may" explain British decline (p. 404); progressive capital tax faces "purely ideological obstacles" (p. 414); Nordhaus's view "opportunely consistent with the US strategy of unrestricted carbon emissions" (p. 424), an imputation of motive with no evidence.
