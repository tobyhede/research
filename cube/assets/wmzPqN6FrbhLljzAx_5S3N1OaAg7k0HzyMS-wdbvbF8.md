**A. The capital share is the product of β and r (p. 143)**

- **Premises**: (p. 143) α = r × β is an accounting identity (from ch01). (p. 142) β is fixed in the long run by s/g, with s and g "largely independent" of each other.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 143) With β known, the question of the split reduces to how r is determined.
- **Note**: the identity holds by construction because r is computed as capital income over capital (p. 144). It carries no causal content; the causal content enters only with Argument C.

**B. The pure return has been roughly constant (pp. 143-148)**

- **Premises**: (p. 144) Observed average returns, computed from national accounts, ran 5–6, 7–8, then 4–5 percent. (p. 146) National accounts omit the informal labor of managing one's own wealth, and that labor is likely larger in fast-growth or high-inflation periods. (p. 147) Deducting an estimate of it lowers returns by 1–2 points. (pp. 147-148) Traditional conversion of capital to rent at 5 (or 4) percent, and novelists' treatment of higher yields on active investments as pay for labor, confirm a pure return of about 4–5 percent.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 147) The pure return has oscillated around 4–5 percent with no pronounced trend, possibly drifting to 3–4 percent.
- **Unstated premise**: that the correction for informal management labor is calibrated independently of the conclusion. It is admitted to be "quite difficult—and to some extent arbitrary" (p. 146), and the correction is largest exactly where observed returns depart most from 4–5 percent (postwar returns near 10 percent, p. 146). The stability of the *pure* return is therefore partly produced by the adjustment.

**C. The elasticity criterion (pp. 155-157), reconstructed**

- **Premises**: (pp. 152-153) Under competition r equals the marginal product of capital. (p. 155) The marginal product falls as β rises. (p. 156) How fast it falls is governed by the elasticity of substitution.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 157) α rises with β if and only if σ > 1; falls if σ < 1; constant at σ = 1 (Cobb-Douglas).
- **Reconstruction (mine; the text relegates the algebra to the online appendix, endnotes 15–17, p. 452)**: with a constant-elasticity production function and r equal to the marginal product, r is proportional to β^(−1/σ) and α = r × β is proportional to β^(1 − 1/σ). So "r falls less than proportionately" (p. 156) is the same statement as 1/σ < 1. Two conditions are built in: r equals the marginal product (Piketty states this assumption each time, p. 157), and β measures a volume of capital along a single production function.
- **Wording**: the English text says that with σ > 1 a rise in β "leads instead to a drop in the marginal productivity of capital, so that" α rises (p. 157). The qualifier that the drop is *small* (less than proportional) is missing; the intended sense is clear from p. 156.

**D. History shows σ > 1 in the long run (pp. 156, 160)**

- **Premises**: (p. 156) In Britain and France α followed the U-curve of β, though shallower; r rose when capital was scarce after 1945 but not enough to invert the U. (p. 160) Capital keeps finding new uses (solar panels, robots, medical technology). (p. 160) Figure 6.5: α rose in most rich countries 1970–2010 as β rose.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 160) Over the very long run σ "seems to have been greater than one", estimated at 1.3–1.6.
- **Concession inside the argument**: (p. 160) the 1970–2010 co-movement is equally consistent with rising bargaining power of capital (capital mobility, tax competition), and the two "have reinforced each other". The data therefore do not separate the elasticity explanation from the institutional one.
- **Evidential status of 1.3–1.6**: endnote 24 says only "See the online technical appendix" (p. 453). No method, sample or standard error is given in the book.

**E. Agricultural societies had σ < 1 (p. 161)**

- **Premises**: In the eighteenth–nineteenth centuries US land values (β and rents) were much lower than Europe's despite far more land. For any single form of capital the price effect eventually outweighs the volume effect.
- **Conclusion**: σ was "significantly less than one" in agrarian economies; capital substitutes readily for labor only when it takes many forms.
- **Note**: this is a cross-sectional comparison (US vs Europe) used to infer a technological parameter. Piketty says it is "the only way to explain" the land-value gap, a strong claim with no alternative (institutions, transport costs, labor scarcity, ch04's treatment of slaves as capital) considered here.

**F. Rising β will probably raise α (pp. 160-162, 168-169)**

- **Premises**: (p. 168) Slower growth, especially demographic, raises β via s/g. (p. 169) Global s ≈ 10 percent and g ≈ 1–1.5 percent give β of 6–10. (p. 169) σ is "probably greater than one". (p. 169) Technological change favouring labor is limited in size and may be offset by financial sophistication and international competition for capital.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 169) α could reach 30–40 percent of global income, near eighteenth–nineteenth-century levels, and might go higher; (pp. 160-161) nothing self-corrects.
- **Check against his own numbers (my calculation)**: take today's rich-country α ≈ 27.5 percent and β ≈ 5.5, so r ≈ 5 percent. With σ in his 1.3–1.6 range, a rise of β to 7–8 lowers r to roughly 4.0–4.3 percent and gives α of about 29–32 percent. The top of his "30 or 40 percent" (β = 8 at r = 5 percent) requires r not to fall at all, which is the infinite-elasticity case he calls irrelevant (p. 157). His own elasticity estimate supports the low end of the range, not 40 percent.

**G. Marx's contradiction is real but avoided by growth (pp. 164-166)**

- **Premises**: (p. 164) Read Marx as β = s/g with g ≈ 0. (p. 165) With s > 0 and g → 0, β → ∞, so either r → 0 or α → 1. (endnote 32, p. 453) r avoids zero only with infinite elasticity. (p. 165) Only productivity and population growth balance accumulation.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 165) The dynamic inconsistency Marx saw corresponds to "a real difficulty"; modern growth avoided the "apocalypse" (p. 170).
- **Interpretive choice**: Piketty concedes Marx's prose was not "limpid" and offers this as "one logically consistent way of interpreting" him (p. 164); the textile-factory accounts showing a capital/output ratio above ten are offered as confirmation (p. 165).

**Position versus argument.** Piketty's claim that the capital share will rise rests on two separable steps: the β forecast (from ch05, s/g) and σ > 1 (this chapter). The second is the hinge. He states it with hedges throughout ("seems", "probably", "apparently"), yet the chapter's summary claims ("no self-corrective mechanism", p. 161; "no natural force", p. 169) are stated without hedges.
