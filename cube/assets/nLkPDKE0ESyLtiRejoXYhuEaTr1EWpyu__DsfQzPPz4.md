- **Builds on ch10 and ch11**: ch10's explanation of concentration through r > g assumed one average return. This chapter adds return heterogeneity as a second mechanism (p. 314). Note 19 admits that ch11 applied a uniform return to all fortunes, which makes Bettencourt look like "a very partial heir" (p. 478). So the ch11 heir/self-made decomposition and this chapter's size-dependent returns pull against each other: high returns on inherited fortunes get counted as "accumulated", not "inherited", wealth.
- **Uses ch05**: the central scenario for global β (Figures 12.4–12.5) and the 10 percent long-run saving rate (p. 337). Rising gross foreign positions (p. 340) refer back to ch05.
- **Uses ch01 and ch02**: compound interest (p. 314) and inflation stable at about 2 percent since the 1980s (p. 330).
- **Feeds ch13**: the funded-pension scenario for China (p. 338) and note 47's pointer to ch13 (p. 481) anticipate ch13's defence of PAYGO. Small savers' difficulty in getting the average return (p. 332) is cited in ch13 n. 47 (p. 487). Harvard's endowment reappears in ch13's discussion of elite university access (ch13 n. 29, p. 486).
- **Feeds ch15**: each section ends by pointing to a progressive capital tax (pp. 320, 323-324, 326, 333, 340). The case for it is built mostly on unequal returns and the need for transparency. Annual wealth declarations are proposed already on p. 321.
- **Feeds ch16**: inflation's virtues are deferred to the discussion of central banks (p. 333).
- **Tension on data quality (my observation)**: the global wealth reports are called "useless for studying long-term evolutions or even reliably detecting trends" (p. 318), yet the headline comparison of today's global concentration with Europe in 1900–1910 (p. 318) rests on them. A comparison of levels across a century is a trend claim in all but name.
- **Tension on direction of global inequality**: p. 319 says it is "by no means certain" that global wealth inequality is rising, while p. 339 says oligarchic divergence is "already well under way". The two are reconciled only by restricting the second to the very top. The evidence for the very top is the Forbes data the chapter calls unreliable, plus endowments, which are institutions, not families.
- **Tension on prodigality**: the family-dissipation argument (p. 329) implies an equilibrium distribution, yet the chapter's projections (p. 319; n. 5, p. 476) assume no dissipation. Piketty concedes convergence "toward a certain equilibrium" (p. 329) but does not say where that equilibrium lies or how it compares with today.
- **Ironic symmetry**: he criticises others for asserting the merits of Gates and Slim without facts (p. 324; n. 20, p. 478). In the same paragraph he asserts that Gates profited from "a virtual monopoly" right after saying he knows "virtually nothing" about how Gates became rich (p. 324).
- **Cross-book link (Hayek, Road to Serfdom ch06)**: Piketty's case for taxation over moral judgment uses rule-of-law language: rules "that apply equally to everyone, regardless of nationality" (p. 325), a tax that treats situations "in a supple, consistent, and predictable manner" (p. 324). This is close to Hayek's formal-rule criterion, used for a redistributive end Hayek thought formal rules excluded. Useful for a theme on general rules and distributive aims.
- **Oil states as oligarchic**: note 49 reclassifies the oil-state threat as oligarchic, since the rents go to a small number of people (p. 481). This weakens the clean contrast between international and oligarchic divergence drawn in the text (pp. 338-339).
