- **Author's claim:** large fortunes earn higher average returns, through economies of scale and a greater capacity to bear risk. That amplifies r > g at the top even when it is modest overall. [Ch 12, line 13019](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L13019) [Ch 12, line 13038](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L13038)
- **Evidence:** from 1987 to 2013 the largest fortunes grew 6.4–6.8% a year in real terms, against 2.1% for average wealth. [Ch 12, line 13117](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L13117) Returns on US university endowments for 1980–2010 rise with size: 6.2% for the smallest, 8.8% for those over $1 billion, 10.2% for Harvard, Yale and Princeton. [Ch 12, line 13537](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L13537)
- **Boundary:** Piketty says the Forbes data have serious biases. The endowment results may partly reflect a rebound in asset prices, and extending them to families is an analogy. [Ch 12, line 13076](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L13076) [Ch 12, line 13613](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L13613)
- **Application [Application]:** treat average-return figures as a floor for the very wealthy and a ceiling for small savers.
