Everything in this section is **[Analysis]** unless it carries a source link.

- **Strongest contribution:** the data. Two centuries of income-tax, estate and national-wealth series, assembled with many collaborators ([Introduction, line 744](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L744)), changed what the inequality debate is about. The French inheritance flow, rebuilt by two independent methods that agree, is the best single series in the book ([Ch 11, line 11566](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L11566)).
- **Weakest claim:** the forecast. It needs three things to hold together: frontier growth stays at 1–1.5% “no matter what economic policies are adopted” ([Conclusion, line 17432](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L17432)), r stays near 4–5% while β rises, and saving does not fall as growth falls. Each is argued separately. Their joint persistence over a century is assumed. The elasticity above one that keeps r up is the thinnest link, and Piketty concedes the bargaining-power alternative ([Ch 6, line 6798](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L6798)).
- **Hidden assumptions:**
  - Market value is the right measure of capital, so asset-price swings count as changes in wealth.
  - Tax records capture the top well enough once corrected for evasion. Piketty says his top shares are lower bounds ([Ch 8, line 8564](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L8564)).
  - France is the more representative future, because its demography looks more like the world's to come ([Introduction, line 1125](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L1125)).
- **Meaningful counterargument:** the rise in β since 1970 is partly a price effect, not accumulation. Piketty's own decomposition puts asset prices at a quarter to a third of the rise in national capital ([Ch 5, line 5852](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5852)). And the argument that rising US inequality comes mostly from wages ([Ch 8, line 9110](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L9110)) sits awkwardly with a book whose title and engine are capital. The two stories meet only in the forecast that today's supermanagers become tomorrow's rentiers ([Ch 11, line 11965](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L11965)).
- **Question left unanswered:** political feasibility. Piketty grants that the global tax needs “a very high and no doubt unrealistic level of international cooperation” ([Ch 15, line 15561](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L15561)). His own history says the last great equalization took two world wars ([Ch 14, line 15517](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L15517)). The book does not show how a peaceful route gets built.
