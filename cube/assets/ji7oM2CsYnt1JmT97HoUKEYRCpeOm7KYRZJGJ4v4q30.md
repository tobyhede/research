- **Tax revenue series, 1870–2010** (Figure 13.1, p. 345): the US, Britain, France and Sweden, with decennial averages (n. 7, p. 483). Sources at piketty.pse.ens.fr/capital21c. The figure is an image only in this file, and the numbers in the text are the only values available here. Definitions: all compulsory payments to all levels of government (n. 1, p. 482), as a share of national income, not GDP (n. 4, pp. 482-483). Gaps of 5–10 points between countries are "real", while a few points may be statistical (n. 5, p. 483). Japan is at 32–33 percent, and Canada, Australia and New Zealand at 35–40 (n. 8, p. 483).
- **Spending composition**: Supplemental Table S13.2 for France, Germany, Britain and the US, 2000–2010 (online only; n. 10, p. 483). Education 5–6 percent and health 8–9 percent of national income (n. 11, p. 483). US total health spending including private insurance is nearly 20 percent of national income, against 10–12 percent in Europe (n. 13, p. 483). The claim that universal systems have a better cost-benefit ratio is asserted "There is no doubt" (n. 13, p. 483) without data in the file.
- **Historical social spending**: Lindert, Growing Public (2004), cited for the slow nineteenth-century development (n. 3, p. 482).
- **Military spending**: 2–3 percent of national income generally, over 4 percent in the US, over 10 percent in Saudi Arabia and the Gulf states (n. 2, p. 482).
- **Welfare take-up**: fewer than half of eligible people applied for France's active solidarity income (n. 16, p. 484). US incarceration: about 1 percent of adults, over 5 percent of adult black males (n. 17, p. 484). Piketty's interpretation that the "carceral state sometimes does the job of the welfare state" (n. 17, p. 484) is his, with racial prejudice offered as a partial explanation of US attitudes (n. 17, p. 484; n. 24, p. 485).
- **Normative texts**: the US Declaration of Independence (n. 19, p. 484), the 1789 Declaration article 1 (p. 349), Rawls's A Theory of Justice and Political Liberalism (n. 22, p. 485; the page given there, "15", looks garbled), Sen, Fleurbaey and Roemer (n. 23, p. 485).
- **Mobility**: Björklund (Sweden) and Lefranc (France) on cohort trends (n. 26, p. 485). Jäntti on international correlations, "also confirmed by twin studies" (n. 28, p. 485). Coefficients 0.2–0.3 (Sweden, Finland), 0.4–0.5 (Britain), 0.4 (Germany, France), 0.5–0.6 (US) (n. 28, p. 485).
- **US college access**: Duncan and Murnane, Whither Opportunity? (2011), ch. 6 (n. 30, p. 486). Harvard cost $54,000 in 2012–2013, tuition $38,000 (n. 29, pp. 485-486). Meer and Rosen (2009) on alumni giving timed to children's college age (n. 31, p. 486). The $450,000 figure for Harvard parents is Piketty's estimate "by comparing various sources of data" (p. 354). Note 33 says such statistics "are very difficult to obtain" (p. 486).
- **Sciences Po**: parents' average income about 90,000 euros from 2011–2012 data (n. 37, p. 486). Fees range from zero to 10,000 euros a year (n. 40, p. 487). The full distribution is in the online appendix.
- **Tuition levels**: British caps of £1,000 (1998), £3,000 (2004) and £9,000 (2012) (n. 34, p. 486, citing Carpentier 2012). Bavaria and Lower Saxony abolished 500-euro fees in 2013 (n. 35, p. 486). Shanghai rankings: 53 of the top 100 universities in the US against 31 in Europe, but 150 against 202 in the top 500 (n. 38, pp. 486-487).
- **Primary and secondary resource allocation in France**: Piketty and Valdenaire (2006) on less experienced teachers in disadvantaged schools (n. 36, p. 486).
- **Boutmy, 1871**: the founding rationale of Sciences Po (p. 355; n. 42, p. 487, with Favre 1981).
- **Pensions**: PAYGO return equal to g is a definitional and theoretical statement (p. 356). The r and g figures point to Figures 10.9–10.11 (n. 44, p. 487), which are Piketty's own projections. The 5–10 times volatility ratio (p. 357) has no source in text or note. Swedish reform and Bozio and Piketty (2008) for the individual-accounts model (n. 46, p. 487). Masson (2009) on the "multi-solidarity" model (n. 43, p. 487).
- **Poor countries**: tax shares by region, 1970s–1990s (pp. 358-359), source not given in the note apparatus beyond Cagé and Gadenne for customs duties (n. 48, pp. 487-488). Piketty and Qian (2009) on China and India, linking fiscal capacity to the spread of wage labour (n. 50, p. 488). Note 49 cites Banerjee and Duflo's Poor Economics approvingly on randomized trials for problems specific to poor countries, but holds that development economics "tends to neglect actual historical experience" (p. 488).
