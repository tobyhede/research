| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Totalitarianism | p. 154 | The system in which a single plan and single set of ends dominate the whole of society; treated as what collectivism becomes in practice (pp. 154, 158). "Collectivist" and "totalitarian" are used almost interchangeably through the chapter (p. 155: "a collectivist or totalitarian system"). |
| Moral results (vs. moral basis) of collectivism | p. 155 | Not whether existing morals demand collectivism, but what moral views a collectivist organization will produce or be ruled by (p. 155). |
| "Strong" (party/man) | p. 155 | Not a numerical majority but a body of support solid enough to carry out whatever it wants; the military-style party (p. 155). |
| Negative principles of selection | p. 156 | The three mechanisms by which a large homogeneous group is assembled: lowest common denominator, the docile and gullible, a negative program against an enemy (pp. 156-157). |
| "Mass" | p. 156 | Used in the avowedly derogatory sense: the least original and independent (p. 156). |
| Particularism | p. 158 | The necessarily limited scope of any collectivism: nationalism, racialism or classism. Opposed to the "wide humanitarianism" of liberalism (p. 158). |
| Liberal socialism | p. 158 | Socialism as Western opinion imagines it, internationalist and humane; said to be purely theoretical, whereas practice is everywhere totalitarian (p. 158). |
| Power | p. 160 | Power over men consciously wielded by other men (p. 160). Hayek's own note distinguishes this from the impersonal sense of power as any determining cause (p. 168, note 12). To the individualist the arch-evil; to the strict collectivist a goal in itself (p. 160). Not a fixed quantity: concentration heightens it, decentralization reduces its absolute amount (pp. 160-161). |
| Economic power vs. political power | p. 161 | Economic power in private hands is never exclusive or complete, never power over a person's whole life; centralized as political power it creates dependence scarcely distinguishable from slavery (p. 161). |
| Individualist ethics | p. 161 | Rules that are general and absolute, prescribing or prohibiting types of action irrespective of the purpose in the particular case; leave the individual conscience free to apply its own rules (pp. 161-162). Explicitly paralleled to formal law / the Rule of Law (p. 161). |
| Collectivist ethics | p. 161 | A system in which the good of the whole is the only criterion; the end justifies the means is its supreme rule; knows no general rules binding in all circumstances; conscience is not free. Hayek doubts it can be called morals (pp. 161-162). Raison d'état is its most explicit formulation (p. 162). |
| Useful habits (vs. moral virtues) | p. 162 | Qualities a collectivist community cultivates (industry, discipline, obedience) which can never be set above orders, hence not virtues (p. 162). |
| Individualist virtues | p. 163 | Tolerance, respect for others and their opinions, independence of mind, Zivilcourage, consideration for the weak, contempt and dislike of power, kindliness, humor, modesty, respect for privacy; also "eminently social" virtues (p. 163). |
| Commercial vs. military type of society | p. 163 | Reused from ch09 (p. 148), now as the social seedbeds of individualist and collectivist virtues respectively (p. 163). |
