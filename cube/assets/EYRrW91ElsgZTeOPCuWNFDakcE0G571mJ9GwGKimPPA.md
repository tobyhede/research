- **Conceptual analysis and definition** carry most of the chapter: the Rule of Law and formal rules are defined (pp. 109-110), and the incompatibility with planning follows largely from the definitions. The claim that planning "necessarily involves the very opposite" (p. 109) is a deduction from how planning is defined, not an observation.
- **Analogy**: Highway Code versus ordering people where to go; signposts versus commanding which road (p. 110); driving on the left or right (p. 114); rules as "a kind of instrument of production" (p. 109); tossing a coin (p. 111).
- **Illustrative examples of planning decisions**: pigs, buses, coal mines, shoe prices (p. 110); nurses' and doctors' wages against services for the sick, milk for children against farm wages, jobs for the unemployed against wages for the employed (p. 112). Hypothetical, not documented cases.
- **Mechanism argument against "fairness"**: capital and labour in one industry agreeing to restrict output and share the spoils at consumers' expense (p. 112).
- **Historical assertion**: the decline of the Rule of Law in Germany "had been going on steadily" before Hitler, and planning "had already done a great deal of the work which Hitler completed" (p. 113). No instances are cited in the text; the editor points to The Constitution of Liberty ch. 16 (pp. 118-119).
- **Authority**: Dicey's definition (Hayek's note 2, p. 118); Maine's status to contract (p. 113); Kant and Voltaire (p. 115); Locke (Hayek's note 7, p. 119).
- **Quotation from opponents**: Mannheim epigraph conceding that formal law belongs only to the liberal competitive phase (pp. 108-109); Carl Schmitt's "just state" against the Rechtsstaat (Hayek's note 5, p. 119); Wells's Declaration of the Rights of Man, clauses quoted and criticised (pp. 116-117); The Economist leader of 1939 on plenary powers (pp. 117-118); E. B. Ashton on totalitarian criminal law (Hayek's note 9, p. 120).
- **Legal cases**: Mr. Justice Darling on the Board of Agriculture being "no more impeachable than Parliament itself", via Lord Hewart (p. 116); the bill of attainder against Richard Rose, the Bishop of Rochester's cook, under Henry VIII (Hayek's note 9, p. 120).
- **Historical example**: discrimination against national minorities in Central European countries through economic policy (p. 118). No country, measure or source is named.
- **Bare assertion / language outrunning evidence**: "There can be no doubt that planning necessarily involves deliberate discrimination" (p. 113); socialists "(and Nazis) have always protested" against formal justice and "always demanded" socialisation of the law (p. 113); the plenary-powers view "leads straight to the totalitarian state" and is the view "all those who want the government to direct economic life must hold" (p. 118); "has been amply demonstrated" used twice of the unnamed Central European cases (p. 118).

**Editorial corrections and additions (Caldwell)**

- Note 6: the editor "was unable to locate the quotation attributed to Kant"; he supplies only the Voltaire source and its French wording (p. 119). The Kant quotation on p. 115 is therefore unverified.
- Note 9: Hayek gave the Ashton quotation as p. 119; the editor corrects it to p. 127 (p. 120).
- Note 7: the editor glosses Hayek's appeal to Locke by noting Locke described the state of nature as "a state of perfect freedom" before men submit to law to preserve liberty and property (pp. 119-120); a mild qualification of "no liberty without law".
- Note 5: editor identifies Schmitt and glosses the Freirechtsschule as the German term for legal realism (p. 119). Notes 2 and 3: cross-references to The Constitution of Liberty chs. 13 and 16 (pp. 118-119). Note 10: background on Wells's Declaration and its later influence on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (p. 120).
- The Economist quotation carries "[sic]" after "always" (p. 117); it is not signed by the editor, so it appears to be Hayek's own, and he returns to the word on p. 118.
