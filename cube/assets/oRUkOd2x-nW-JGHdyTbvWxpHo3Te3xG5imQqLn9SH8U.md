- Definition of freedom: ch. 1 declined to define it and offered 'tolerance' (p. 65); here it becomes freedom from coercion by other men (p. 74). This negative definition is the one the rest of the book relies on.
- The phrase 'economic freedom' changes owner between chapters: Hayek's own in ch. 1, meaning freedom of economic activity as a condition of political freedom (p. 65); the socialists' here, meaning freedom from want (p. 74). Synthesis should not let the two be run together; ch. 7 returns to the point.
- My observation: Hayek grants that circumstances limit choice unequally (p. 75) but treats that as outside freedom by definition. The argument that the 'new freedom' is merely power or wealth is definitional, not demonstrated. It also sits uneasily with ch. 1, where the liberal era is praised for giving the workingman 'personal independence' through material progress (p. 67), which treats wealth as bearing on freedom.
- The socialism-to-fascism thesis continues the Introduction (p. 58) (p. 61) and anticipates ch. 12. Drucker's stage theory, that fascism follows the failure of communism (p. 76), and Voigt's identity claim (p. 76) are not the same thesis as Hayek's own, naziism as outcome of socialist trends (p. 58).
- The claim that both extremes recruit from 'the same type of mind' (p. 77) anticipates ch. 10.
- 'Democratic socialism is unachievable' (p. 78) is the thesis ch. 5 (Planning and Democracy) must make good.
- The good faith of socialists (p. 75) (pp. 77-78) matches the 'people of good will' of the Introduction (p. 58) and is essential to the book's unintended-consequences structure.
- Ch. 3 opens by separating socialist ends from socialist means (p. 80); this chapter treats 'socialism' as a single thing throughout, so its historical claims apply strictly only to socialism as method.
