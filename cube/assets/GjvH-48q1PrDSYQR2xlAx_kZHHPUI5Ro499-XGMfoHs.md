- **Depends on ch01 and ch02**: α = r × β (ch01) and the growth projections (ch02) are inputs; the 2100 forecast is only as good as ch02's assumption that global growth falls to 1.5 percent.
- **Feeds ch06**: α = r × β with β = s / g gives a rising capital share only if r falls less than proportionally as β rises, i.e. an elasticity of substitution between capital and labour above one. ch06 takes this up. The present chapter is silent on what happens to r as β rises toward 7.
- **Feeds ch10-ch11**: high β in a slow-growth society is the precondition for inheritance's return and for r > g to bite; "quasi-stagnant society" (p. 119) is the bridge.
- **Feeds ch12**: international imbalances from differing s / g (p. 138) and natural-resource wealth (p. 142) are deferred to the chapter on global wealth and oil exporters.
- **Tension on how much is "explained" (my observation)**: the chapter says the 1970–2010 rise in private wealth is explained "largely" by s / g (p. 132) and "without assuming a significant structural increase in the relative price of assets" (p. 127), yet also attributes one-tenth to one-quarter to privatisation (p. 133) and one-quarter to one-third to asset prices (p. 137). The figures are not on a common base: p. 133 concerns private wealth, p. 137 national capital. Read together, saving explains perhaps half to two-thirds of the rise in national β, not "nearly the entirety". Piketty reconciles by calling the price rise a catch-up (p. 134), which makes the long-run claim depend on the 1910–1950 price fall being of the same size, a claim he himself calls approximate (pp. 134-135).
- **Tension on price neutrality (my observation)**: the law needs relative asset prices to be neutral in the long run (pp. 121-122), but the chapter argues that the price of capital is "a social and political construct" (p. 135), set by rent control, governance law and regulation, and ch04 shows German β would rise by one to two years at book value (ch04, p. 104). If institutions durably set prices, the "price effect" is not noise around an s / g equilibrium but a second determinant of β.
- **Tension on classification (my observation)**: whether a rise in share prices is "price" or "volume" depends on counting retained earnings as saving (p. 127). This is defensible, but it means the headline finding (volume dominates) is partly produced by an accounting choice. Piketty is explicit that household saving alone would point to price effects (p. 127).
- **Net vs gross saving (my observation, external debate)**: the law holds net s fixed as g changes. As g goes to zero with fixed net s, β grows without limit, which would require gross saving to absorb nearly all income through depreciation. If instead gross saving is the stable behavioural parameter, β = s / (g + δ) (note 12, p. 447) stays bounded by s / δ. Piketty states both forms but defends neither as the behavioural one. This choice matters most for his slow-growth projection (p. 140), which assumes net s stays at 10 percent while g halves.
- **Link to ch03 on public debt**: the Italian case (pp. 132-133) is the 1970–2010 analogue of Britain 1770–1810 (ch03, p. 93): private wealth rising faster than national wealth because private saving funds public deficits.
- **Link to ch04**: the 1950s trough "lower than the simple logic of accumulation" predicts (p. 134) relies on ch04's decomposition (one-quarter to one-third price effects, ch04 p. 108).
