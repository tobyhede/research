**A. Education and technology explain the long run, not the top (pp. 220-223, 228-229)**

- **Premises**: (p. 222) Wage levels grew with output per head. (pp. 221-222) France's stable hierarchy and the US college premium are consistent with skill supply racing technology. (pp. 228-229) But the post-1980 US rise is a discontinuity between the top 1 percent and "the 9 percent" with no matching discontinuity in measured skills.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 229) Education explains the college premium; it "does not seem" to explain the take-off of the top centile, which is the larger phenomenon.
- **Assessment**: The skills evidence is asserted ("it is hard to see any discontinuity", p. 228) without data in the text or a specific source in the endnotes (note 11 points only to Figures 8.6–8.8, which show incomes, not skills). Superstar-type models predict convex pay from small skill differences, so a pay discontinuity without a skill discontinuity is not, by itself, fatal to market explanations. Piketty does not engage those models here; the cross-country argument (B) does more work.

**B. Cross-country divergence points to institutions (pp. 229-233)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 230-231) Top shares rose two to three times more in the US than in Britain, Canada and Australia, and much more than in continental Europe and Japan. (pp. 232-233) Technology, including IT, and productivity growth were similar across rich countries.
- **Unstated premise**: that the market for top executives is national rather than global, and that firm size, sector mix (finance) and the scale of stock-market capitalisation do not differ enough across these countries to explain the gap.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 229) "institutional differences between countries rather than general and a priori universal causes such as technological change played a central role".
- **Assessment**: This is the strongest argument in the chapter. Its weakness is the unstated premise: Piketty himself grants that supermanager pay "should of course be seen in relation to firm size" (p. 242) but does not test it across countries.

**C. Marginal productivity is undefined at the top, so pay is bargained (pp. 239-241)**

- **Premises**: (p. 239) Top earners are mostly senior managers in near-unique jobs. (pp. 239-240) Under imperfect information, individual marginal product cannot be estimated; experiments would cost more than the salary at stake (note 34). (p. 240) Pay is set by self-interested insiders and peer committees.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 240) Top pay is "largely arbitrary" and reflects hierarchy and bargaining power; (p. 241) its level is bounded by governance rules and social norms.
- **Assessment**: The argument shows that marginal productivity cannot be *verified*, not that pay departs from it. The empirical support comes from (D) and from the cross-country pattern (B). The norm hypothesis is explicitly admitted to move the question rather than answer it (p. 241).

**D. Pay for luck refutes the productivity justification (p. 243)**

- **Premises**: (p. 243) If pay followed marginal productivity, it should vary with the part of firm performance management controls, not with external shocks. (p. 243) In fact pay rises most when performance improves for external reasons (Bertrand and Mullainathan 2001; Bebchuk and Fried 2004, note 35).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 243) This is "the most convincing proof" of failed corporate governance and of the absence of a rational productivity basis for very high pay.
- **Assessment**: The finding is real and well cited, but it supports a governance-failure (rent-extraction) reading, which is a bargaining story, more than a pure social-norms story. Bertrand and Mullainathan's title ("The Ones without Principals Are") ties pay for luck to weak shareholder oversight, a governance variable Piketty folds into "institutions".

**E. Low top tax rates amplify bargaining and feed back politically (pp. 243-244)**

- **Premises**: (p. 243) Pay for luck varies with the top marginal tax rate. (p. 243) After 1980 top rates fell sharply in the English-speaking countries, raising the private return to bargaining for pay. (pp. 243-244) The resulting incomes fund parties, lobbies and think tanks that defend low rates.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 243-244) A second, political force for divergence, alongside self-set pay.
- **Assessment**: Here the chapter only announces the argument; the evidence (cross-country elasticities of top income shares to top rates) is in ch14. Note that this is a bargaining model: high rates reduce the gain from extracting pay, not the gain from working harder.

**F. Household surveys understate inequality (pp. 238-239)**

- **Premises**: (p. 238) In Colombia and Argentina tax data give top-centile shares around 20 percent. (pp. 238-239) The highest incomes in household surveys are only 4–5 times average, implying a top-centile share under 5 percent.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 239) Surveys, often the only source used by the World Bank and governments, are biased and complacent; they must be combined with tax data.
