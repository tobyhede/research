| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tax revenues / compulsory payments | p. 345 | Defined in n. 1: all taxes, fees, social contributions and other payments "that citizens must pay under penalty of law", to every level of government and public agency. "Taxes" is shorthand for all compulsory charges (p. 482). They are expressed as a share of national income (about 90 percent of GDP), so they look about 10 percent larger than shares of GDP (n. 4, pp. 482-483). |
| Regalian functions | p. 345 | The state's core sovereign tasks: "police, courts, army, foreign affairs, general administration". These can be met with 7–8 percent of national income (p. 345) and today cost "a little less than one-tenth" (p. 347). |
| Social state | p. 347 | The state built by the twentieth-century rise in taxes: education, health, replacement incomes and transfers, costing 25–35 percent of national income (pp. 347-348). Note 9: the term captures the state's missions "better than the more restrictive term 'welfare state'" (p. 483). |
| Fiscal state / fiscal and social state | p. 348 | The tax-collecting state whose growth "basically reflects the constitution of a social state" (p. 348). In the poor-country section, "fiscal and social state" means the pair that development requires (p. 359). |
| Transfers in kind | p. 347 | Public spending on education and health, which provides services instead of cash and so reduces household monetary disposable income (p. 347; n. 14, p. 483). |
| Replacement incomes / transfer payments | p. 347 | Replacement incomes are pensions and unemployment compensation. Transfer payments are family allowances, guaranteed income and the like. Both are paid out of taxes back into household disposable income, leaving aggregate disposable income unchanged (p. 347). They cost 10–15 (up to 20) percent of national income, two-thirds to three-quarters of it pensions (p. 347). |
| Replacement rate | p. 348 | A pension as a proportion of the wage earned before retirement. Capped for middle and high earners in "Beveridgian" US and UK systems, almost proportional in "Bismarckian" and similar systems (p. 348; n. 15, pp. 483-484). |
| Modern redistribution | p. 348 | Not explicit transfers from rich to poor but financing "public services and replacement incomes that are more or less equal for everyone" (p. 348), organised by a "logic of rights" and "equal access" to fundamental goods (p. 349). |
| Logic of rights / fundamental social rights | p. 349 | Equal rights of access to goods "deemed to be fundamental": education, health, retirement (pp. 349-350). Open question: how far the list extends (culture, housing, travel?) (p. 349). |
| Common utility | p. 349 | From article 1 of the 1789 Declaration: "social distinctions can be based only on common utility". Piketty reads it as reversing the burden of proof: equality is the norm and inequality needs justification. The drafters meant the abolition of the privileges of the Ancien Régime. Piketty extends it to inequalities "in the interest of all and in particular of the most disadvantaged" (p. 349). Note 20 denies that it means a utilitarian sum of utilities (p. 484). |
| The most disadvantaged | p. 349 | Note 21: those "who have to cope with the most unfavorable factors beyond their control", such as family endowments (inheritance, cultural capital) and luck (special talents) (pp. 484-485). |
| Difference principle | p. 349 | Rawls: inequalities are just only if they bring compensating benefits to everyone, especially the least advantaged. Piketty calls it "similar in intent" to his reading of common utility (p. 349; n. 22, p. 485). |
| Capabilities approach | p. 349 | Sen's approach, "not very different in its basic logic" (p. 349). Note 23 adds Fleurbaey and Roemer (p. 485). |
| Intergenerational correlation (of education and earned income) | p. 353 | A measure of how far the skill hierarchy is reproduced across generations. It is the mobility measure used, and it excludes capital income (p. 353; n. 27, p. 485). Coefficients: 0.2–0.3 in Sweden and Finland, 0.5–0.6 in the US (n. 28, p. 485). |
| Pay-as-you-go (PAYGO) | p. 355 | A pension system in which current workers' contributions are "directly paid out as benefits to retirees" and nothing is invested (pp. 355-356). Its rate of return "is by definition equal to the growth rate of the economy" (p. 356). |
| Capitalized (funded) system | p. 356 | Contributions are invested in assets to pay the contributors' own future pensions (p. 356). |
| Unified retirement scheme based on individual accounts | p. 358 | Piketty's proposed reform: a single PAYGO scheme with equal rights regardless of career path, modelled on the Swedish 1990s reform (n. 46, p. 487). |
| Mixed economy | p. 352 | Public financing combined with production by private, public and intermediate organisations (foundations, associations), and varying forms of ownership (pp. 351-352). |
