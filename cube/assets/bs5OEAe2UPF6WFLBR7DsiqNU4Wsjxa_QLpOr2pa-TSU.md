- **Opens Part Four on ch10–ch12**: the capital tax is introduced as the ideal answer to the "inegalitarian spiral" (p. 342) established in ch12 (pp. 320, 340 there), then deliberately set aside. The chapter's own reforms (education, pensions) do not address capital concentration directly.
- **Feeds ch14**: confiscatory top income rates (p. 344) are promised for ch14. Ch14 opens by describing ch13 as having treated "the overall level of taxes as a given" (p. 360).
- **Feeds ch15 and ch16**: the "utopian" global capital tax and the European fallback (p. 342). Central banks as lender of last resort (pp. 343-344) anticipate ch16.
- **Links to ch12**: Harvard's endowment and elite admissions (n. 29, pp. 485-486). Note 38's "800 US universities" points back to ch12 (p. 487). Note 47 on small savers' low returns (p. 487) recalls ch12 (p. 332 there). ch12's funded-pension scenario for China (p. 338 there) is answered by the case for PAYGO here.
- **Links to ch09**: the education-technology race (pp. 352-353 refer back to Part Three). The upward shift of qualifications without lower wage inequality is ch09's result, reused here to show education did not deliver mobility.
- **Links to ch02**: low growth, about 1–1.5 percent per capita at the frontier, is the premise for both the tax-stabilisation argument (p. 351) and the PAYGO problem (p. 356).
- **Tension: "modern redistribution" against progressive taxation (my observation)**: p. 348 says modern redistribution is not transfers from rich to poor but equal provision. ch14–ch15 make steep progressivity central. The two fit together only if equal provision is financed progressively, which ch13 does not discuss (deferred to ch14).
- **Tension: the burden of proof (my observation)**: Piketty's reading of article 1 puts the burden of proof on inequality (p. 349). In ch12, though, he refuses to judge individual fortunes (in ch12: "Every fortune is partially justified" (p. 323)) and proposes a tax by size, not by justification. The common-utility test therefore works at the level of the tax schedule, not individual fortunes. This is coherent, but it means the principle never decides any particular case.
- **Tension: consensus against democratic contest**: the "partly artificial" consensus on abstract principles (p. 349) and the claim that only democratic deliberation can settle substance (p. 350) sit beside strong policy verdicts throughout Part Four.
- **Number check (my observation)**: "a correlation coefficient two-thirds higher than in Sweden" (p. 353) matches the ranges in note 28 only at their nearest ends (0.3 against 0.5). Taking midpoints (0.25 against 0.55), the US coefficient is more than double Sweden's. The text understates the gap compared with its own note.
- **Number check**: "Recruitment is thus 5 times broader than at Harvard" (p. 354) compares top 10 percent with top 2 percent average-income positions. As note 32 (p. 486) and note 37 (p. 486) say, these are averages, not recruitment ranges, so "5 times broader" is loose.
- **Cross-book link (Hayek, Road to Serfdom ch06)**: Piketty's "logic of rights" to equal access (p. 349) is a rule-based, categorical form of redistribution: universal entitlements, not person-by-person allocation. It bears on the Road to Serfdom ch06 open question of whether general rules can carry a distributive aim. Piketty's universal provision is a test case for Hayek's criterion that policy must not aim directly at distributive justice. Note also Piketty's open line that "taxation is neither good nor bad in itself" (p. 350) against Hayek's focus on the form, not the size, of state action.
