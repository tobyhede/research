| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inequality of wealth (capital ownership) | p. 244 | Distribution of net private wealth among living adults, measured by shares of top decile, top centile, middle 40 percent and bottom 50 percent of total private wealth (shares of private rather than national wealth; "little difference", note 6, p. 465). |
| Patrimonial middle class | p. 244 | The middle 40 percent of the wealth hierarchy, "distinctly wealthier" than the bottom half, owning between a quarter and a third of national wealth; its emergence is "no doubt the most important structural transformation" in the long-run wealth distribution (p. 244; defined again as "the middle 40 percent", p. 251). |
| Probate records / estate and gift tax as source | p. 245 | French *droits de mutation* from 1791: a universal recording fee on all transfers by bequest or gift, covering all assets, all owners, all sizes (p. 245). Proportional and low (1–2 percent) until 1901 (p. 246). The source of the French series; Britain has homogeneous data only from 1919–1920 and the US federal estate tax (1916) covers "a tiny minority" (p. 246). |
| Mortality multiplier | p. 247 (named in note 7, p. 465) | Method that turns wealth at death into wealth of the living by reweighting each decedent by the inverse of the mortality rate of his age cohort (with wealth-differential mortality corrections). Piketty says the living/dead difference is "barely a few points" (p. 247). |
| Patrimonial society | p. 248 | A society "characterized by a hyperconcentration of capital, in which inheritance and marriage played a key role" and in which a large fortune gives comfort unobtainable by work or study; France before and after 1789 (p. 248). |
| Hyperconcentration of wealth | p. 248 | Top decile owning 80–90 percent and top centile 50–60 percent of wealth: the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century European norm and, roughly, that of traditional agrarian societies generally (pp. 248, 250). |
| r > g, "fundamental force for divergence" | p. 254 | The rate of return on capital exceeding the growth rate of output/income; when it holds, past wealth "is recapitalized much more quickly than the economy grows, even when there is no income from labor" (p. 254). Status: "a historical fact, not a logical necessity" (p. 255); "a contingent historical proposition" (p. 258). |
| Inheritance society | p. 254 | A society with "both a very high concentration of wealth and a significant persistence of large fortunes from generation to generation" (p. 254). |
| Pure rate of return (pretax) | p. 256 | Return on capital net of any remuneration for the owner's management labour; set at 4.5 percent as a "minimum value" for early periods in Figure 10.9 (p. 256). Distinguished from after-tax-and-loss return (Figure 10.10, p. 257). |
| Net (after-tax, after-loss) return | p. 257 | Pure return minus taxes on capital and estimated war destruction; fell to 1–1.5 percent in 1913–1950, below g "for the first time in history" (p. 257). |
| Rate of time preference θ | p. 259 | Standard-model parameter of impatience; in a zero-growth economy r = θ (note 17, p. 466). Piketty calls the theory "somewhat tautological" and too "simplistic and systematic" (pp. 259-260). |
| Infinite horizon model | p. 260 | Model in which agents plan for all descendants at their own θ; implies infinite long-run elasticity of saving (note 18, p. 466) and r = θ + γ × g (note 19, pp. 466-467). Rejected as unrealistic but used to show r > g holds in a perfect capital market (p. 260). |
| Primogeniture / entails (*substitution héréditaire*) | p. 262 | Inheritance rules giving all or most property, especially the main landed estate, to the eldest son, often with a duty to preserve it for the next heir (p. 262). |
| Equipartition | p. 262 | Revolutionary/Civil Code rule of equal division among siblings; *quotité disponible* only a quarter for three or more children (p. 262). |
| Equilibrium distribution | p. 261 | Long-run wealth distribution reached for a given structure of shocks; its inequality "is an increasing function of the gap r − g" (pp. 263-264). Above some threshold of r − g there is no equilibrium and inequality grows without limit (p. 265). |
| Pareto distribution / Pareto coefficient | p. 264 | Power-law upper tail produced by multiplicative shocks; the coefficient measuring inequality "is a steeply increasing function of the difference r − g" (p. 264). Valid "only to the upper tail" and "only approximate and locally valid" (p. 266). Inverted coefficients run 1.5–3.5 (note 32, p. 468). |
| Rentier | p. 268 | Holder of capital living on its income; the interwar rentiers who did not cut spending ate into their capital (p. 268). Fuller definition in ch11 (p. 308). |
