| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public debt as a distribution question | p. 400 | Rich countries carry public debt of about one year of national income (90% of GDP) while emerging economies, poorer in income and capital, carry about 30% of GDP; hence debt is "a question of the distribution of wealth, between public and private actors in particular, and not a question of absolute wealth" (p. 400). Restated as "Private wealth rests on public poverty" (p. 423). |
| Net public wealth | p. 400 | Public assets minus public debt; in Europe roughly zero, since assets and debt are each about one year of national income (p. 400). Reused at p. 423 to argue national wealth (public plus private) is as high as it has been in a century. |
| Exceptional (one-time) tax on private capital | p. 401 | A levy on the stock of private wealth used to retire public debt. A flat 15% would repay nearly a year of national income (p. 401); preferred form is progressive, e.g. 0% below €1m, 10% €1–5m, 20% above €5m to raise 20% of GDP once (p. 403), or the ordinary annual 0/1/2% schedule for ten years earmarked for debt reduction (p. 403). Distinct from the permanent annual capital tax of ch15, though Piketty says the two can be combined (p. 403). |
| Debt repudiation / haircut | p. 401 | Complete or partial default, with bondholders forced to accept a cut in the value of bonds (Greece 2011–2012). An exceptional capital tax is "equivalent to a total repudiation" except that its incidence is predictable and it falls on ultimate owners, not banks (pp. 401-402). |
| Financial cadaster | p. 402 | Permanent, automatic reporting to tax authorities of all bank accounts, stocks, bonds and other financial assets held by people in their jurisdiction; "Without such a financial cadaster, every policy choice would be risky" (p. 402). Reused for the Troika's lack of data (p. 412). Carries over ch15's automatic exchange of bank information. |
| Redemption fund | p. 403 | Proposal of the German Council of Economic Experts (2011) to mutualise Eurozone public debt above 60% of GDP and pay the fund down to zero. Piketty: "far from perfect" because it lacks democratic governance, but compatible with a capital tax (p. 403); "a good starting point" lacking a political component (p. 416). Note 27 adds that rollover means all debt would soon be mutualised (p. 504). |
| Nominal vs real asset | p. 403 | A nominal asset's price is fixed in advance and does not depend on inflation (government bonds); a real asset's price moves with the economy, generally at least as fast as inflation (real estate, shares) (p. 403). The distinction drives his analysis of inflation's incidence. |
| Inflation (as redistribution) | p. 403 | Erodes the real value of nominal debt: 5% instead of 2% inflation cuts the real debt/GDP ratio by "more than 15 percent" (p. 403). In its "idealized form" a tax on idle capital (p. 405); in practice "a relatively crude and imprecise tool" (p. 406). |
| Austerity | p. 400 | Debt reduction through primary budget surpluses. "The worst solution in terms of both justice and efficiency" (p. 400). Illustrated by nineteenth-century Britain's century of 2–3% primary surpluses (p. 404). |
| Lender of last resort | p. 407 | The consensus primary function of central banks since the 1930s: in a panic, create the liquidity necessary to prevent collapse of the financial system (p. 407). |
| Central banks redistribute, not create | p. 408 | Central bank operations are always loans, creating equal financial assets and liabilities, so national capital is unchanged at the moment of creation (p. 408); later effects on wealth depend on the real economy and can go either way (pp. 408-409). Note 20: there is no "money printing press" in the sense of a gift (p. 503). |
| Unconventional monetary policy | p. 404 | Post-2008 lengthening of loans to banks (three to six months, then nine to twelve), lending to non-financial firms, and outright purchase of long-dated and sovereign bonds (p. 409). No consensus on its proper form (p. 408). |
| Stateless currency / currency without a state | p. 411 | The euro: a currency created on a vast scale without a state, "a central bank without a government" (p. 414), product of the late-1980s belief that central banking's only job was controlling inflation (p. 414). |
| Troika | p. 411 | European Commission, ECB and IMF, working since 2009–2010 on Europe's combined public-debt and banking crisis (p. 411). |
| Eurozone budgetary parliament | p. 416 | A new chamber drawn from national parliaments (note 28: about fifty members per large country, prorated by population, p. 504) to decide by majority vote the level of pooled Eurozone debt and, with a European finance minister, the budget and deficit (pp. 416-417). Preferred over the existing European Parliament, which includes non-Eurozone states and would clash with national sovereignty (p. 416). |
| Residence of the capital asset | p. 418 | Taxing wealth by the location of the asset (as property tax already does for real estate) rather than the owner's residence, extended to financial assets via the location of the business activity or company (p. 418). Requires automatic bank-data sharing. |
| Golden rule of capital accumulation | p. 419 | Phelps (1961): the equality r = g, attained when so much capital has accumulated that the return on capital, taken as equal to its marginal productivity, falls to the growth rate (pp. 419-420). Then α = s: all capital income must be reinvested to hold β constant (p. 420). Piketty treats it as an upper limit on desirable accumulation, not a target (p. 421; note 42, p. 507). |
| Capital saturation | p. 420 | Strategy of accumulating so much capital that r falls to g, leaving rentiers nothing to consume if they want their wealth to keep pace; one way, but not the best, to "end the reign of the rentier" (pp. 420-421). Linked to Keynes's "euthanasia of the rentier" (note 44, p. 507). |
| Golden rule (European fiscal sense) | p. 422 | A "rather different idea" (p. 422): the Maastricht limits of 3% of GDP for deficits and 60% for debt, now proposed for constitutional entrenchment. Note 46 gives the generic sense: a simple rule fixing obligations to future generations (p. 508). |
| Discount rate (Stern/Nordhaus) | p. 424 | The rate at which future environmental damage is weighed against present cost: Stern ≈ growth rate (1–1.5%), Nordhaus ≈ average return on capital (4–4.5%) (p. 424). |
| Natural capital | p. 424 | Humanity's environmental endowment, whose degradation by climate change is "the world's principal long-term worry" (p. 424). Note 52: its substitutability for other wealth "is far from infinite" (p. 509). |
| Educational capital | p. 425 | Introduced as a counterweight to the debt obsession: "The more urgent need is to increase our educational capital and prevent the degradation of our natural capital" (p. 425). Ties to the repeated comparison of debt interest with university spending (pp. 405, 423). |
| Democratic control of capital / economic democracy | p. 425 | New forms of property and governance between purely private (joint-stock) and purely public (top-down state) capital; "the market and the ballot box are merely two polar extremes" (p. 425). Depends on corporate accounting transparency and on a real right to intervene, including worker board seats (p. 426). |
| Economic and financial transparency | p. 425 | Publication of detailed accounts of private corporations and government agencies, not of individual incomes, which are "of no intrinsic interest" except for officials or where trust cannot otherwise be built (pp. 425-426). |
