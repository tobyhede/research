- Do endowment returns generalise to family fortunes of 10–100 million euros, the group that holds about 20 percent of global wealth and drives the projection (p. 319)? Piketty himself doubts it (p. 329). Check whether ch15 relies on the endowment gradient for its rate schedule.
- Does appraisal-based valuation of alternative assets make the "no greater volatility" claim (p. 328) unreliable? This would weaken the inference that the return gap is not a risk premium.
- The 6.4 percent figure (p. 316) uses trough-to-peak endpoints. The 4 percent figure for 1990–2010 runs peak-to-trough. Is "at least twice" average wealth growth (p. 317) robust across all windows, or only across these two? Supplemental Table S12.1 is not in this file.
- How is 60–70 percent inherited (p. 323) derived beyond the a priori judgment? Can it be reconciled with ch11's method, given note 19 (p. 478)?
- Is the "Earth owned by Mars" anomaly fully accounted for by Zucman's tax-haven wealth, and does the conclusion that rich countries are net creditors (p. 342) depend on the three-quarters ownership share, which is asserted from "all the evidence" (p. 341)?
- Under what conditions does Piketty think a 60 percent top-thousandth share would provoke "a violent political reaction" (p. 319)? Compare with ch17 and ch16 on the politics of capital and with the war-shock account in ch10.
- The oil-fund projection (10–20 percent of global capital by 2030–2040, p. 336) depends on unnamed forecasts of $200 oil and on a reinvestment fraction "considerably larger than it is today" (p. 336). How load-bearing is this for later chapters?
