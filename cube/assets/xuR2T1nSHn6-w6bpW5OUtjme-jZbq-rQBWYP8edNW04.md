**A. The restated core: r > g drives divergence (pp. 426-427)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 426-427) r can exceed g for long periods. (p. 427) When r > g, past wealth grows faster than output and wages. (p. 427) Returns rise with the size of the fortune. (p. 427) Frontier growth will not exceed 1–1.5%; returns are 4–5%.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 427) r > g will "again become the norm in the twenty-first century", with potentially terrifying consequences for wealth concentration.
- **Unstated premises**: that r is roughly insensitive to the accumulation of capital at high β (ch06's elasticity of substitution above one, which the Conclusion does not restate); that the savings out of capital income are high enough for r > g to translate into rising concentration (ch10's model). The Conclusion's note 1 concedes a further one: a higher g might raise r, so the gap need not shrink (p. 510).
- **Force of "contradiction"**: the Conclusion calls r > g "a fundamental logical contradiction" (p. 427), but ch10 explicitly said it was "a historical fact, not a logical necessity" (p. 255). What is logical is the implication from r > g to faster growth of past wealth; whether r > g holds is empirical. The rhetoric of "contradiction" (Marxian in echo) outruns the more careful earlier formulation.

**B. Why a progressive annual capital tax rather than heavy capital-income taxation (p. 427)**

- **Premises**: (p. 427) Indiscriminate heavy taxation of capital income could push r below g but would risk killing accumulation and lowering g. (p. 427) A progressive tax on the stock targets the largest fortunes and spares new accumulators. (p. 428) Very large fortunes have little to do with entrepreneurship and do not promote growth.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 427) The progressive annual capital tax avoids "an endless inegalitarian spiral" while preserving incentives.
- **Assessment**: the claim that huge fortunes are useless for growth (p. 428) is stated as what "Historical experience shows"; the supporting evidence is in ch11 and ch14 (e.g., the absence of a growth penalty from high top rates), not in the Conclusion.

**C. The capital tax requires European democracy (p. 428)**

- **Premises**: (p. 428) The tax needs international cooperation beyond the nation-state. (p. 428) Competition, however perfect, cannot remove r > g. (p. 428) Small European nations retreating to national sovereignty will be powerless against the global economy.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 428) Bet everything on democracy at European scale.
- **Concession**: integration might erode existing social states and build only "a vast market predicated on ever purer and more perfect competition"; "the risk is real" (p. 428).

**D. Economics as political economy (pp. 428-430)**

- **Premises**: (p. 428) What distinguishes economics among the social sciences is its normative purpose. (p. 429) Social scientists cannot avoid taking stands and should not hide behind abstract principles or demolition of others' data. (p. 429) Mathematical models often mask empty content; experimental methods prove causal relations of limited interest and neglect history. (pp. 429-430) History, especially the twentieth century, cannot be replayed and is the principal source of knowledge.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 430) Economists should be methodologically pragmatic, use whatever tools are available, and work with other disciplines.
- **Assessment**: the attack on formal modelling sits awkwardly with the book's own reliance on β = s/g, α = r × β and r > g as organising devices; Piketty's position is better read as a demand that models be subordinate to specified facts and problems ("without a clear specification of the economic facts one is trying to explain", p. 429) than as a rejection of models.

**E. Why serial history died and why it should return (pp. 430-431)**

- **Premises**: (p. 431) Data processing was laborious, leaving little room for interpretation. (p. 430) Furet's turn against Marxist historians led him away from economic and social history. (p. 431) The programme stopped before the twentieth century, where politics visibly shapes the series.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 431) A unified economic, political, social and cultural approach is now possible and indispensable; the old infrastructure/superstructure dichotomy must go.

**Position versus argument.** The Conclusion argues for nothing new; it re-ranks the book's claims. It hardens the descriptive claim (r > g as a "contradiction") while softening the epistemic status of all conclusions ("tenuous", p. 426). The policy claim (progressive capital tax) is stated as the one right solution, while the means (European democracy) are presented as a wager.
