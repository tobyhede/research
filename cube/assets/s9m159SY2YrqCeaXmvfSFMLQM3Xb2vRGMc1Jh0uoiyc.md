**France**

- The top decile's share of national income fell from 45–50 percent on the eve of World War I to 30–35 percent today, a 15-point drop (p. 196).
- That fall is "due entirely" to lower top incomes from capital; the top decile's share of wages has stayed around 25 percent, and the bottom half's around 25–30 percent of wages, with no long-run trend (p. 197).
- The top centile's share fell from over 20 percent of national income in 1900–1910 to 8–9 percent in 2000–2010 (barely 7 percent in the early 1980s); its share of *wages* stayed at about 6–7 percent throughout (pp. 197-198).
- No general structural compression of wage inequality occurred, against Kuznets's prediction; this is "no doubt the most important lesson the twentieth century has to teach" (p. 198).
- The compression is concentrated in 1914–1945 and was caused by war, depression and the policies they brought (rent control, nationalisations, inflation that ruined holders of government debt), which cut the capital/income ratio and capital's share (pp. 198-199).
- Because capital is more concentrated than labor, shocks to capital fall hardest on the top decile and centile (p. 199).
- Estate-tax data since 1791 show nothing presaging a spontaneous fall in wealth concentration before 1914 (p. 199).
- In 1932 capital income was the main source of income for the top 0.5 percent; in 2005 it dominates only in the top 0.1 percent. The capital-dominated group was 5 times larger in 1932 and 10 times larger in the Belle Époque (pp. 199-200).
- So the top centile today is barely more unequal in income than in wages, but the change came without any widening of the wage hierarchy: rentiers fell; managers did not rise (p. 200).
- Why rentiers have not come back, given that the capital/income ratio has returned to Belle Époque levels, is "the more complex" and more important question; progressive income and inheritance taxes are one answer, but not the only one (pp. 200-201).
- At the top, the rise of capital income's share is due to financial assets, mainly dividends; rent stays around 10 percent of income and even falls within the top centile (p. 203).
- The 1914–1945 fall in the top decile share (about 18 points) is three-quarters due to the top centile (about 14 points) (p. 206).
- Short- and medium-run movements are real and politically decisive: wage compression in both world wars and post-war re-widening; rising inequality 1945–1967; compression 1968–1983 driven by minimum-wage increases; re-widening after the 1982–1983 austerity turn (pp. 208-210).
- Since the late 1990s top French salaries have risen sharply: the top centile's wage share from under 6 percent to 7.5–8 percent by the early 2010s; gains of over 50 percent in ten years at the top 0.1 and 0.01 percent (p. 211).
- The real long-run issue in France is not the top decile's income share but the renewed importance of inherited wealth, which needs direct study (p. 211).

**United States**

- The US was less unequal than Europe in 1900–1910 (top decile a little over 40 percent versus 45–50 percent in France) because its capital/income ratio and capital share were lower and wealth was somewhat less concentrated (p. 212).
- Inequality rose in the 1920s, with the top decile above 50 percent in 1928–1929, and capital gains already mattered (p. 213).
- The 1914–1945 compression was smaller than in Europe: "Europe in 1914–1945 witnessed the suicide of rentier society, but nothing of the sort occurred in the United States" (p. 213).
- 1950–1980 was the US low point (top decile 30–35 percent) and, for white citizens, the US was then more equal than France (pp. 213-214).
- Since 1980 the top decile share rose from 30–35 percent to 45–50 percent, about 15 points (p. 214); with a correction for under-declared capital income it slightly exceeded 50 percent in 2007 and the early 2010s (p. 214).
- Capital gains add volatility but little to the structural trend: excluding them, the top decile still rose from about 32 to over 46 percent; the structural contribution of capital gains is about one point (pp. 214-215).
- The 2008 crisis did not halt the trend; 2010 exceeded 2007 excluding capital gains (p. 215).
- About 11 of the 15 points went to the top 1 percent, roughly half of that to the top 0.1 percent (pp. 215-216).
- Rising inequality contributed to financial instability, through stagnant lower and middle incomes and household borrowing, but it was not the sole or primary cause of 2008; a rising capital/income ratio and huge cross-border asset positions may matter more (pp. 216-217).
- The US rise is "largely" a rise in wage inequality at the top. The top decile's wage share rose from 25 to 35 percent, about two-thirds of the rise in its share of total income (pp. 217-218).
- Rising wage inequality was not offset by career mobility: measured over 10, 20 or 30 years, the increase is the same (p. 218).
- Capital income still accounts for about a third of the rise in US income inequality; the top centile shows "cohabitation", not replacement (pp. 218-219).
- In 1929 capital income dominated for the top 1 percent; in 2007 only for the top 0.1 percent, and without capital gains only at the top 0.01 percent (p. 219).
- 60–70 percent of the top 0.1 percent in 2000–2010 are top managers; athletes, actors and artists under 5 percent; finance about 20 percent of the top 0.1 percent against under 10 percent of GDP (pp. 219-220).
