| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| National planning (planning on a national scale) | p. 221 | Economic planning by each state independently; practicable only if the authority can "shut out all extraneous influences", so it piles up restrictions on movement of men and goods and makes international economic relations relations between "whole nations organized as trading bodies" (p. 221). |
| Economic solidarity (national) | p. 221 | The "artificially fostered" common economic interest of all inhabitants of a country, a product of national planning and a danger to peace (p. 221). |
| "Struggle" of competition | p. 221 | Only metaphorically a struggle; contrasted with the "contest of force" between states that replaces it (p. 221). |
| International planning | p. 222 | Conscious direction of economic affairs by a "supernational authority" over many peoples (p. 222). Hayek holds it "cannot be anything but a naked rule of force" (p. 223) and is practicable only when "in effect done by one single predominant power" (p. 228). |
| Planning (two senses in this chapter) | pp. 222, 230 | (i) the book's standard sense: central direction of economic life according to a unitary plan with a fixed order of priorities (pp. 222, 225); (ii) a looser sense in the federation passage, where federation leaves "the way free for all desirable planning", meaning planning that can be done locally, without restrictive measures, and where "true agreement can be reached" among all affected (p. 230). The shift is unmarked; sense (ii) is close to ch. 3's "planning for competition" and to ordinary local public action. |
| Distributive justice (between peoples) | p. 222 | Agreed ideals for ranking the claims of different nations; Hayek denies that any exist ("the moral bases for such an undertaking are completely lacking") (p. 222). |
| Grossraumwirtschaft | p. 223 | Large-area economy of the German kind; realizable "only by a master-race, a Herrenvolk" (p. 223). Links to Carr in ch. 13 (p. 195). |
| International (economic) authority | p. 226 | Used in two opposed senses. Rejected: an authority that "doles out raw materials and allocates markets" or has "specific economic powers", including autonomous commodity or transport controls "recognized by all the national governments, but subject to none" (pp. 226-227). Accepted: an authority that "merely keeps order and creates conditions in which the people can develop their own life" (p. 226). |
| International political authority / supernational authority | p. 229 | The accepted form: "a superior political power which can hold the economic interests in check", "itself not mixed up in the economic game"; has no power to direct peoples what to do but can restrain them from damaging others (p. 229). Must be "very powerful" for enforcing the common law, yet constitutionally prevented from tyranny (p. 231). |
| Negative powers | p. 229 | Powers to forbid: "it must, above all, be able to say 'No' to all sorts of restrictive measures" (p. 229). Their content is "essentially the powers of the ultra-liberal 'laissez faire' state" (p. 229). |
| Sovereignty | p. 228 | Two kinds separated: economic sovereignty, which must not be "unfettered" for any state, large or small (p. 228); and political sovereignty, of which a "clearly defined part" is to be renounced to the federation (p. 228). Hayek reverses the common view: not an international economic authority with unrestricted political sovereignty for states, but "almost exactly the opposite" (p. 229). |
| Rule of Law (international) | p. 227 | First as "a simple Rule of Law" that realists say an international government cannot enforce (p. 227); then as the constraint that must "strictly" circumscribe the international authority's powers (p. 229); finally as a double safeguard, against "the tyranny of the state over the individual" and against "the tyranny of the new superstate over the national communities" (p. 231). Same meaning as ch. 6 (formal rules known beforehand, "rules of the game", p. 228), now applied to states as subjects and extended to protect individuals against their own states. |
| Federation / federalism | p. 229 | The form of international government in which "certain strictly defined powers are transferred to an international authority" while countries remain responsible for internal affairs (p. 229). Called "nothing but the application to international affairs of democracy", but "a democracy with definitely limited powers" (p. 229). Valued as a division of power that limits both the whole and the member state (p. 230). In the epigraph, Acton's point is that it curbs "the power of the whole people" (p. 220). |
| International law | p. 230 | Until backed by an enforcing power, "a pious wish"; "there can be no international law without a power to enforce it" (p. 230). |
| Restrictionism / restrictive measures | p. 229 | Trade barriers, market-sharing, raw-material controls and similar state measures that damage other peoples; the chief thing the federal authority must prevent (pp. 229, 230). |
| Community of nations of free men | p. 232 | The stated goal, contrasted with both "an omnipotent superstate" and "a loose association of 'free nations'" (p. 232). The unit of freedom is the individual, not the nation. |
| Small states | p. 228 | Bearers of "beauty and decency"; their independence is preserved only "within a true system of law" (p. 231). "A world fit for small states to live in" is an aim (p. 231). |
