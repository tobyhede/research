**A. Planning cannot be done by formal rules (pp. 109-110)**

- **Premises**: (p. 109) Formal rules are set in advance for long periods and do not aim at particular people's needs. (pp. 109-110) A planning authority must meet actual needs as they arise and choose between them. (p. 110) Decisions on quantities, prices and which plants operate depend on the circumstances of the moment and require balancing the interests of persons and groups.
- **Unstated premise**: that there is no intermediate form, such as general rules that set planning targets or criteria in advance and are applied by formula. He treats every allocation decision as a fresh act of discretion.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 110) Someone's view of whose interests matter more becomes "part of the law of the land", a new rank order imposed by coercion.

**B. The economic (knowledge) argument for general rules (p. 111)**

- **Premises**: Only the individuals concerned know the circumstances of time and place. To use that knowledge in plans they must be able to predict state action. Predictable state action must follow rules fixed independently of concrete circumstances, so its particular effects are unpredictable. State action aimed at particular ends must respond to the full circumstances of the moment and is therefore itself unpredictable.
- **Unstated premise**: that predictability of state action and predictability of its effects on particular people cannot both be had; and that the use of dispersed individual knowledge is the dominant economic consideration (the full argument is "only briefly" stated here, p. 111).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 111) The state should confine itself to rules for general types of situation.

**C. The moral (impartiality) argument (pp. 111-112)**

- **Premises**: (p. 111) If the state can foresee exactly who is affected and how, it leaves those affected no choice and is itself choosing between ends. (p. 111) General rules operate in unforeseeable circumstances, so their effect on particular people is unknown. (p. 111) Impartiality just is having no answer to such questions. (pp. 111-112) Where effects on particular people are known and aimed at, government "must, of necessity, take sides".
- **Unstated premises**: that legislators really are ignorant of who will gain from general rules (as opposed to being able to predict, statistically, which classes gain); that knowing an effect and intending it are morally equivalent for a legislator; that impartiality in this sense is the relevant standard of justice.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 112) Law whose particular effects are foreseen becomes "an instrument used by the lawgiver upon the people and for his ends".

**D. Formal versus substantive equality (p. 113)**

- **Premises**: The Rule of Law is the absence of legal privileges for designated people. To give different people the same result they must be treated differently. A law that prescribes how well off particular people shall be is a rule of status.
- **Unstated premise**: that general rules cannot themselves aim at a distributive pattern. A rule defined in general terms (for instance a tax schedule or a benefit entitlement by category) is not considered here as a possible vehicle; "deliberately aiming" and "aiming directly" carry the weight.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 113) Formal equality before the law is "in conflict, and in fact incompatible" with government activity deliberately aiming at material equality; pursuit of distributive justice destroys the Rule of Law.

**E. Legality is not the Rule of Law (pp. 115-116)**

- **Premises**: (p. 115) Full legal authority to act says nothing about whether the law prescribes how to act or leaves it to discretion. (p. 115) Hitler may have gained unlimited powers constitutionally. (p. 116) If the law says a board may do as it pleases, its acts are legal but not under the Rule of Law.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 116) The Rule of Law is a limit on legislation, and a particular enactment can infringe it; (p. 116) such limits amount to recognising inviolable individual rights, whether in a bill of rights or by tradition.
- **Unstated premise**: that some standard above the legislature exists and can be made effective; he says the form "matters comparatively little" (p. 116) but gives no account of enforcement.

**F. Rights and planning cannot be combined (pp. 116-118)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 116-117) Individual rights obstruct planning; Wells therefore hedges each right with "common welfare" qualifications that empty it. (p. 117) Freedom of movement and of the press cannot be secured when the authority controls transport, currency, location of industry, paper and distribution. (p. 118) Central European states discriminated against national minorities through ordinary economic policy without breaching statutory minority protections.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 118) Formal recognition of rights "loses all significance" under complete control of economic life.

**Position versus argument.** He holds that the liberal state should not pursue distributive justice (p. 113). The reason given here is formal and legal, that doing so requires treating persons differently by authority, and not that existing distributions are just; he concedes outright that the Rule of Law "produces economic inequality" (p. 113).
