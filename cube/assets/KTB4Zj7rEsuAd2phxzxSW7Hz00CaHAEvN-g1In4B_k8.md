- How was the 1913–1950 decomposition into volume and price effects done (p. 108)? The shares depend on which price index deflates asset prices; ch05's caveat that price comparisons over long periods are "approximate at best" (ch05, p. 135) applies.
- Is the German "paradox" (p. 104) about measurement, or does it show that market-valued β is not a measure of productive capital at all? If governance rules can move β by one to two years of income, cross-country β comparisons in ch05 inherit that noise.
- The land argument (pp. 108-109) assumes an inelastic demand for land. Is there evidence on American land prices beyond probate records? Was the low price also because land titles were insecure or land was taken from Native Americans at no market price? The chapter does not discuss dispossession.
- The slave-capital estimate rests on Jones, Goldsmith and Fogel (p. 114). Fogel and Engerman's work is contested on productivity; does the estimate depend on those contested parts?
- Piketty uses r = 5 percent and α = 40 percent in the slave thought experiment but gives 7–8 percent for actual slave returns (pp. 116-117). Is the higher return a risk premium (slaves could flee, die), consistent with his later account of returns rising with risk and fortune size (ch12)?
- The rejection of human capital (p. 117) is aimed at capitalised labour income as a stock. Does it touch the human-capital theory of earnings (education raises productivity), which ch09 discusses? The two uses should be kept apart in synthesis.
