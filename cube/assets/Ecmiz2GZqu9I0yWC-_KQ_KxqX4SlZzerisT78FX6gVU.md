| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Global tax on capital | p. 378 | "a progressive global tax on capital, coupled with a very high level of international financial transparency" (p. 378); specified as "a progressive annual tax on individual wealth" on "the net value of assets each person controls" (p. 379). Global in two senses: worldwide coordination, and covering all asset types "no exceptions" (p. 380). |
| Useful utopia | p. 378 | An ideal unlikely to be enacted soon but serving as "a worthwhile reference point, a standard against which alternative proposals can be measured", approachable incrementally (p. 378). |
| Taxable wealth (net worth) | pp. 379-380 | Market value of all financial assets (deposits, stocks, bonds, partnerships, stakes in listed and unlisted firms) and nonfinancial assets (especially real estate), "net of debt" (pp. 379-380). For the very rich, the kind of figure Forbes publishes (p. 379). |
| Financial transparency / democratic transparency | p. 381 | "clarity about who owns what assets around the world" (p. 381); the precondition for both the tax and rational democratic debate and financial regulation (pp. 381-382). |
| Compulsory reporting law | p. 382 | A very low (0.1%) capital tax whose real function is to make everyone register ownership to be recognised as legal owner; a "cadastral financial survey of the entire world" (p. 382). |
| Automatic transmission (sharing) of banking information | p. 383 | Banks report holdings to tax authorities automatically, including foreign holdings, so authorities can issue precomputed asset statements (pp. 382-383); contrasted with information "only on formal request" (p. 384). |
| Precomputed asset statement | p. 383 | A form sent by the tax authority listing a taxpayer's reported assets and liabilities, to be corrected if needed, modelled on W-2/1099 reporting and US property tax assessment (pp. 382-383). |
| Contributive justification | p. 386 | Capital measures the contributive capacity of the very wealthy better than income, which for them is "often not a well-defined concept" (p. 386); "the most important justification" (p. 388). |
| Incentive justification | p. 386 | A tax on the stock pushes owners to seek higher returns or sell to more dynamic investors (p. 388); valid but "should not be overstated" (p. 388). |
| Insurance logic | p. 388 | The case for taxing the flow of capital income rather than the stock, because returns are volatile and a stock tax hits loss-making firms (p. 388). The ideal system compromises between incentive and insurance logics (p. 388). |
| Economic income | p. 386 | All new resources (dividends, capital gains, etc.) that could finance consumption or increase the capital stock (p. 386); Piketty's note 12 gives Hicks's definition: the maximum that could be consumed while remaining as wealthy at the end of the period as at the beginning (p. 496). Contrasted with declared (taxable) income. |
| Exceptional (one-time) capital levy | p. 389 | A tax on capital at high rates (e.g., France 1945, up to 25%, 100% on gains during the Occupation) that cannot be repeated annually (p. 389); developed in ch16. |
| Capital controls | p. 379 | Government restrictions on inflows and outflows of capital; first named as an "unsatisfactory" nationalist substitute (p. 379), discussed at length with China as the leading case (pp. 395-396). |
| Liberal form of capital control | p. 397 | Piketty's description of the capital tax: regulation through a general tax rather than administrative permission (p. 397). |
| Petroleum rents | p. 397 | Income from natural resources whose distribution depends on arbitrary borders; an ideal capital tax in a global democracy would redistribute it (p. 397). |
| Redistribution through immigration | p. 398 | Moving labour to where wages are higher, as an alternative to moving capital (p. 398). |
