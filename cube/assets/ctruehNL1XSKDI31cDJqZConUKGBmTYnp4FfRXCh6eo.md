- **From ch06**: ch06 ends by asking whether the "deep structures of capital" have changed at the level of individual distribution (p. 170). This chapter's answer in outline: the macro share of capital barely changed, but its distribution did (the patrimonial middle class), though less than believed (pp. 188-189).
- **Capital gains (my observation)**: capital income here includes capital gains (p. 175), while ch06's average return excludes them (ch06 p. 151). The chapters use two definitions of capital income; for the r > g argument (ch10) it matters which one r means.
- **Differential returns**: the multiplier from higher returns on large fortunes (p. 175) repeats ch06 (pp. 143-144, 147) and is deferred to ch12. It is the bridge between the macro r of ch06 and the concentration mechanism of ch10.
- **Inheritance**: the claim that inheritance explains wealth concentration (p. 177) is asserted here and argued in ch10–ch11; the Modigliani vs Kaldor contrast was flagged in ch06 endnote 36 (p. 454). Vautrin's figures are revisited in ch11 (endnote 2, p. 454).
- **Feeds ch08–ch09**: the supermanager society (p. 191) is the subject of ch08's US story and ch09's account of executive pay; the "highly political chronology" of French profit shares in ch06 (p. 164) and the policy-driven compression announced here (p. 170) set up ch08.
- **Feeds Part Four**: the "ideal society" wealth distribution (p. 185) and the redistribution question deferred to Part Four (p. 183) lead to ch13–ch15. The call to break national accounts into decile groups (p. 195) anticipates ch15's demand for financial transparency.
- **Justification and Hayek (cross-book)**: the "apparatus of justification" (p. 190) and the claim that democratic modernity accepts talent-and-effort inequality (p. 174) can be set against *The Road to Serfdom* ch06, where Hayek concedes that the Rule of Law produces economic inequality and defends it because that inequality is not designed to affect particular people (see the road-to-serfdom deep-read note ch06-planning-and-the-rule-of-law.md). Piketty's argument is that tolerance of inequality depends on belief in its justification; Hayek's that impersonal inequality is easier to bear. Both treat legitimacy as decisive, from opposite directions.
- **Measurement is political**: "never neutral" (p. 196) and the "chaste veil" of official statistics (p. 194) extend ch06's account of the politicized capital-share debate (ch06 pp. 158-159). By the same standard Piketty's own choices (deciles, adults, individual vs household, pensions as wages) are not neutral either; endnotes 5, 9 and 24 (pp. 454-456) show he knows they matter.
- **Internal inconsistency (minor)**: the middle 40 percent's wealth share is 35 percent in the European example (p. 187), "scarcely more than a third" of Europe's wealth (p. 188) and "one-quarter to one-third" collectively (p. 189). The range covers Europe and the US, but the three phrasings are not reconciled.
- **Decomposition is verbal (my observation)**: the "three terms" decomposition (p. 171) is not a formal decomposition (e.g. of a variance into two variances and a covariance). Shares of total income cannot be additively split into a labor part and a capital part plus interaction without a stated method, so the decomposition is a heuristic, not a measurement.
