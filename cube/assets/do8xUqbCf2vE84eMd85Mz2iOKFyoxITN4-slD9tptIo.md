**Slow growth makes capital large.** The second fundamental law, β = s / g, says the long-run capital/income ratio equals the saving rate divided by the growth rate. As growth falls, especially population growth, β rises back toward nineteenth-century levels. [Ch 5, line 5110](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5110) [Ch 5, line 5129](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5129)

---

- **Author's claim:** in the long run β = s / g. Saving 12% with 2% growth gives capital worth six years of income. At 1.5% growth it is eight years. [Ch 5, line 5110](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5110) [Ch 5, line 5137](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5137)
- **Reasoning:** g counts population growth as well as growth per head. Europe and the US had similar growth per head, but Europe's slower population growth gives it the higher ratio. [Ch 5, line 5153](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5153)
- **Evidence:** in Britain and France capital stood at 6–7 years of income through the nineteenth century, fell to 2–3 years by 1950 and was back at 5–6 by 2010. [Ch 3, line 3637](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L3637) From 1970 to 2010 private wealth in the eight richest economies rose from 2–3.5 to 4–7 years of income. Saving flows predict most of that rise, and Japan's almost exactly. [Ch 5, line 5332](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5332) [Ch 5, line 5402](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5402) [Ch 5, line 5418](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5418)
- **Boundary:** the law takes decades, applies only to capital that can be accumulated, and assumes asset prices track consumer prices on average. [Ch 5, line 5181](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5181) [Ch 5, line 5214](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5214) Rising asset prices explain a quarter to a third of the 1970–2010 rise, and privatization a further tenth to a quarter. [Ch 5, line 5852](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5852) [Ch 5, line 5640](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5640) **[Analysis]** The projection of a world ratio near 700% by 2100 assumes saving holds at 10% while growth halves. If saving falls with growth, β rises less. [Ch 5, line 5981](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5981)
- **Application [Application]:** treat β = s / g as a sanity check on claims about wealth. Low growth and steady saving mean a large stock of wealth relative to income.

> “in a quasi-stagnant society, wealth accumulated in the past will inevitably acquire disproportionate importance.” [Ch 5, line 5129](https://github.com/tobyhede/research/blob/main/books/capital-in-the-twenty-first-century/source/source.txt#L5129)
