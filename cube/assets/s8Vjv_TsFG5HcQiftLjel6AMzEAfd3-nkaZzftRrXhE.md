| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inequality of returns on capital (unequal returns) | p. 313 | The hypothesis, then the claim, that wealthier owners get a higher average return than less wealthy ones. Two causes: economies of scale in portfolio management (the main one in practice) and a greater capacity to take risks and be patient (p. 314). Illustration: if the average is 4 percent, the wealthy may get 6–7 and the less wealthy 2–3 (p. 314). It "amplifies and aggravates" r > g, and r − g can be high for large fortunes even when it is not high for the economy as a whole (p. 314). |
| Economies of scale in portfolio management | pp. 313-314 | Larger portfolios can pay for better advisers at a smaller percentage cost. Harvard spends about $100 million a year, "just over 0.3 percent" of a $30 billion endowment (p. 328). This is the explanation offered for the gradient in endowment returns (pp. 328-329). |
| "Natural" countervailing force | p. 314 | A force that involves no government intervention. The only one is growth: fast global growth keeps the relative rise of the largest fortunes moderate (p. 314). |
| Fixed-fractile measure (top 1/20 million, top 1/100 million) | p. 316 | Piketty's way of reading the Forbes list: follow the wealth of a fixed share of the world's adults (about 150 people in 1987, 225 in 2013) instead of counting billionaires in nominal dollars (p. 316). |
| Global wealth reports | p. 318 | Annual reports by banks and insurers (Crédit Suisse since 2010, Merrill Lynch, Allianz) that try to estimate the world wealth distribution. They "rely on heroic hypotheses" and are "useless" for long-run trends, but they discipline public debate (p. 318). |
| HNWI | p. 318 | "High net worth individuals", the millionaires studied by financial institutions (p. 318). |
| Pure heirs / partial heirs / pure entrepreneurs | pp. 322-323 | Forbes's three groups of billionaires, each about a third of the total. Forbes never defines the boundary between "pure" and "partial" or says how much was inherited (pp. 322-323). |
| Moral hierarchy of wealth | p. 323 | The ranking of fortunes as deserved or undeserved (Gates against Slim, Western against non-Western billionaires). Piketty calls the debate "sterile" (p. 323) and "in practice often" Western ethnocentrism (pp. 324-325), and replaces it with uniform taxation (p. 325). |
| Pure return on capital | p. 327 | The return "that comes simply from owning capital, apart from any remuneration of the labor required to manage it" (p. 327). Endowment returns net of fees, taxes and inflation measure it (p. 327). |
| Alternative investments / alternative investment strategies | pp. 327-328 | Private equity, unlisted foreign stocks, hedge funds, derivatives, real estate and raw materials. Their "only common feature" is that they abandon stocks and bonds open to everyone (p. 328). Their share rises from 10 percent of small endowments to over 60 percent of those above 1 billion (p. 328). |
| Inflation tax | p. 331 | Inflation as a tax "on the idle rich, or, more precisely, on wealth that is not invested". It can be escaped entirely by holding real assets (p. 331). |
| Sovereign wealth funds | p. 333 | State investment funds, mostly of petroleum exporters (Norway, Abu Dhabi, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar, Russia) and of non-petroleum states (China, Hong Kong, Singapore) (pp. 333-335). |
| Natural resource rent | p. 336 | "The difference between receipts from sales and the cost of production" of natural resources. About 5 percent of global GDP since the mid-2000s, half of it petroleum (p. 336). |
| International divergence | p. 338 | The rich countries gradually acquired by China or by the oil states' funds (pp. 338-339). |
| Oligarchic divergence | p. 339 | All countries, including China and the oil states, "owned more and more by the planet's billionaires and multimillionaires". Already "well under way", more probable and harder to fight than international divergence (pp. 339-340). Note 49 recasts oil-state divergence as itself oligarchic (p. 481). |
| Political secession of the largest fortunes / loss of democratic sovereignty | pp. 339-340 | The largest fortunes escape any single state's power to regulate or tax, and the very rich can change nationality. This, not foreign ownership, is the main source of the rich countries' "feeling of dispossession" (pp. 339-340). |
| Net foreign asset position | p. 340 | A country's foreign assets minus foreign-owned domestic assets. Officially slightly negative for the rich countries combined (about −4 percent of global GDP in 2010) (p. 340). |
| "Earth owned by Mars" anomaly | p. 341 | Official statistics show the world as a whole in net debt, which is impossible. Zucman explains it by unreported assets held in tax havens (pp. 341-342). |
