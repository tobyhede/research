| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Growth decomposition | pp. 55-56 | Output growth = population growth + per capita output growth. Only the second improves living standards (p. 56). g in r > g is total output growth. |
| Law of cumulative growth | p. 57 | "a low annual growth rate over a very long period of time gives rise to considerable progress" (p. 57); the multiplier is (1 + g)^T (Table 2.2). |
| Law of cumulative returns | pp. 57-58 | A return of a few percent, compounded over decades, greatly increases the initial capital if reinvested, or if only a part "small in comparison with the growth rate of the society" is consumed (p. 58). "essentially identical" to cumulative growth (pp. 57-58). |
| Generation (time scale) | p. 57 | About thirty years: "the most relevant time scale for evaluating change in the society we live in" (p. 57). 1% a year = about 35% a generation; 2–2.5% = doubling (p. 57). |
| Malthusian regime | p. 58 | Very low but not zero population growth before 1700, with gains often wiped out by epidemic and famine (p. 58). |
| Demographic transition | p. 59 | Rising life expectancy no longer offsets falling birth rates, so population growth slows (p. 59). Happened early in France (p. 61). |
| Fertility arithmetic | p. 60 | Small changes in children per couple have large effects; endnote 4: 1.8 surviving children per woman → about −0.3% a year; 2.2 → +0.3% (p. 440). |
| Growth as equalizer | p. 62 | Strong demographic (and economic) growth reduces the weight of inherited wealth: "every generation must in some sense construct itself" (p. 62). |
| Circulation of elites (growth and mobility) | p. 63 | A second, weaker equalising mechanism: growth creates new functions and skills each generation, which raises mobility for those whose parents were not in the elite (pp. 63-64). |
| World technological frontier | p. 70 | The most productive economies. No frontier country has sustained per capita growth above 1.5% for a long period (p. 70). |
| Catch-up | p. 70 | Growth of 3–4% or more occurs only in countries catching up; "by definition" transitional and impossible globally (p. 70). |
| Trente Glorieuses | p. 72 | The thirty years from the late 1940s to the late 1970s of fast growth in continental Europe; explained as catch-up after 1914–1945, not as a policy achievement (pp. 72-74). |
| Double bell curve | p. 74 | Global growth rates for population and per capita output each rise and then fall over 1700–2100; population peaks around 1950–1970, per capita output around 2012–2050, and per capita output falls to just above 1%, not zero (pp. 74-75). |
| Median scenario | pp. 71, 75 | Piketty's working projection: rich countries 1.2% per capita a year to 2100; emerging countries 5% to 2030 and 4% to 2050; global output growth about 3.5% to 2030, 3% in 2030–2050, about 1.5% after 2050 (pp. 71, 75-76). Called "relatively optimistic" (p. 71). |
| Real vs nominal growth | p. 76 | Real growth = nominal growth minus inflation measured by the consumer price index (p. 76). The split is "in part arbitrary" because relative prices and new goods make price indices uncertain (p. 76). |
| Relative prices | pp. 65-66 | Over the long run, prices of goods shift relative to each other (industry falls, food stays roughly average, services rise), so a single average price index fails to capture changes in purchasing power (pp. 65-66). |
| Monetary stability / franc germinal | pp. 77-78 | Before 1914 inflation was close to zero; the franc germinal kept the livre tournois's silver content (4.5 g) and the same metal parity held from 1726 to 1914 (pp. 77-78). |
