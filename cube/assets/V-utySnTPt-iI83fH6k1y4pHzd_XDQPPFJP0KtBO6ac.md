| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forces of convergence / divergence | p. 426 | Restated from the Introduction: convergence "associated in particular with the diffusion of knowledge and skills"; divergence potentially threatening to democratic societies and social justice (p. 426). |
| r > g as the Central Contradiction of Capitalism | p. 426 | Section title. The private rate of return on capital r "can be significantly higher for long periods of time" than the growth rate of income and output g (pp. 426-427). Called "a fundamental logical contradiction" (p. 427) and "the fundamental structural contradiction of capitalism (r > g)" (p. 427). Earlier the book called r > g "fundamental force for divergence" (p. 254; also the Introduction section title at p. 22) and "a historical fact, not a logical necessity" (p. 255). |
| "The past devours the future" | p. 427 | Piketty's slogan for r > g: once constituted, capital "reproduces itself faster than output increases" (p. 427), so inherited wealth outgrows wages and output. |
| Entrepreneur becomes rentier | p. 427 | The life-cycle form of r > g: the entrepreneur "inevitably tends to become a rentier, more and more dominant over those who own nothing but their labor" (p. 427). |
| Progressive annual tax on capital | p. 427 | "The right solution" (p. 427). Illustrative schedule: 0.1 or 0.5% under €1m, 1% €1–5m, 2% €5–10m, up to 5 or 10% on fortunes of several hundred million or billions (p. 427), recapping ch15 (pp. 380, 390). Its purpose is to stop "an endless inegalitarian spiral while preserving competition and incentives for new instances of primitive accumulation" (p. 427). |
| Primitive accumulation | p. 427 | Used approvingly for new entrepreneurial wealth creation, which the capital tax should preserve (p. 427); a deliberate inversion of the Marxian term. |
| Common utility | p. 428 | From Article 1 of the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man, with which the book opens: social distinctions are justified only by common utility. Extreme wealth inequalities have none and do not promote growth (p. 428). |
| Regional political integration | p. 428 | The level at which a capital tax becomes possible; "only regional political integration can lead to effective regulation of the globalized patrimonial capitalism of the twenty-first century" (p. 428). The nation-state remains the right level for many social and fiscal policies and for new forms of shared ownership (p. 428). |
| Political economy (vs "economic science") | p. 428 | Piketty dislikes "economic science" as "terribly arrogant" and prefers "political economy", which conveys economics' distinguishing "political, normative, and moral purpose" (p. 428): the rational study of what public policies and institutions bring us closer to an ideal society (pp. 428-429). |
| Economics as a subdiscipline of the social sciences | p. 428 | Alongside history, sociology, anthropology and political science; restated from the Introduction (p. 428). |
| Scientistic illusion | p. 429 | The tendency of the new experimental methods to prove "a pure and true causal relation" while forgetting the question is of limited interest, and to neglect history (p. 429). |
| Serial history | p. 430 | The French programme (1930–1980) of compiling long statistical series on prices, wages, incomes, profits and literacy, exemplified by Bouvier, Furet and Gillet (1965) and Furet and Ozouf (1977). Its virtues were rigour and data; its flaws, aridity and little interpretation (pp. 430-431). Piketty's own project is presented as its successor. |
| Patrimonial capitalism | p. 428 | Reused: capitalism in which inherited wealth dominates; "globalized patrimonial capitalism of the twenty-first century" (p. 428). |
