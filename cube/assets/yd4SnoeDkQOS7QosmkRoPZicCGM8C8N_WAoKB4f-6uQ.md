**A. The fall of the rentier explains the French compression (pp. 196-200)**

- **Premises**: (p. 196) The top decile's share of national income fell about 15 points over the century. (p. 197) The top decile's share of wages, and the bottom half's, did not change. (pp. 197-198) The top centile's share of income fell by more than half while its share of wages stayed at 6–7 percent. (p. 199) Capital income is overrepresented at the top, and 1914–1945 brought large shocks to capital.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 198) The reduction of inequality is "largely explained by the fall of the rentier"; no Kuznets-type structural compression occurred.
- **Assessment**: The decomposition is close to an accounting identity once the wage series are taken as given: if wage shares are flat and total shares fall, the fall must be in non-wage income. The weight therefore rests on the reliability of separate wage series (from the 1917 *cédulaire* wage tax, note 4) and on national-accounts denominators. Note 8 concedes private-sector pay before 1914–1917 is "little known" and is inferred from civil-service scales.

**B. The compression was political and violent, not spontaneous (pp. 198-199)**

- **Premises**: (p. 198) The compression is concentrated in 1914–1945; shares reached their low after World War II and did not recover. (p. 199) Estate-tax data show wealth concentration still rising on the eve of World War I.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 198) "it was war, and not harmonious democratic or economic rationality" that reset the distribution; the history of inequality is "chaotic and political".
- **Assessment**: The counterfactual (that without 1914 concentration would have kept rising) rests on the wealth series of ch10, not on income data, which begin only in 1915. The argument is strong on timing, weaker on mechanism: it bundles physical destruction, bankruptcy, inflation and policy without apportioning them here (that was ch05's job).

**C. Rentiers have not come back, and that needs explaining (pp. 200-201)**

- **Premises**: (p. 200) Capital dominates today only above the top 0.1 percent. (pp. 200-201) The capital/income ratio has returned to Belle Époque levels (Part Two).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 201) The non-return of the rentier is the interesting question; progressive taxation is one cause, others may be "equally important".
- **Assessment**: The puzzle is posed, not solved; ch10–ch11 take it up. It sits uneasily with the claim on p. 211 that the "real issue" is the renewed importance of inheritance: the chapter asks why rentiers stayed down and also warns they are coming back.

**D. The interwar paradox resolved by income source (pp. 205-207)**

- **Premises**: (p. 206) "The 1 percent" lived on dividends and interest, which collapsed in the Depression. (pp. 206-207) "The 9 percent" were salaried managers and civil servants, largely spared unemployment, holding nominal pay while prices fell 25 percent in 1929–1935, and receiving nominally rigid rents.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 207) The top decile share rose in 1929–1935 while the top centile share fell; the Popular Front then reversed it.
- **Methodological conclusion**: (p. 207) A Gini coefficient would have hidden all of this; decomposition by fractile and income source is necessary.

**E. US inequality since 1980 is mainly wage inequality at the very top (pp. 214-220)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 214-215) Capital gains add only about one point to the structural trend. (pp. 215-216) About three-quarters of the 15-point rise went to the top centile. (pp. 217-218) The top decile's wage share rose 10 points, about two-thirds of the total-income increase. (p. 219) 60–70 percent of the top 0.1 percent are top managers.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 219) The new US inequality is about "supermanagers" more than "superstars"; capital income still contributes about a third (p. 218).
- **Assessment**: This is the chapter's most consequential finding for the book's thesis. The main driver of the best-known modern rise in inequality is not r > g but labor income. Piketty presents it straightforwardly; the explanation (bargaining power, norms, top tax rates) comes in ch09 and ch14. The occupational evidence comes from matched tax and corporate records (Bakija, Cole and Heim, note 42) and depends on "what definitions one chooses" (p. 219). Note 42 adds a strong qualification: at the top 0.1 percent capital income is over half of income with capital gains and about a quarter without, and the origin of these fortunes is unknown.

**F. Inequality and the 2008 crisis (pp. 215-217)**

- **Premises**: (p. 216) The top 10 percent took three-quarters of US growth 1977–2007, the top 1 percent nearly 60 percent; the bottom 90 percent grew under 0.5 percent a year. (p. 216) Stagnant incomes plus deregulated lenders made household borrowing likely. (p. 217) The internal transfer (about 15 points) is nearly four times the trade deficit (about 4 points).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 216) Inequality "contributed" to instability, but (p. 217) was not the sole or primary cause.
- **Assessment**: The mechanism is asserted with "absolutely no doubt" (p. 216) but supported only by reference to Kumhof–Rancière and Rajan (note 32); no evidence on household debt is presented. The comparison of internal transfer to trade deficit sets a stock-like redistribution against a flow imbalance; both are in points of national income, but their causal links to financial fragility are different.
