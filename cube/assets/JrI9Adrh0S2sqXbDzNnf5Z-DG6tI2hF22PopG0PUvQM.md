| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Race between education and technology | p. 220 | The "most widely accepted theory" of wage inequality (Goldin and Katz). Two hypotheses: wage equals marginal productivity; productivity depends mainly on skill and on supply and demand for skill (p. 220). If skill supply lags technology's demand, inequality rises (p. 221). |
| Marginal productivity | p. 220 | A worker's "individual contribution to the output of the firm or office for which he works" (p. 220). Estimable, roughly, only for replicable jobs (p. 226); for unique jobs under imperfect information it becomes "something close to a pure ideological construct" (pp. 239-240). |
| Skill-biased technological change | p. 228 | The explanation of top-pay growth by new technology making unique skills more productive; called tautological, since any change in the wage hierarchy can be attributed to some supposed technical change (p. 228). |
| Labor market as social construct | p. 224 | The labor market is "not a mathematical abstraction" but "a social construct based on specific rules and compromises" (p. 224). |
| Minimum wage / wage schedules | p. 224 | Institutional rules setting wage floors and pay scales, nationally or by branch bargaining (Germany, Sweden) (p. 225). Justified by unmeasurable productivity, wage insurance, specific investments and monopsony (pp. 225-227). |
| Wage insurance | p. 226 | Guaranteed monthly pay that shifts short-run income risk to wealthier owners and managers; the move from daily to monthly wages helped constitute the working class (p. 226). |
| Specific investments | p. 226 | Firm-specific skills that workers will underinvest in if pay can be changed unilaterally; "probably the most important argument in favor of fixed wage scales" and for stakeholder ("Rhenish") governance (pp. 226-227). |
| Monopsony | p. 227 | A small group of employers who are nearly the only buyers of labor locally and can push wages below marginal productivity; a minimum wage then can be "just but also efficient" (p. 227). |
| Supermanager | p. 220 | Carried from ch08: top executives of large firms with historically unprecedented pay. Here "largely an Anglo-Saxon phenomenon" (p. 229). |
| Social norms (of pay) | p. 241 | Beliefs, specific to each country and period, about what different people contribute to output; they constrain what boards and shareholders will tolerate. A firm can hardly go against them (p. 241). |
| Pay for luck | p. 243 | Bertrand and Mullainathan's finding that executive pay rises most when firm performance improves for external reasons beyond management's control (p. 243). |
| Meritocratic extremism | p. 242 | "the apparent need of modern societies, and especially US society, to designate certain individuals as “winners”" and reward them more if they seem selected for intrinsic merit (p. 242). Developed in ch11. |
| Top marginal income tax rate (as amplifier) | p. 243 | High top rates act as a "protective barrier", low ones as an "incentive to mischief"; their post-1980 fall in English-speaking countries raised executives' incentive to bargain for pay (p. 243). Developed in ch14. |
| "Black hole" of growth | p. 238 | The gap between output growth in national accounts and income growth in household surveys (India, China); partly explained by unmeasured gains at the top (p. 238). |
