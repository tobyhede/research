- How far does the list of fundamental rights extend (culture, housing, travel, p. 349)? Piketty raises the question and does not answer it. Is the answer meant to come only from democratic deliberation (p. 350)?
- Does the Rawlsian reading of "common utility" do real work in Part Four, or is it decorative? Check whether ch14 (optimal top rates) or ch15 (capital tax) argues from the interest of the most disadvantaged, or from efficiency, transparency and democratic control.
- What exactly is the evidence for the "5–10 times less volatile" claim (p. 357)? No source is given in text or note.
- The tax-stabilisation argument (p. 351) treats low growth as limiting consent to higher average tax rates. Is the post-1980 plateau evidence of a growth constraint, or of a political shift (the "ultraliberal wave" Piketty himself invokes for poor countries, p. 359)? He uses ideology to explain the poor-country decline but growth to explain the rich-country plateau.
- Is the proposed unified individual-accounts PAYGO scheme (p. 358) compatible with Beveridgian capped pensions, or is it implicitly Bismarckian (proportional to lifetime earnings)? Note 46 points to the Swedish notional-account model (p. 487), which is contributory.
- How does the case against income-contingent loans (n. 41, p. 487), which calls them a surtax on students of modest background, square with the progressive income tax Piketty defends in ch14? Both are taxes on later earnings.
- Is "ultraliberal" pressure as the cause of the poor-country revenue decline (p. 359) supported beyond Cagé and Gadenne's customs-duty result?
