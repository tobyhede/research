- **French incidence study, 2010** (p. 363): overall taxes 47% of national income; bottom 50% pay 40–45%, next 40% pay 45–50%, top 0.1% pay 35%. Source: Landais, Piketty and Saez, *Pour une révolution fiscale* (2010), in Piketty's note 4 (p. 488). Piketty's note 5 says the estimate omits income in tax havens and assumes tax shelters are equally common at all levels, so it probably overstates the effective top rate (pp. 488-489). The generalisation to Europe and the US is "All signs are" (p. 363), not a study.
- **Estate taxation in France**: average estate and gift tax "barely 5 percent", 20% for the top centile of inheritances (Piketty's note 7) (p. 489).
- **Figure 14.1** (top income tax rates, US, UK, Germany, France, 1900–2013) and **Figure 14.2** (top inheritance tax rates, same countries) (p. 365), (p. 369). Sources: online appendix only ("piketty.pse.ens.fr/capital21c"). Captions: US top income rate 70% (1980) to 28% (1988) (p. 365); US top inheritance rate 70% (1980) to 35% (2013) (p. 369). Piketty's note 15 says French rates exclude the 25% surcharge on the childless (pp. 489-490); notes 25 and 32 say the estate rates are for direct-line heirs (pp. 491-492).
- **Dates of adoption** of progressive income taxes: Denmark 1870, Japan 1887, Prussia 1891, Sweden 1903, Britain 1909, US 1913, France July 15, 1914 (p. 364).
- **Rate histories**: France 2% (1914), 50% (1920), 60% (1924), 72% (1925) (p. 365); Prussia 3% (1891–1914), 4% (1915–1918), 40% (1919–1920); US 67% then 77% in 1918–1919; Britain 8% (1909), over 40% after the war (p. 366); French tax on financial income 3% (1872–1890), 4% (1890–1914) (p. 367); French estate tax top rate 5% (1902–1910), 6.5% (1911–1914), effective rate on top centile of estates 3% after 1901 vs 1% before (p. 368); German estate tax on family bequests 0% to 35% in 1919 (p. 369); British top estate rate 8% (1896) to 15% (1908) (pp. 369-370); US top income rate 25% (late 1920s), 63% (1933), 79% (1937), 88% (1942), 94% (1944), about 90% to the mid-1960s, 70% early 1980s, average 81% 1932–1980 (p. 371), with state income taxes of 5–10% on top (Piketty's note 30) (p. 492); Germany 90% in 1947–1949 under occupation (p. 371); Japan 85% under occupation, 55% in 1950 (Piketty's note 31) (p. 492); Britain 98% in 1941–1952 and 1974–1978 (Piketty's note 33) (p. 492), with 83% on labour income when capital income faced 98% (Piketty's note 34) (p. 492). Most rate series sit in Piketty's own earlier work (*Les hauts revenus en France*, 2001) and the online appendix.
- **Historical testimony**: Leroy-Beaulieu's *Essai* (1881) as an example of data-free argument (p. 368), with Piketty's note 21 dissecting his indigence statistics (pp. 490-491); Joseph Caillaux in 1907–1908 conceding from estate statistics that "a small number of people possess the bulk of this country’s wealth" (Piketty's note 24) (p. 491); Willford King (1915) on US concentration (p. 370); Irving Fisher's 1919 AEA presidential address, proposing estate taxes of two-thirds rising to 100% after three generations, inspired by Rignano (p. 371) (Piketty's note 29) (pp. 491-492); Josiah Wedgwood (1929, preface 1938) and Bertrand Russell on "plutodemocracies" (pp. 372-373).
- **Cross-country correlation** of top-rate cuts with top-share increases: eighteen OECD countries in the WTID, from Piketty, Saez and Stantcheva, "Optimal Taxation of Top Labor Incomes: A Tale of Three Elasticities" (forthcoming), fig. 3 (Piketty's note 38) (p. 493). The text's "perfectly correlated" (p. 373) is not given a coefficient.
- **Growth comparison**: no significant relation between top-rate cuts and per capita growth, robust to start year (Piketty's note 39) (p. 493); US per capita GDP 20% higher in level but growing at about the same rate as Europe (pp. 374-375), the level gap due to hours worked (Piketty's note 42) (p. 493); US per capita growth 2.3% (1950–1970), 2.2% (1970–1990), 1.4% (1990–2012) (Piketty's note 44) (p. 493). Piketty warns that comparisons over a few years can justify anything and need ten to twenty years (p. 374) (Piketty's note 41) (p. 493).
- **Executive pay database**: nearly three thousand listed firms in fourteen countries (Piketty's note 46) (p. 494), supporting the luck/talent and tax-rate findings (pp. 375-376).
- **Rival explanations named and rejected**: Gabaix and Landier (2008) on firm size, rejected because firm size grew similarly everywhere but pay did not and their data are US-only (Piketty's note 47) (p. 494); Rajan and Zingales, Acemoglu-Robinson-Verdier, Grusky on competition (Piketty's note 48) (p. 494); Acemoglu-Robinson-Verdier on US innovation spilling abroad, which Piketty calls "essentially theoretical" and based on patents per capita (Piketty's note 45) (pp. 493-494); the claim that 1950–1980 executives took pay in kind, which he says the evidence contradicts (Piketty's note 49) (p. 494).
- **Political capture literature**: Hacker and Pierson, Schlozman-Verba-Brady, Noah (Piketty's note 52) (p. 494). **Skill-gap literature** criticised for relying on surveys: Goldin and Katz, Blank, Rajan (Piketty's note 53) (p. 494).
- **Bare assertion**: that the flight of executives to Canada and Mexico is "devoid of common sense" and contradicted by "all the firm-level data at our disposal" (p. 377), with no data shown; that US politicians are wealthier than European ones, offered with the admission that "data on this are sparse" (p. 378).
