- **Inevitability, 1956 against 1976.** In 1956 the consequences of socialist planning are called inevitable and totalitarian forces will prevail if the policy is pursued (p. 48), and the logic of its policies drove Labour into coercion (p. 45). In 1976 he denies having said that any movement toward socialism is bound to lead to totalitarianism (p. 55). The two are reconcilable only by reading the 1956 claim as conditional on (a) full socialist planning and (b) persistence in it, with the escape clause that a people can change course (p. 46). My observation: the 1976 denial rejects the any-movement version, yet the paragraph just before it says the welfare-state route tends to very much the same ultimate outcome (p. 55), which reintroduces a strong tendency claim for partial measures. The denial is of necessity, not of direction.
- **Welfare state, 1956 against 1976.** 1956: some welfare-state aims are practicable and laudable and the question is which instruments are used (p. 43). 1976: he regrets concessions to interventionism as unwarranted and treats welfare-state redistribution as a slower road to much the same end (p. 55). The 1976 position is harder than the 1956 one, and both need checking against the book's own allowances for social insurance and a minimum income in the main chapters (chapter 9 is the likely place; not in my units).
- **Is socialism dead or growing?** 1956: hot socialism is nearly dead, its illusions discarded by its leaders (p. 42). 1976: the influence of socialist ideas has markedly increased since the book was written (pp. 55-56). Consistent only because the word has changed meaning (p. 55).
- **What counts as the book's merit.** 1956: the thesis was not original; the merit is the analysis of process (p. 42). 1976: the book may contain much he could not have convincingly demonstrated (p. 56), and in 1956 there were points he would state more cautiously or fortify with more proof (p. 44). These concessions sit beside the claim that experience strengthened his concern (p. 45).
- **Scope of the thesis as restated in 1956.** The totalitarianism is that which central control of all economic activity tends to produce (p. 42). That wording limits the claim to comprehensive control, which is narrower than the worry about piecemeal measures a page later (p. 43).
- **Russia.** Both later texts say the book underplayed the Soviet case for wartime reasons (pp. 39-40, p. 55). Synthesis should treat the German-centred evidence of the main chapters as a choice Hayek later called a fault (p. 55).
- **Rule of Law.** 1956 calls chapter 6 the central chapter and the source of the tests any reform should pass (p. 43), calls it all too brief (p. 45), and says its reasons explain the British administrative state (p. 48). The Rule of Law chapter is thus the part Hayek himself nominates as the positive criterion.
- **Chapter 10.** Hayek quotes his own chapter 10 on socialists' democratic inhibitions to show he never imputed totalitarian aims (p. 48; Caldwell's note 23, p. 52).
- **Origins.** The 1976 preface names the Beveridge memorandum as the first step (p. 53); that memorandum is the 1933 document printed in the appendix (see ap01, pp. 237-241).
- **Control of Engagement Order.** Hayek 1956 reads it as proof that planning forces coercion (pp. 45-46). Friedman reads the same episode as the moment liberty won and planning was dropped (see ap01, p. 250). Same fact, opposite lessons.
- **Chapter 16.** The conclusion calls the book essentially critical (p. 234). The 1976 preface agrees that it is incomplete without an account of the market order (p. 54).
- **Conservatism.** The 1956 distinction (p. 44) anticipates the essay Caldwell cites, Why I Am Not a Conservative (note 14, p. 51); a link for cross-book synthesis with The Constitution of Liberty.
