- **Labour/capital split.** The textbook two-thirds/one-third figure (p. 32); endnote 5 gives 65–70% vs 30–35% (p. 436). The twentieth-century movement is stated, not yet shown; data come in ch06.
- **Rich-country orders of magnitude, 2010 (pp. 40-42).** Income 30,000–35,000 euros per head, private wealth 150,000–200,000 euros; β > 6 in Japan and Italy, < 5 in the US and Germany (p. 40). Source: official national wealth accounts compiled with central banks (p. 47); country detail in the online appendix (endnote 10, p. 436). Endnote 12: cash is only 1–2% of wealth; public assets roughly equal public debts (p. 436).
- **Returns by asset (pp. 42-44).** Stocks 7–8%, real estate and bonds 3–4%, rural land 4–5% (also in Austen and Balzac, where capital is twenty years of rent), a Paris apartment worth 1m euros renting at 2,500 euros a month (3%), a 100,000-euro apartment at 400 euros a month (nearly 5%), listed firms at 12–15 years of profits (6–8%). These are illustrative, not sourced series.
- **History of national accounting (pp. 45-47).** Petty (1664), King (1696), Boisguillebert (1695), Vauban (1707), Lavoisier (1791), Giffen (1870–1900), de Foville and Colson; annual series by Kuznets and Kendrick, Bowley and Clark, Dugé de Bernonville; post-war official series; 1990s–2000s wealth accounts. Endnote 19 points to Vanoli and Stone on SNA 1993, the first system with consistent capital accounts (p. 437).
- **Global output and population, 1700–2012 (Figures 1.1–1.3, pp. 48-49).** Europe 47% of world GDP in 1913, 25% in 2012; Europe 26% of world population in 1913, 10% in 2012; Asia-Africa per capita GDP from 37% of the world average in 1950 to 61% in 2012. Source: Angus Maddison (p. 48; endnote 20, p. 438), whose series cover only output and population, not national income, the capital-labour split or the capital stock (endnote 20).
- **Table 1.1, 2012 regional distribution (pp. 49-50).** World 7 billion people, 70 trillion euros of output; EU 540m at 27,000 euros; Russia-Ukraine 200m at 15,000; US-Canada 350m at 40,000; Latin America 600m at 10,000; sub-Saharan Africa 900m at 2,000; China 8,000; Japan about 30,000. Endnote 22 notes the EU bloc includes non-members such as Switzerland and Norway (p. 438). Values at PPP.
- **PPP vs exchange rates (Figures 1.4–1.5, pp. 50-52).** Euro/dollar: $1.30 market vs $1.20 PPP in 2012; euro/yuan: 8 market vs 5 PPP. PPP correction 1.6 for China and 2.5 for India (p. 51). Source: ICP (World Bank, Eurostat); endnote 27 cites the 2005 ICP report (p. 438). Maddison argued official statistics underestimate Chinese GDP (p. 51; endnote 30, p. 439).
- **Net foreign income (pp. 52-53).** +1–2% of GDP for the US, France and Britain; +2–3% for Japan and Germany; continents balance within 0.5%; Africa −5% (as low as −10% in some countries) from UN, World Bank and IMF balance-of-payments data since 1970 (p. 53). Endnote 33: the outflow of capital income from Africa is about three times the inflow of international aid (p. 439).
- **Derivation of Africa's foreign-owned share (p. 53).** If income is 5% below output and capital's share is 30%, the foreign-owned share of capital is about 5/30 ≈ 17%, which Piketty rounds to "nearly 20 percent". This assumes foreign-owned capital earns the same return as domestic capital. Since housing and farmland are rarely foreign-owned, the foreign share of manufacturing capital may be 40–50% (p. 53).
- **1913 imbalances (p. 53).** British national income about 10% above domestic product; France more than 5%; Germany a close third. Source: online appendix (endnote 34, p. 439).
- **Static trade gains (p. 55).** Endnote 36: opening India and China yielded static gains of just 0.4% of world GDP (3.5% for China, 1.6% for India) (p. 440); the study is not named in the note.
- **Endnote numbering anomaly.** Endnote 6 (attached to the identity National income = domestic output + net income from abroad, p. 36) repeats endnote 5's labour/capital split almost word for word, while endnote 7's definition of national income as net national product fits the earlier position (p. 436). The notes for this chapter appear to be shifted by one from note 6 onward, so readers should check note content against the text it is attached to.
