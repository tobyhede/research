| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| political book | p. 37 | Hayek 1944: a book whose whole argument is derived from certain ultimate values, as opposed to a neutral scientific treatise or an essay in social philosophy. |
| socialists of all parties | p. 37 | Hayek's dedication. 1956: meant without mockery; the addressees were left-leaning friends and colleagues, the socialist intelligentsia of England (p. 39). 1976: he is proud of the insight behind it (p. 54). |
| hot socialism | p. 42 | Hayek 1956: the organized movement toward deliberate organization of economic life by the state as chief owner of the means of production. Said to be nearly dead in the West, its century ending around 1948. |
| Welfare State | p. 43 | Hayek 1956: a hodgepodge of ill-assembled and often inconsistent ideals that has replaced socialism as the reformers' goal; some aims practicable and laudable, the danger lies in the instruments chosen. 1976: folded into the new meaning of socialism (p. 55). |
| liberal | p. 44 | Hayek 1956: used throughout in the original nineteenth-century sense still current in Britain; in American usage it has come to mean nearly the opposite. |
| conservatism | p. 44 | Hayek 1956: a necessary element of a stable society but not a social program; paternalistic, nationalistic, power-adoring, traditionalistic, anti-intellectual; a defender of established privilege. Distinct from true liberalism. |
| privilege | p. 44 | Hayek 1956: the state granting and protecting rights to some that are not available on equal terms to others. Liberalism is the denial of all privilege in this sense. |
| psychological change | p. 46 | Hayek 1956: the alteration in the character of a people produced by extensive government control, working over one or two generations; named as one of the book's main points. |
| new kind of servitude | p. 47 | Tocqueville's phrase, adopted by Hayek 1956: a regular, quiet, gentle servitude under a network of small rules, compatible with the outward forms of freedom. Source of the book's title (Hayek's note 22, p. 52). |
| New Despotism | p. 48 | Lord Chief Justice Hewart's phrase, adopted by Hayek 1956: arbitrary administrative government by an honest bureaucracy, in practice free from parliamentary control (pp. 48-49; Caldwell's gloss p. 53). |
| socialism (1944 sense) | p. 55 | Hayek 1976: nationalization of the means of production plus the central economic planning this makes possible and necessary. |
| socialism (1976 sense) | p. 55 | Hayek 1976: chiefly extensive redistribution of incomes through taxation and the institutions of the welfare state. |
| interventionist superstitions | p. 55 | Hayek 1976: the current beliefs he had not shed in 1944 and which led him to make concessions he now thinks unwarranted. He does not list them. |
