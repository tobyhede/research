- What does the PSS paper's identification actually rest on? The chapter gives only "perfectly correlated" (p. 373) across eighteen countries and a firm-level panel (Piketty's note 46) (p. 494). How are reverse causation (capture) and common shocks (globalization, finance) handled? Check the paper and the online appendix.
- Is the macro growth test (p. 374) powerful enough? Differences of "a few tenths of a percent" (p. 375) are what is at issue, and Piketty admits such comparisons are hard to draw precisely (p. 375). Absence of a significant relation is not evidence of absence.
- The French incidence study (p. 363) is Piketty's own co-authored work and, by note 5, probably overstates top rates (pp. 488-489). Have later incidence studies (e.g., for the US) confirmed a regressive top?
- Does the war thesis (pp. 364-370), (p. 377) survive Piketty's own pre-war examples: Denmark, Japan, Prussia, Sweden, Britain, the US all adopted progressive taxes in peacetime "after due deliberation" (p. 364)? The thesis is about rates, not adoption; keep that distinction in the synthesis.
- What is the base for the 80% rate: labour income only (the PSS paper is about "top labor incomes", Piketty's note 38) (p. 493) or total income? The chapter's earlier argument (p. 362), (p. 372) wants capital income taxed at least as heavily.
- How does the claim that 80% rates would lift pay "at lower levels" (p. 377) work? It is asserted as a consequence of the bargaining model but no mechanism or evidence is given.
- If the US is "big enough" to tax alone (p. 377) and executive flight is implausible, why is capital flight so central in ch15? The answer presumably is that capital is more mobile than people; check ch15's treatment.
