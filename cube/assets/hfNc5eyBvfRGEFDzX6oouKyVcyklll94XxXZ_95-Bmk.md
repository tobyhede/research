- **US top decile share, 1910–2010 (Figure I.1, pp. 21-22).** Extends Kuznets's 1913–1948 series using federal income tax returns plus national income estimates. Top decile 45–50% in the 1910s–1920s, 30–35% by the late 1940s, stable 1950–1970, back to 45–50% by 2000 (p. 21). Provenance: Piketty and Saez (2003), endnote 22 (p. 434); data at the online appendix (p. 21). Kuznets's own source: *Shares of Upper Income Groups in Income and Savings* (1953), endnote 11 (p. 433).
- **European capital/income ratio, 1870–2010 (Figure I.2, pp. 22-23).** Private wealth net of debt in Britain, France and Germany, in years of national income: about 6–7 in 1910, 2–3 in 1950, 4–6 in 2010 (p. 23). Provenance: Piketty and Zucman (2013), endnote 31 (p. 435); extends Goldsmith's national balance sheets (p. 18; endnote 32, p. 435).
- **Three source families (pp. 15-18).** (1) Income tax returns plus national accounts, compiled in the WTID by about thirty researchers; series start when an income tax began, mostly 1910–1920, earlier in Japan and Germany (p. 16). (2) Estate tax returns, following Lampman (US 1922–1956) and Atkinson and Harrison (Britain 1923–1972); French estate data go back to the Revolution, built with Postel-Vinay and Rosenthal (p. 17; endnotes 27-28, p. 434). (3) National wealth estimates, back to the early eighteenth century for Britain and France (pp. 17-18).
- **Why tax records rather than surveys.** Endnote 19: household surveys rarely go back before 1970 and "tend to seriously underestimate higher incomes" (p. 434); tax records cover high incomes and a century of history. Endnote 12 (p. 433): national accounts are needed for the denominator because only part of the population files returns.
- **Wage stagnation, early nineteenth century (p. 8).** Wages stagnated from the 1800s to the 1850s in Britain and France while growth accelerated, and the capital share rose. Endnote 5 (p. 432) cites Robert Allen's term for this, Engels’s pause, from his 2007 and 2009 papers. Piketty says this is estimated "with the imperfect sources available today" (p. 8).
- **France vs Britain wealth concentration around 1900 (p. 26).** Asserted here; evidence deferred to Part Three.
- **Population figures (pp. 5, 25).** France 20 million in 1700 vs Britain 8 million; France about 30 million in 1780; US 3 million at independence, 100 million by 1900, above 300 million today.
- **Stock-market capitalisation (p. 25).** Rich countries regained the market capitalisation relative to GDP of Paris and London in the early 1900s only in the twenty-first century. No source given here.
- **Source caveat.** Piketty admits that estate data exist for fewer countries than income data (p. 17), that long-term trends are "far better documented in the rich countries" (p. 24), and that pre-1910 income-inequality series do not exist (p. 17).
