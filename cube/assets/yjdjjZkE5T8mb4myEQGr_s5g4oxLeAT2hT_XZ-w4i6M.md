**A. Size constant, composition transformed (pp. 84-89)**

- **Premises**: (p. 86) National capital = farmland + housing + other domestic capital + net foreign capital, all at market value. (pp. 86-87) Series for Britain and France (Figures 3.1-3.2) show farmland collapsing as agriculture's share of activity fell from nearly three-quarters to a few percent, offset by rises in housing and other domestic capital. (p. 85) The total stays between six and seven years before 1914 and returns to five to six by 2010.
- **Conclusion**: "The nature of capital has changed" (p. 87) but it "has lost none of its importance" (p. 87); the mid-century fall was a war effect, not a structural change (p. 85).
- **Unstated premise**: that market valuations of assets as different as eighteenth-century farmland and twenty-first-century shares are comparable in "years of income". The chapter treats market value as the only possible aggregator; ch04 (p. 107) makes this explicit and admits the "arbitrariness".

**B. Public debt is a claim within the population, not national wealth (pp. 83, 86, 97)**

- **Premises**: (p. 83) A bond is a claim of bondholders on taxpayers. (p. 86) In the national balance sheet it is an asset of the private sector and a liability of the public sector, and nets out if held domestically.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 83) Bonds belong in private wealth only; (p. 97) Ricardo correctly saw Britain's debt as "a claim of one portion of the population on another".
- **Consequence drawn**: national β can be stable while private β rises (Britain 1770–1810, p. 93), so the private/public split matters for distribution even when national totals do not move.

**C. Who gains from public debt depends on r, g and inflation (pp. 93-96)**

- **Premises**: (p. 94) Lending to the state and receiving interest for decades beats paying taxes without compensation. (p. 94) Deficits raise demand for private wealth and so its return. (p. 94) In 1815–1914 inflation was about zero and bond yields 4–5 percent, above growth. (p. 94) Worked example: deficits of 5 percent of GDP for twenty years add debt of 100 percent of GDP; at 5 percent interest the state pays 5 percent of GDP a year to bondholders indefinitely if it never repays principal.
- **Conclusion**: Britain's debt "enhanced the influence of private wealth" (p. 93) and served lenders "quite well" (p. 93); Marx and nineteenth-century socialists were right to see debt as a tool of private capital (p. 94).
- **Contrast case**: (p. 95) In the twentieth century inflation turned debt into a transfer *from* lenders, making deficit finance progressive in effect; Piketty says this view persists although inflation has fallen and the distributional effects are now "relatively obscure" (p. 95).

**D. Against representative-agent debt neutrality (pp. 97-98)**

- **Premises**: (p. 97) Representative-agent models give every agent the same bonds. (p. 98) In fact public debt is held by a minority, and wealth has always been highly concentrated.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 98) Barro's neutrality result ignores the internal redistribution debt effects "when it is repaid as well as when it is not"; to study debt without inequality "is in fact to say nothing" about what is at stake.
- **Note**: the argument is conceptual, not empirical. No data on the distribution of bondholding are given here (he defers to "literary sources", p. 93).

**E. Public ownership as political choice (pp. 98-101)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 98-99) The Depression discredited laissez-faire; the Soviet war role raised the prestige of statism (Schumpeter 1942, Samuelson 1970); in France collaboration charges against the business elite led to nationalisations (Renault, banks, coal). (p. 99) By 1950 French public assets exceeded a year of national income with debt inflated away.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 100) France had a "capitalism without capitalists" during the Trente Glorieuses; (p. 101) after 1986 it returned to private ownership "without really understanding why".
