**A. Why German private wealth is low (pp. 104-105)**

- **Premises**: (p. 104) German private wealth (about four years) is below France and Britain (five to six) despite high saving. (p. 104) Cheap housing, partly from post-reunification supply, explains a little. (p. 104) Most of the gap is in "other domestic capital", i.e. firms valued at market prices. (p. 104) At book value German private wealth would match French and British levels.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 104-105) The gap reflects the stakeholder model's lower market valuation, not lower productive capital or lower social value.
- **Unstated premise**: that book value is a reasonable measure of "social" value. Book value is itself an accounting convention, and note 4 (p. 445) admits the gap may partly be "statistical biases". The argument also concedes, in effect, that β measured at market value depends on corporate governance law.

**B. Decomposing the 1913–1950 collapse (pp. 105-108)**

- **Premises**: (p. 105) National capital fell by more than four years of income in all three countries. (p. 106) Measured destruction: about one year in France, one and a half in Germany, under 10 percent of income in Britain. (p. 106) Britain lost about two years of income in net foreign assets. (p. 107) Saving was low and absorbed by public deficits; foreign assets were sold. (pp. 107-108) Rent control, financial regulation and taxation lowered real estate and share prices.
- **Conclusion**: "the budgetary and political shocks of two wars proved far more destructive to capital than combat itself" (p. 106); volume effects two-thirds to three-quarters, price effects one-quarter to one-third (p. 108).
- **Note**: the decomposition is stated as a result, with the detailed estimates deferred to the online appendix (note 9, p. 445). The reader cannot reproduce it from the book.

**C. Why land in America was worth little (pp. 108-109)**

- **Premises**: (p. 108) Hectares per head were far greater than in Europe, so capital per head in volume was higher. (pp. 108-109) Past some threshold, abundance lowers the price so much that price × volume is lower than with less volume.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 108) "the price effect more than counterbalanced the volume effect".
- **Unstated premise**: that the demand for land was price-inelastic (an elasticity below one), so that more land reduces its total value. This is the same kind of claim that ch06 makes about the elasticity of substitution between capital and labour, but here it is asserted without estimate. Supporting evidence: land prices in probate records and wills (p. 109).

**D. Slavery and the first law (pp. 116-117)**

- **Premises**: (p. 116) α = r × β holds by definition; with α = 40 percent for nonhuman capital and r = 5 percent, nonhuman β = 8. (p. 116) Apply the same capitalisation to the 60 percent of income that slaves yield their owners: 0.60 / 0.05 = 12. (p. 117) Total = 1 / 0.05 = 20 years.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 116-117) slave capital can exceed all other wealth; actual US values were lower for the reasons on p. 117.
- **Extension**: (p. 117) Any capitalisation of labour income at a rate close to r makes "human capital" exceed nonhuman capital whenever the labour share exceeds half. So "There is no need for amazement" (p. 117); the comparison should be of flows, not capitalised stocks.
- **Note 15 (p. 445)**: if slaves are treated as persons, slavery is a debt between individuals and nets out of national wealth, like public debt. This is the same accounting logic as ch03's treatment of government bonds (ch03, p. 83).
