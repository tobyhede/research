**A. Why wealth concentration points to inheritance (pp. 176-177)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 176-177) If wealth were accumulated mainly as a precaution against short-term wage shocks, wealth inequality would be smaller than instantaneous wage inequality. (p. 177) If it were mainly life-cycle saving for retirement (Modigliani), wealth inequality would mirror labor inequality, shifted in time. (pp. 176-177) Observed wealth inequality is "always and everywhere" much greater than labor inequality. (p. 177) Wealth concentration is "nearly as great within each age cohort" as overall.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 177) Concentration "is explained mainly by the importance of inherited wealth and its cumulative effects", with extreme returns also playing a role.
- **Unstated premise**: that precaution, life cycle and inheritance (plus returns) exhaust the candidate mechanisms; entrepreneurial wealth creation within a lifetime and heterogeneous saving rates are not considered here. The within-cohort claim, which does the work against the life-cycle explanation, rests on endnote 12, "See the online technical appendix" (p. 455).

**B. Why decile shares beat synthetic indices (pp. 175-176, 192-195)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 192-193) A single index cannot represent a multidimensional distribution; the social and political meaning of inequality differs between bottom, middle and top. (p. 193) Gini mixes labor and capital, whose mechanisms and justifications differ. (p. 193) Tables expressed in money force attention to actual levels and expose data anomalies and incomparabilities. (p. 194) P90/P10 ignores everything above P90, where the top decile's share can range from 20 to 90 percent. (pp. 194-195) P10 thresholds are unstable for measurement reasons while shares are stable.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 195) Distribution tables of shares are "the best tool", better than synthetic indices and interdecile ratios.
- **Note**: the tables still report Gini coefficients (p. 192), and Piketty admits they are "sometimes useful" (p. 192). The argument is for primacy, not exclusion. A share table is also a summary: it compresses a continuous distribution into four or five groups, which endnote 22 concedes biases the Gini downward (p. 456).

**C. Two routes to extreme inequality (pp. 190-192)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 189-190) Total-income inequality depends on labor inequality, capital inequality and the capital share. (p. 191) A top-decile income share near 50 percent can arise when wealth is concentrated at 90/50 and capital income dominates the top (Belle Époque), or when top labor incomes are extreme (US now).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 191) "there are two different ways for a society to achieve a very unequal distribution of total income".
- **Qualification**: (p. 192) The dichotomy is "naïve and overdrawn"; the two combine, and the higher US wealth concentration suggests they already do.

**D. Justification, not magnitude, is the key issue (pp. 190-191)**

- **Premises**: (p. 190) A top-decile share of 90 percent of income would probably provoke revolution without effective repression. (p. 190) Extreme wealth concentration is tolerable if capital income is a small share of income. (pp. 190-191) Whether extreme inequality is sustainable depends "perhaps primarily" on the "apparatus of justification": belief that the rich work harder or that restraining them hurts the poor.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 191) The key issue is justification, so the structure of inequality matters more than its level.
- **Unstated premise**: that sustainability is the relevant test. The argument shows why justification matters causally (what societies tolerate), and then uses it as if it settled what matters normatively. The chapter's normative standard is stated only as democratic modernity's belief that talent-and-effort inequality is "more justified" (p. 174).

**E. The patrimonial middle class (pp. 187-189)**

- **Premises**: (p. 188) In 1900–1910 the top decile owned 90 percent of wealth in France, Britain and Sweden, the top centile over 50 percent (over 60 percent in Britain), the middle 40 percent 5–10 percent. (pp. 188-189) Today the top decile owns about 60 percent in Europe and over 70 percent in the US; the middle 40 percent owns a third in Europe and a quarter in the US; the bottom half still owns about 5 percent.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 188) The growth of a patrimonial middle class was "the principal structural transformation" of twentieth-century wealth distribution; (p. 189) it is "fragile" and the overall reduction smaller than commonly believed.

**Position versus argument.** Piketty insists the class labels "play virtually no role" (p. 179), yet the chapter's central historical claim is about a *middle class* gaining wealth (p. 188), and the rentier/supermanager typology carries strong normative weight. The neutral vocabulary of deciles coexists with an openly normative frame (Vautrin, justification, "chaste veil" of official statistics).
