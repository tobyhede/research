- **Who reads the thesis as inevitability.** Knight states it as inevitability twice over (p. 241). Hayek's own 1933 memo uses inevitably and necessary outcome (pp. 240-241). Chamberlain gives a deterministic end result (p. 245). Marschak instead praises Hayek's contempt for trends and for the love of the inevitable (p. 243). Friedman speaks of predictions about the consequences of collectivism (p. 248) and of two roads (p. 251), a directional claim. Hayek in 1976 denies the any-movement version (fm02, p. 55), and the Conclusion attacks belief in inevitable tendencies (ch16, p. 235). So a sympathetic expert reader in 1943 took the inevitability reading as the plain sense, before any hostile reviewer did.
- **Constructive gap.** Knight (p. 242) and Marschak (p. 243) both say the book is negative and does not draw the line between acceptable and unacceptable state action. Hayek says the same of the book in the Conclusion (ch16, p. 234) and in 1976 (fm02, p. 54). Chamberlain alone offers a list of what the design permits (p. 245).
- **Welfare measures.** Chamberlain says social security cannot be had as a conscious object of policy (p. 244) and that a minimum of compulsory social insurance is compatible with Hayek's design (p. 245). Friedman classes transfer programmes as the new channel of collectivism (p. 250), in line with Hayek's 1976 redefinition of socialism (fm02, p. 55) and harder than Hayek's 1956 view that some welfare aims are laudable (fm02, p. 43).
- **Control of engagements order.** Friedman: the order showed liberal values strong enough that planning was sacrificed (p. 250), and its withdrawal showed a reservoir of popular support for liberal values (p. 252). Hayek 1956: the same order shows the logic of planning forcing coercion (fm02, pp. 45-46), and there was not yet much ground to think Britain had changed course (fm02, p. 46). Friedman's reading matches Hayek's escape clause, not his alarm.
- **Tradition as safeguard.** Friedman credits the British tradition of liberty with stopping planning (p. 250). Hayek 1956 says even a strong tradition of political liberty is no safeguard (fm02, p. 46).
- **German history.** Knight's list of neglected factors (p. 242) is the standing objection to the main historical chapters and to the 1933 memo's single-cause account (pp. 238-239). Hayek's 1956 admission that he chose German illustrations for wartime reasons (fm02, pp. 39-40) does not answer it.
- **The 1933 memo and the book.** The memo already contains the pedigree thesis, the claim that economic collectivism cannot be confined, the sociology-of-knowledge point, and the warning that other nations follow at a distance (pp. 238-241). It is the Beveridge memorandum Hayek names in 1976 as the book's first step (fm02, p. 53; Acknowledgments, p. 256).
- **Publication story, small discrepancies.** Hayek 1956 and Friedman both give three rejections (fm02, p. 40; p. 253). Scoon has an economics department member suggesting the book and a friend in Washington supplying proofs (p. 245), whom Caldwell names as Knight and Director (p. 255); Friedman has Machlup in Washington showing proofs to Director and Knight presenting them (p. 253). The roles overlap but do not match exactly.
- **Audience.** Scoon confirms that the American text was revised to be specific about the United States (p. 246); Hayek 1956 says the book was written with only the British public in mind and reappears unchanged (fm02, p. 44). Both can be true, but the American edition is not simply the English text.
- **Title.** Scoon's Russell derivation (p. 246) is corrected by Caldwell (p. 255) and by Hayek's own 1956 note crediting Tocqueville (fm02, p. 52).
- **Forecasts that failed.** Knight predicted a small market (pp. 242-243); the 1933 memo predicted the radical economic wing would probably prevail (p. 240); Friedman's 1971 pessimism about ideas was partly withdrawn in 1994 (p. 252).
