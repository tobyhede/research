Note 12: self-declared income excludes imputed rent since 1960, so "the 9 percent" appear to have under 10 percent capital income; with imputed rent it is about 20 percent.
- **Sources for France**: income-tax declarations from 1915 (general income tax adopted 15 July 1914) and the *cédulaire* wage tax from 1917; totals from national accounts using Kuznets's method; 1910–1914 completed from pre-war estimates by tax authorities and economists (p. 199; note 4). Estate-tax records since 1791 (p. 199). Detailed series in Piketty, *Les hauts revenus en France* (2001) (note 3). French wartime tax statistics were compiled without interruption and were unusually rich (note 16).
- **Tax-source limits** (pp. 203-205): capital income under-declared through evasion and legal exemption, so top shares are lower bounds; correction for France adds 2–3 points (up to 5) to capital's share, putting the top decile near 50 percent in the Belle Époque and slightly above 35 percent now (p. 204). Corrections are not applied systematically across WTID countries, so series "probably slightly" understate the post-1970 rise (p. 204). Capital gains are excluded in France but measured in the US; including them would raise capital's share at the top ten-thousandth from 60 to 70–80 percent (p. 205). Tax returns say nothing about whether capital was inherited or saved (p. 205).
- **Interwar France**: prices fell 25 percent 1929–1935; private-sector wages fell more than 50 percent; public wage bill flat in nominal terms until 1933; civil-service raises 1927–1931; Laval's 1934–1935 cuts; Matignon Accords and September 1936 devaluation (pp. 206-207). Top decile 47 to 29 percent and top centile 21 to 7 percent across 1914–1945 (note 17).
- **Post-war France**: minimum wage created 1950; Grenelle Accords raised it 20 percent in 1968; partial indexation 1970; minimum-wage purchasing power up over 130 percent 1968–1983 against about 50 percent for the mean wage, versus 25 percent against more than doubling in 1950–1968 (p. 210; note 20 cites Baudelot and Lebeaupin, INSEE 1979). 1982–1983 austerity turn (p. 210).
- **Recent top French salaries**: Landais (2007) and Godechot (2012) (p. 211; note 22).
- **Figure 8.5**: top decile share, US, 1910–2010, with and without capital gains (p. 211). **Figure 8.6**: decomposition of the top decile (top 1, next 4, bottom 5 percent) (p. 212). US federal income tax from 1913; separate wage data only from 1927 (p. 212; notes 23-24).
- **US fractile thresholds 2010** (household income): P90 $108,000; P95 $150,000; P99 $352,000; P99.9 $1.5 million (pp. 215-216; note 29).
- **US correction for capital income**: uncorrected top decile 49.7 percent (2007) and 47.9 percent (2010); correction adds 2–3 points (note 26; the note prints "1007" for 2007).
- **Growth shares 1977–2007**: Atkinson, Piketty and Saez (2011), Table 1 (p. 216; note 33).
- **Wartime wage compression in the US**: National War Labor Board approved raises 1941–1945, mostly for the lowest paid (p. 217); Piketty–Saez (2003), Goldin–Margo (1992) (note 36).
- **Figures 8.7–8.8**: top decile and top centile shares of income versus wages, US (p. 218).
- **Mobility**: Kopczuk, Saez and Song (2010), Social Security earnings data since 1937 (p. 218; note 38).
- **Figures 8.9–8.10**: composition of top US incomes, 1929 and 2007 (p. 219).
- **Who the top earners are**: Kaplan and Rauh (2009) show proxy-statement data on the five highest-paid executives per listed firm cannot account for the rise (p. 219; note 41); Bakija, Cole and Heim (2010) matched tax and compensation records give 60–70 percent managers in the top 0.1 percent (p. 219; note 42).
- **Wolff and Zacharias (2009)**: the top centile is a "cohabitation" of capital-rich and labor-rich; note 39 records Piketty conceding that his 2003 article with Saez overstated the replacement of "coupon-clipping rentiers" by "working rich".
