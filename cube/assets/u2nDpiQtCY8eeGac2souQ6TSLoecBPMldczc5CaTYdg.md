| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capital share (α) | p. 143 | Share of national income going to capital income: the sum of all capital income in the national accounts "regardless of legal classification" (rents, profits, dividends, interest, royalties), pretax, excluding interest on public debt, divided by national income (p. 144; endnote 1, pp. 450-451, says including public-debt interest would add 1–2 points, up to 4–5 in high-debt periods). Linked to β and r by the first fundamental law α = r × β (p. 143). |
| Average rate of return (r) | pp. 143-144 | The same capital-income total divided by the national capital stock (p. 144). An "abstract construct" (p. 143) that averages very different assets; it counts flows of rents, dividends, interest and profits and neglects capital gains and losses, which Piketty argues roughly offset inflation over 1910–2010 (p. 151). Real, not nominal (p. 150). Pretax (p. 149). |
| Pure return on capital | p. 146 | Observed average return minus an estimate of the unpaid "informal" labor of managing one's own portfolio (time, attention), which national accounts omit (p. 146). Estimated as 1–2 points below the observed return and treated as a minimum (p. 147). Defined on p. 148 as the return "after deducting all management costs, including the value of the time spent in portfolio management" (pp. 148-149). |
| Mixed (entrepreneurial) income | p. 145 | Income of nonwage workers in individually owned businesses that combines labor and capital remuneration. Apportioned using the economy-wide capital-labor split (p. 145). About 10 percent of output today, possibly more than half of national income in the eighteenth–nineteenth centuries (p. 145). |
| Real vs nominal assets | p. 150 | Real assets: prices move with the related activity (housing, shares). Nominal assets: fixed nominal value (deposits, unindexed bonds). Real assets are three-quarters to nine-tenths of household assets (p. 151). |
| Marginal productivity of capital | pp. 152-153 | "the additional output due to one additional unit of capital" (pp. 152-153); worked example 100 euros of land or tools adding 5 euros a year, i.e. 5 percent (pp. 153-154). Equal to r under "pure and perfect" competition (pp. 152-153); r may sit above or below it with bargaining power, monopoly or monopsony (pp. 153-154). Defined "independently of the institutions" that split income (p. 155): social return persists even when private return is abolished (Soviet case). |
| Production function | p. 156 | "a mathematical formula reflecting the technological possibilities that exist in a given society" (p. 156). |
| Elasticity of substitution (σ, Piketty uses no symbol) | p. 156 | How easily capital substitutes for labor, or labor for capital, in production (p. 156). σ = 0: fixed coefficients; excess capital has zero marginal product (p. 156). σ infinite: r fixed regardless of the quantity of capital, the "robotized" economy (pp. 156-157). σ < 1: r falls more than proportionally as β rises, so α falls; σ > 1: α rises; σ = 1: α constant (p. 157). |
| Cobb-Douglas production function | p. 157 | The σ = 1 case (Cobb and Douglas, 1928); α is a fixed "purely technological parameter" (p. 157). Endnote 17 gives Y = K^α L^(1−α) and the linear Y = rK + vL for infinite elasticity (p. 452). |
| Volume effect / price effect | p. 160 | Volume effect: more capital (β up) raises α. Price effect: lower r lowers α. When σ > 1 the volume effect wins (p. 160); for any single form of capital such as farmland the price effect eventually wins (p. 161). |
| "Too much capital kills the return on capital" | p. 153 | Diminishing marginal productivity of capital, which Piketty calls "obvious" (p. 155). The question is how fast r falls, not whether it does (p. 155). |
| Engels' pause | p. 163 | Robert Allen's name for the stagnation of British wages c. 1800–1860, when α rose about 10 points, from 35–40 to 45–50 percent (p. 163). |
| Structural growth (g) | p. 164 | "the sum of productivity growth and population growth" (p. 164), which pre-Solow economists including Marx did not clearly identify. |
| Principle of infinite accumulation | p. 164 | Reused from fm01: Marx's mechanism read as β = s/g with g ≈ 0, so β → ∞ and either r → 0 or α devours national income (pp. 164-165). |
| Balanced growth path | p. 168 | The β = s/g path on which capital, income and output grow at the same pace; does "not guarantee a harmonious distribution of wealth" (p. 168). |
