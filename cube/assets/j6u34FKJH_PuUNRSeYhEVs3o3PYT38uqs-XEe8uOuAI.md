What, in the end, has the book shown, how certain is it, and what kind of economics produced it? Piketty restates the whole argument in a few pages: a market economy left to itself has forces of convergence (diffusion of knowledge and skills) and forces of divergence, the principal one being r > g, which he now calls "The Central Contradiction of Capitalism" (p. 426). With frontier growth of 1–1.5% and returns of 4–5%, r > g is likely to become the norm again (p. 427); the remedy is a progressive annual tax on capital, which requires international cooperation and, in Europe, democracy on a European scale (pp. 427-428). He then defends "political and historical economics": economics as a subdiscipline of the social sciences whose distinguishing feature is its normative purpose, sceptical of mathematical modelling and of a "scientistic" use of controlled experiments, and resting on historical experience as "our principal source of knowledge" (pp. 428-430). He ends with the claim that everyone, especially citizens, should take money and its measurement seriously, because "Refusing to deal with numbers rarely serves the interests of the least well-off" (pp. 431-432).
