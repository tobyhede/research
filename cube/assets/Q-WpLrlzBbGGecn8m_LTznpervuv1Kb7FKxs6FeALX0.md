- **A1. Guaranteed income entails direction of labor.**
  **Premises**: (p. 145) where people choose their own occupations, pay must correspond to usefulness to others, not subjective merit + (p. 146) usefulness changes through unforeseeable circumstances, so results cannot always match effort + (pp. 146-147) if losers were protected and gainers stripped of gains, pay would depend on an authority's view of what one ought to have done, and would no longer induce desirable moves + (p. 147) changes in the distribution of labor are constantly necessary in any society + (p. 147) if not brought about by pecuniary rewards and penalties they must be brought about by orders.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 146-147) security of an invariable income for all is possible only by abolishing free choice of employment.
  **Unstated premises**: that the only two allocation devices are price differentials and command (no mixed case such as partial income insurance that dampens but preserves differentials); that the guarantee is of the full former income, not a fraction.

- **A2. Incentives as information.**
  **Premises**: (p. 147) to choose intelligently, a person needs a readily intelligible yardstick of the social importance of occupations + (p. 147) only remuneration that tracks usefulness provides it.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 147) even with the best will in the world, free choice is impossible if rewards bear no relation to usefulness. This is the more important aspect of the incentive problem; motivation is the second (p. 147).
  **Unstated premise**: that market remuneration is a tolerably accurate measure of usefulness to society (argued for elsewhere in the book, assumed here).

- **A3. Discipline under planning.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 147-148) most men need external pressure to give their best + (p. 148) discipline in an enterprise depends on a surrounding unplanned area into which the dismissed worker vanishes (Coyle) + (p. 148) a planned manager bears neither risk nor gain, so he must be judged by rule, and his avoidable error is a crime against the community.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 148) the planned economy's ultimate sanction is the hangman; the manager's security is bought at the price of the safety of freedom and life.

- **A4. The dichotomy.**
  **Premises**: (p. 148) either both choice and risk rest with the individual or he is relieved of both + (p. 148) the army is the closest familiar approach to the second type.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 148) there is no third possibility; full security for all is the security of the barracks. A partially military society understates the unfreedom because a free sphere remains to exit to (p. 149).
  **My observation**: this conclusion sits awkwardly with the chapter's own first four pages, which describe a third thing: individual choice and risk with a floor under the risk. The dichotomy holds only for "full" security; Hayek's wording at p. 148 ("full economic security") preserves consistency, but the "no third possibility" phrasing is stronger than the argument licenses.

- **A5. The ratchet of privilege.**
  **Premises**: (p. 149) guaranteeing some a fixed part of a variable whole makes the remainder fluctuate proportionally more + (pp. 149-150) within a market, incomes can be secured only by restricting output or entry, which closes opportunities to outsiders + (p. 150) as protected trades multiply, those displaced have nowhere to go, so adjustment falls on employment and output rather than prices and wages + (pp. 150-151) the greater the gap between secure and insecure, the more security is prized.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 150-151) the more we try to provide full security by interfering with the market, the greater insecurity becomes, and the demand for security escalates until, as stated earlier, no price including liberty seems too high (p. 146).
  **Unstated premise**: that the process is cumulative and not self-limiting politically; that outsiders respond by demanding inclusion rather than abolition of privilege.

- **A6. The cultural mechanism (Germany).**
  **Premises**: (p. 151) restriction plus socialist disparagement of risk-taking and profit changes values before institutions + (p. 152) where rank comes almost only from salaried state service and independence is precarious and despised, few will prefer freedom.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 152) liberty becomes almost a mockery and people conclude it is not worth having without security; this is how the spirit of freedom was slowly smothered in Germany, and no people can be confident of withstanding the same process (p. 152).
