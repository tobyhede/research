| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| r (rate of return on capital) | pp. 22-23 | "the average annual rate of return on capital, including profits, dividends, interest, rents, and other income from capital, expressed as a percentage of its total value" (pp. 22-23). An average across all asset types and owners; pre-tax at this stage. Defined more fully in ch01 (p. 42). |
| g (growth rate) | p. 23 | "the rate of growth of the economy, that is, the annual increase in income or output" (p. 23). Total (not per capita) growth: population plus per capita output (decomposed in ch02, pp. 55-56). |
| r > g | pp. 22-23 | The "fundamental inequality" and "main force of divergence in my theory" (pp. 23-24). Not a market imperfection: the more perfect the capital market, the more likely it holds (p. 24). More likely when g is low, "though not automatic" (p. 22). |
| Capital/income ratio (β) | p. 17 | Total stock of wealth measured in years of national income (pp. 17, 22). Figure I.2 measures *private* wealth net of debt over national income (p. 22). Formal definition in ch01 (p. 40). |
| α = r × β | p. 29 | Capital's share of national income equals return on capital times the capital/income ratio (p. 29). Presented as elementary; called the "first fundamental law" in ch01 (p. 41). |
| β = s / g | p. 29 | The capital/income ratio equals "in the long run" the savings rate divided by the growth rate (p. 29). Only stated here; derived in ch05. |
| Scarcity principle (Ricardo) | pp. 6-7 | When population and output grow, a fixed-supply asset (land then; urban real estate or oil now) can see its relative price rise for decades, enough to shift the distribution and "destabilize entire societies" (p. 7). Supply and demand do not rule out "a large and lasting divergence" (p. 7). |
| Principle of infinite accumulation (Marx) | p. 9 | "the inexorable tendency for capital to accumulate and become concentrated in ever fewer hands, with no natural limit to the process" (p. 9). Piketty keeps the insight but adds that, with positive but low growth, accumulation stops at a finite but possibly destabilizing level (p. 10). |
| Kuznets curve | p. 13 | Inequality first rises then falls over industrialisation, automatically and everywhere (p. 13). Piketty treats it as a Cold War product built on fragile evidence (pp. 13-14). |
| Balanced growth path (Solow) | p. 11 | A growth trajectory in which output, incomes, profits, wages, capital and asset prices all grow at the same pace, so every group gains equally (p. 11). The book's foil: there is "no fundamental reason" to think growth is automatically balanced (p. 15). |
| Forces of convergence / divergence | pp. 19-20 | Convergence: chiefly the diffusion of knowledge and investment in skills; supply-and-demand and factor mobility are weaker and ambiguous (p. 19). Divergence: (1) top earners separating from the rest; (2) accumulation and concentration when growth is weak and r is high, the more destabilising of the two (pp. 20-21). |
| Diffusion of knowledge | p. 19 | The main force for both productivity growth and reduced inequality, within and between countries; "the public good par excellence—rather than a market mechanism" (p. 19). Depends on education policy and institutions (p. 20). |
| Rising human capital hypothesis | p. 19 | The belief that technology increasingly rewards skill, so labour's share rises and capital's falls. Judged "largely illusory" (p. 20). |
| Generational warfare thesis | p. 20 | The belief that life-cycle saving has replaced class conflict over inherited wealth. Also judged largely illusory: wealth inequality is mainly within age cohorts (p. 20). |
| Income from labor / income from capital | p. 16 | Labour income: wages, salaries, bonuses, non-wage labour earnings. Capital income: rent, dividends, interest, profits, capital gains, royalties, and other income from "the mere fact of owning capital", regardless of legal classification (p. 16). |
| Structure of inequality | p. 17 | Not only the level of inequality but its origins (inheritance vs saving, labour vs capital) and the justifications offered for it (p. 17). |
| World Top Incomes Database (WTID) | p. 16 | The joint database of about thirty researchers built from income tax data plus national accounts; "the primary source of data for this book" (p. 16). |
| Representative-agent models | p. 15 | Models in which every agent has the same wage and wealth, so growth benefits all by definition; blamed for economists' neglect of distribution (p. 15). |
| Common utility | p. 27 | The 1789 Declaration's standard, adopted provisionally as the test of justified inequality: social distinctions "founded only upon common utility" (p. 27). Piketty calls it "imprecise but seductive" (p. 27). |
