- **Self-description as critical.** The Conclusion's admission that the book is essentially critical (p. 234) is the passage Marschak points to in his reader's report (ap01, p. 243), matches Knight's complaint that it hardly considers alternatives (ap01, p. 242), and is conceded again by Hayek in 1976 (fm02, p. 54).
- **Against inevitability.** The Conclusion attacks belief in inevitable tendencies and the idea that past policy was inevitable (p. 235). This supports Hayek's 1976 denial that he argued inevitability (fm02, p. 55) and sits awkwardly with his own 1956 phrase about the inevitable consequences of socialist planning (fm02, p. 48) and Knight's summary of the thesis (ap01, p. 241). My observation: Hayek denies historical inevitability of the trend while asserting a necessary connection between a policy and its results; the two uses of the word should be kept apart in synthesis.
- **Not a return to the past.** The disclaimer about the reality of the nineteenth century (p. 235) is the book's answer to the charge of nostalgia or laissez-faire; compare Chamberlain's remark that Hayek is no devotee of laissez faire (ap01, p. 245).
- **Chapter 15.** The one exception to the critical task is international order (p. 234), which refers back to the federation proposals of the preceding chapter.
- **Principles, not a programme.** The 1956 foreword says the tests for reform are in the Rule of Law chapter (fm02, p. 43); the Conclusion names no such test and speaks only of certain principles (p. 235).
- **Bibliography as a map for cross-book work.** Mises's Socialism is ranked first (p. 236); Lippmann, Polanyi, Robbins, Roepke and Simons are the constructive supplements. Simons's title, A Positive Program for Laissez Faire (p. 237), names what this book declines to give.
- **Canon.** The closing list of Tocqueville, Acton, Constant, Burke and The Federalist (p. 237) matches the epigraphs and the 1956 use of Tocqueville and Acton (fm02, pp. 37, 41, 47). Burke's presence is worth noting against the 1956 distinction between liberalism and conservatism (fm02, p. 44).
