- The progressive income tax was the major twentieth-century fiscal innovation and helped reduce inequality; it is now threatened by international tax competition and by never having had its foundations "clearly thought through", since it was adopted in an emergency (p. 360). The same is true of the progressive estate tax (p. 360).
- Taxation is "preeminently a political and philosophical issue"; every major political upheaval has a fiscal revolution at its heart (p. 360).
- In modern fiscal states total tax payments are close to proportional to income for most people; financing half of national income requires everyone to contribute (p. 362).
- Progressivity at the very top still matters: progressive taxation of top incomes and estates "partly explains" why wealth concentration never regained Belle Époque levels after 1914–1945, and the post-1980 fall in US and UK progressivity "probably explains much" of the rise of the highest earned incomes (p. 362).
- Tax competition has led governments, especially in Europe, to exempt capital income, producing a race to the bottom (p. 362); in most countries taxes have become or will become regressive at the top (p. 362).
- A system regressive at the top risks "fiscal secession" by the wealthy and erodes the middle class's fiscal consent; the social state needs at least a minimum of progressivity (p. 363).
- Measuring progressivity by income alone ignores lightly taxed inheritance; by lifetime resources the system is more regressive still (p. 363).
- Globalization hurts the least skilled in rich countries; a more progressive system could improve their position, and without it the losers from free trade may turn against it (pp. 363-364).
- Progressive taxation was "as much a product of two world wars as it was of democracy" (p. 364). Before 1914 top rates were everywhere tiny (below 10%, generally below 5%), similar to eighteenth- and nineteenth-century rates (pp. 365-366).
- The French Third Republic, despite universal male suffrage and an egalitarian self-image, refused full progressivity for nearly half a century; the 1901 estate tax reform barely touched accumulation (pp. 367-368).
- Opponents such as Leroy-Beaulieu argued, without data, that France was already egalitarian and needed no progressive tax; the 1901 probate statistics showed French wealth nearly as concentrated as British (pp. 368-369). Even a low-rate tax produces knowledge and transparency (p. 369).
- The United States, followed by Britain, invented confiscatory top rates: first above 70% on income (1919–1922) and estates (1937–1939) (p. 370). The aim was to end incomes judged "socially unacceptable and economically unproductive", not revenue (p. 370).
- The progressive tax is "a relatively liberal method for reducing inequality": it respects competition and private property while changing incentives under democratically debated rules (p. 370). Continental Europe used nationalization and direct salary-setting as partial substitutes (p. 370).
- US top federal income tax rate averaged 81% over 1932–1980 (p. 371); no continental European country sustained such rates (pp. 371-372). Top US estate rates stayed at 70–80% from the 1930s to the 1980s (p. 372); Britain reached 98% on top incomes and estates in the 1940s and 1970s (p. 372).
- Top rates applied to very few people: thresholds equivalent to 500,000–1 million euros in 2000–2010 terms, i.e., 0.1–0.5% of the population (p. 372).
- After 1980 US and UK top rates fell from 80–90% to 30–40% (28% after 1986), while France and Germany stayed at 50–60% (p. 373). Part of the reason was the sense of being overtaken in the 1970s, though the catch-up was "largely a mechanical consequence" of the 1914–1945 shocks to Europe and Japan (p. 373).
- Across developed countries, the size of the fall in top marginal rates since 1980 is "perfectly correlated" with the rise in the top centile's income share (p. 373).
- The marginal-productivity explanation of this correlation is implausible (pp. 373-374); the bargaining explanation fits better (p. 374).
- There is no statistically significant relation between the fall in top rates and productivity growth since 1980; per capita GDP growth has been about the same in all rich countries (p. 374).
- Piketty, Saez and Stantcheva's executive-pay data favour the bargaining model over managerial productivity (p. 375). Executive pay responds more to luck than to talent, the luck elasticity is higher where top rates are lower, and firm size, finance, or weak competition do not explain the cross-country pattern (p. 376). Only dissuasive taxation of the pre-1980 kind "can do the job" (p. 376).
- The optimal top rate in developed countries is "probably above 80 percent" for the top 1% or 0.5%, but no formula can settle the rate; only "collective deliberation and democratic experimentation" can (p. 376).
- An 80% rate on incomes over $500,000 or $1 million would not reduce US growth and would yield little revenue because it would reduce such pay; revenue would need rates of 50–60% on incomes above $200,000 (pp. 376-377).
- The United States is big enough to do this alone; small European countries without coordination are not (pp. 376-377).
- Adoption is unlikely; the US political process may have been captured by the 1%, though Piketty prefers to credit ideas and better (tax) data (p. 377). Since war, not universal suffrage, created progressive taxation, there is "little reason for optimism" (p. 377), and the US risks becoming the Old Europe of the twenty-first century (p. 378).
