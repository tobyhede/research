| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proportional tax (flat tax) | p. 362 | A tax whose rate "is the same for everyone" (p. 362). |
| Progressive tax | p. 362 | A tax whose rate is higher for those who earn, own, or consume more (p. 362). Defined by the rate, not by the amount paid. |
| Regressive tax | p. 362 | A tax whose rate falls for richer people, either through legal or illegal exemption (optimization, evasion) or because the schedule itself is regressive; Thatcher's poll tax is the example (p. 362). Piketty's note 3 explains the poll tax was a flat per-adult payment, so its rate was lower for the rich (p. 489). |
| Taxes on income / capital / consumption | p. 361 | The standard tripartite classification, which Piketty calls ambiguous: the income tax also taxes capital income; capital taxes include levies on capital income flows (corporate tax) and on the stock (real estate, estate, wealth taxes); consumption taxes are "indirect" because paid as part of the price (p. 361). |
| Social contributions | p. 361 | A "fourth category" that appeared in the twentieth century: a special tax on (usually labour) income earmarked for social insurance funds (p. 361). The legal categories are "partly arbitrary" (p. 362). |
| Top (marginal) rate | p. 365 | "the tax rate on the highest income bracket" (p. 365). Piketty's note 14 adds that it applies only to income above a threshold, usually to under 1% of the population, and gives only an upper bound on the effective rate of the wealthiest (p. 489). |
| Fiscal secession | p. 363 | The wealthiest escaping progressive taxation so that the system becomes regressive at the top; threatens "fiscal consent" (p. 363). |
| Fiscal consent | p. 363 | The general willingness, especially of the middle class, to pay for the fiscal and social state; undermined when the top pays less than the middle (p. 363). |
| Rastignac's dilemma | p. 363 | Reused from ch11: inherited wealth is lightly taxed relative to labour income, so ranking people by lifetime resources (earned income plus capitalised inheritance), which he calls "a more satisfactory criterion" for progressivity, would show the system even more regressive at the top (p. 363). |
| Overall (total) income | p. 364 | The base of the early progressive income tax: the sum of labour income (wage and nonwage) and all capital income (rent, interest, dividends, profits, sometimes capital gains) (p. 364). |
| Categorical / schedular tax | p. 366 | Pre-progressive income taxes applying separate, usually flat, rates of 5–10% to each category ("schedule") of income; Britain from 1842 until the 1909 "supertax" (p. 366). |
| Indicial tax system | p. 367 | The French revolutionary system of taxing on external indices of ability to pay (doors and windows, rental value) rather than declared income (p. 367). |
| Confiscatory tax on "excessive" incomes | p. 370 | Rates of 70–80% or more whose primary goal is not revenue but "to put an end to such incomes and large estates" or make them very costly; an "American invention" (p. 370). |
| Earned / unearned income | p. 372 | Anglo-American distinction between labour income and capital income; the top rates in Figure 14.1 applied to unearned income, which was taxed more heavily (p. 372). |
| Bargaining power explanation | p. 374 | Piketty's account of rising top pay: lower top rates raise executives' payoff from pushing boards for raises, and because individual contributions are hard to measure they succeed (pp. 374-376). Set against the marginal productivity / labor supply explanation (pp. 373-374). |
| Elasticity with respect to "luck" vs "talent" | p. 376 | Sensitivity of executive pay to firm performance explained by sector-wide movements ("luck") versus performance not explained by sector variables ("talent") (p. 376). |
| Optimal top tax rate | p. 376 | The rate Piketty, Saez and Stantcheva estimate for the top 1% or 0.5%: "probably above 80 percent" (p. 376); Piketty's note 50 gives 82% (p. 494). He disclaims the precision. |
