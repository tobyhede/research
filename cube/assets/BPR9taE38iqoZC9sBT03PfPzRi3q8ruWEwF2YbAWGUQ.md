Do the British and French patterns of ch03 hold elsewhere, and what explains the twentieth-century collapse of the capital/income ratio? Piketty extends the series to Germany (from 1870), the United States (from 1770) and Canada (from 1860). Germany repeats the European U-curve and public-private story, with lower private wealth today that he attributes to low stock-market valuation under "Rhenish capitalism". The 1914–1945 collapse in Europe was mainly a matter of lost foreign assets, low saving and low asset prices, and only to a small extent physical destruction. The United States had a lower and more stable ratio, because land was abundant and cheap and immigrants arrived without capital, *unless* slaves are counted as capital, in which case the antebellum South held more wealth relative to income than old Europe. The chapter ends by arguing that putting a market value on "human capital" makes sense only in a slave society.
