| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic security (general) | p. 143 | A vague, ambiguous term; in one sense a real condition of liberty (independence of mind is rare among those unsure of making their way), in another, "too absolute", sense the gravest threat to it (pp. 143-144). |
| Limited security (first kind) | p. 144 | Security against severe physical privation: a given minimum of sustenance for all; briefly, security of a minimum income. Achievable for all, hence no privilege; provided outside of and supplementary to the market (p. 144). |
| Absolute security (second kind) | p. 144 | Security of a given standard of life or of one's relative position; the particular income a person is thought to deserve. Cannot be given to all in a free society; providable only for some and only by controlling or abolishing the market (p. 144). Later glossed as protection against undeserved diminutions of income (p. 145) and as the "security of the barracks" when generalized (p. 148). |
| Privilege | p. 144 | A benefit that cannot be had by all and is granted to some at the expense of others whose security is thereby diminished (pp. 144, 146). Security of the second kind is a privilege by definition; the first kind is not because it is universal. |
| Just remuneration | p. 145 | Pay commensurate with subjective merit (effort, intention) rather than with the objective results, i.e. usefulness to others. The demand for absolute security is another form of this demand (p. 145). |
| Planning "in the good sense" | p. 145 | Planning that does not replace the market, e.g. monetary policy against fluctuations; contrasted with the special kind of planning meant to supersede the market (p. 145). |
| Restrictionism / "control" | p. 149 | Limitation of output or entry so that prices secure an adequate return to insiders; the only way a market economy can guarantee incomes to particular producers; said to cover almost all planning actually practiced (p. 149). |
| Commercial vs. military type of society | p. 148 | Two irreconcilable types of organization: either choice and risk both rest with the individual, or he is relieved of both. No third possibility (p. 148). |
| Beamtenstaat | p. 152 | Germany as a state of appointed functionaries where income and status in almost all spheres are assigned and guaranteed by authority (p. 152). Editor glosses as civil service state or, pejoratively, bureaucratic state (p. 153, note 8). |
| Freedom / liberty | p. 143 | Used here chiefly as freedom to choose one's employment and bear its risk (pp. 145-148); also independence as a social value opposed to salaried security (p. 151). It has a price in material sacrifice (p. 153). |
| Incentives (two senses) | p. 147 | (a) willingness to do one's best; (b) more important, the informational yardstick by which an individual can judge the social importance of occupations (p. 147). |
