- **Against technological determinism of monopoly (empirical).**
  **Premises**: (p. 89) the scale argument predicts large firms driving out small in most industries; (p. 89) the TNEC final report and the Wilcox monograph find the superior efficiency of large establishments undemonstrated and monopoly often due to collusion and public policy; (p. 89) monopolists regularly rely on state help; (unstated) what holds for American industry around 1940 holds generally, and (p. 89) 'an investigation of conditions in England would lead to very similar results', asserted without the investigation.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 89) there is nothing inevitable about the growth of monopoly.

- **Argument from historical order.**
  **Premises**: (p. 90) if monopoly came from technology or the evolution of capitalism it would appear first in the most advanced economy; (p. 90) it appeared first in the then young industrial countries, the United States and Germany, both protectionist; (p. 90) in Britain it grew fast only after protection in 1931; (p. 90) it is absurd to suppose technological necessities that operated in Germany in the 1880s reached Britain only in the 1930s; (unstated) Britain was the most advanced economy throughout, and no difference other than policy explains the timing.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 90-91) monopoly follows policy, and policy follows ideas; 'nothing is inevitable but thinking makes it so'.

- **The complexity (knowledge) argument: the chapter's main positive argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 91) central direction requires that one person or board survey all the relevant facts; (p. 91) in a complex division of labour the factors are too numerous for any synoptic view; (pp. 91-92) therefore decisions must be decentralised to agents who adjust to facts only they can know; (p. 92) decentralised decisions still have to be mutually adjusted; (p. 92) no centre can know, or collect and disseminate fast enough, the details of constantly changing supply and demand; (p. 92) competitive prices automatically record the effects of individual actions and guide each decision; (unstated) the knowledge in question cannot be gathered or computed centrally in principle, not just with present means; (unstated) actual prices under actual competition transmit that information well enough to coordinate.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 91-92) the more complex the economy, the more competition is the only adequate method of coordination and the less central planning can work; complexity is an argument against planning.

- **The fork on the planners' argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 88) one version says technology destroys competition; (pp. 92-93) another says technology cannot be used unless competition is suspended; Hayek notes these are 'almost opposite'.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 88) (p. 93) neither shows necessity; at most the second shows a choice.

- **Freedom over calculable gain.**
  **Premises**: (p. 93) in some cases compulsory uniformity might make everyone better off by present lights; (p. 93) enforcing it makes future development depend on the knowledge particular people now possess; (p. 94) we cannot predict which variant will lead to something better; (p. 94) long-run material progress depends on variety; (unstated) the expected long-run gain from open-ended variety outweighs the forgone present gain, or else freedom is to be preferred even where it does not.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 94) the case for freedom applies even where compulsion would seem to bring only advantages and may in the instance do no harm. Hayek explicitly declines to claim the sacrifice will be repaid in every case (p. 94), so the conclusion rests on a rule-level, not case-level, justification.

- **The specialist argument (why planning is popular and why that is no recommendation).**
  **Premises**: (p. 94) any one technical ideal could be realised if made the sole aim; (p. 94) they cannot all be realised at once, and seeing this requires stepping outside one's specialism; (pp. 95-96) each enthusiast expects the planners to share his own priorities; (p. 96) the aims conflict; (unstated) there is no agreed scale of values by which a planning authority could rank the aims.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 96) the coalition for planning rests on an illusion that dissolves as soon as planning is adopted, and rule by the best specialists would be unbearable and irrational. The unstated premise is the explicit subject of ch. 5.
