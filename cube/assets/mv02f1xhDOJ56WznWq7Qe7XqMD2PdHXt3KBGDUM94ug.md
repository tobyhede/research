**A. Progressivity at the top is necessary for the social state (pp. 362-364)**

- **Premises**: (p. 362) The modern fiscal state is roughly proportional for most people. (pp. 362-363) Capital income escapes progressive taxation through tax competition, so the top centiles pay lower overall rates (France 2010: 35% for the top 0.1% against 40–50% for the rest). (p. 363) A middle class paying more than the rich will withdraw consent: "why continue to pay for others?"
- **Conclusion**: (p. 363) The social state requires that the tax system "retain a minimum of progressivity, or at any rate that it not become overtly regressive at the top".
- **Unstated premise**: that perceived fairness of the overall schedule, rather than the services received, drives the middle class's fiscal consent. The claim about consent is asserted, not evidenced.

**B. Progressive taxation was a product of war, not of democracy (pp. 364-370)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 364-366) Progressive income taxes existed before 1914 in many countries but with top rates below 10%. (pp. 365-366) Top rates jumped only after the war (France 2% to 50% in 1920; Prussia 4% to 40%; US to 77%; UK 8% to over 40%). (p. 365) The French law of 1920 was passed by the most right-wing Chamber, whose members had opposed the 2% rate. (p. 367) The Third Republic had universal male suffrage for decades without embracing progressivity. (p. 369) The German estate tax stalemate of 1906–1909 was broken only in 1919.
- **Conclusion**: Progressive taxation was "as much a product of two world wars as it was of democracy" (p. 364); and later, "It was war that gave rise to progressive taxation, not the natural consequences of universal suffrage." (p. 377)
- **Note**: the conclusion hardens across the chapter. On p. 364 war and democracy are co-causes and "a movement had clearly been launched" before the war (p. 366); by p. 377 war is the cause. Piketty's note 26 cites Scheve and Stasavage (2012) on war and inheritance taxation as support (p. 491).

**C. Confiscatory rates as a liberal instrument (p. 370)**

- **Premises**: (p. 370) Rates of 70–80% raise little revenue, so their purpose is to end or discourage very high incomes and estates. (p. 370) They involve no prohibition or expropriation and are set by democratic rules.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 370) The progressive tax is "a relatively liberal method for reducing inequality" and "an ideal compromise between social justice and individual freedom".
- **Unstated premise**: that discouraging an income through price (tax) is categorically different from prohibiting it, even at rates where the effect is the same. He signals this by calling the US invention "confiscatory" while classing it as liberal.

**D. Falling top rates caused rising top pay through bargaining, not productivity (pp. 373-376)**

- **Premises**: (p. 373) Across developed countries the fall in top rates since 1980 is "perfectly correlated" with the rise in the top centile's share. (pp. 373-374) The marginal-productivity story (lower rates spur effort, raising productivity and pay) runs into the difficulties shown in ch09. (p. 374) If productivity had risen, growth should differ; but there is no statistically significant relation between rate cuts and per capita growth, and the US and UK have not grown faster than Germany, France, Japan, Denmark, or Sweden since 1980. (p. 374) When 80–90% of a raise went to the state, executives had little reason to fight for it; after 1980 they did, and individual contributions are hard to measure while compensation committees are "incestuous". (p. 376) Pay responds more to luck than to talent, and more so where top rates are low.
- **Conclusion**: Rising executive pay is "fairly well explained by the bargaining model" (p. 375) and "does not have much to do with a hypothetical increase in managerial productivity" (p. 375); only dissuasive taxation can stop it (p. 376).
- **Unstated premises**: that the direction of causation runs from rates to pay. Piketty himself raises political capture by the 1% (p. 377), which would run the other way (rising top incomes buying lower rates). The macro growth test assumes that a productivity effect at the top would be large enough to show up in national growth rates.

**E. Optimal top rate above 80% (pp. 376-377)**

- **Premises**: (p. 376) Bargaining, not productivity, drives top pay, so confiscatory rates reduce "economically useless (or even harmful)" pay without reducing output (p. 376). Piketty's note 40 says the labor supply elasticity can be ruled out above 0.1–0.2 (p. 493). (p. 377) Executives will not flee en masse; historical and firm-level data contradict it.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 376) The optimal top rate is "probably above 80 percent" on the top 1% or 0.5% (82% in Piketty's note 50) (p. 494); (p. 377) an 80% rate on incomes above $500,000 or $1 million in the US, plus 50–60% above $200,000 for revenue.
- **Design**: Piketty's note 51 separates two functions: confiscatory 80–90% rates on the top 0.5–1% "would end indecent and useless compensation", while 50–60% rates on the top 5–10% raise revenue (p. 494).
- **Qualification**: (p. 376) the estimate's precision is not to be trusted; rates are for democratic deliberation; social norms may influence pay independently of tax.

**Position versus argument.** The historical record (when and how rates rose and fell) is well documented and separable from the policy claim. The policy claim rests almost entirely on the Piketty-Saez-Stantcheva paper (cited as forthcoming), summarised in a few sentences; the reader of this chapter cannot evaluate its identification strategy.
