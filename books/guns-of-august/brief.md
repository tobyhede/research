# The Guns of August

## Header

- **Title and author:** The Guns of August, by Barbara W. Tuchman (1962), with a foreword by Robert K. Massie and the author's Preface to the later anniversary edition.
- **Source:** `The Guns of August (Barbara W. Tuchman).epub`
- **Coverage:** 39 of 41 spine entries were extracted. Two had no extractable text and are not covered: spine 3 (title page) and spine 11 (an unnamed front-matter page, `col4`); both are probably image-only, which was not confirmed. All narrative text was read: the Foreword, Preface, Author's Note, chapters 1 to 22 and the Afterword (about 185,000 words). The Sources and Notes (about 20,000 words) were sampled and tallied by script, not read. Maps and illustrations are not in the extracted text. Chapters 11 and 12 were read by a delegated reader, not by the writer of this brief; chapters 15 to 22 were read out of order.
- **Locators:** this EPUB has no stable page numbers. Citations are line numbers in the extracted source text, which opens beside the report in the HTML version.
- **Reading mode:** rapid understanding.
- **Confidence:** high for what the book argues, because every chapter was read and all ten quotations were checked mechanically against the source. Medium for the chapter scores and for judgments about her sources, which are made without having read the endnotes.

## 30-second brief

- **Problem:** why the war of 1914 turned into a deadlock within a month. General staffs planned for a short war even when their commanders foresaw a long one.
- **Thesis:** each power executed a rigid plan built on false assumptions about the others. Every offensive failed, so no one got a decisive battle, and the Marne settled only that the war would go on.
- **Who benefits:** readers who want the opening of the war as a story of decisions, and anyone interested in how institutions follow a plan past the evidence.
- **Worth reading?** Yes. The argument compresses well; the scenes and portraits that make it persuasive do not. Eight chapters and the Afterword carry it.

### Three things to remember

1. A plan that cannot be changed stops being an instrument of policy and becomes the policy.
2. The staffs had the evidence. They read it through what they wanted to do.
3. Her claim is about duration: the first month decided that the war would be long, in the field and in people's minds.

### What the book changes

It replaces the picture of a war that simply broke out with one of a war whose opening was written in advance by staffs that could not revise, and whose continuation was fixed by the way it was begun. The reader comes away watching for the moment when someone says a thing cannot be altered, and asking whether that is true. It also leaves a caution about its own method: nearly every witness was defending himself, and the author says so.

## 10-minute deep brief

### 1. The plans governed the men

- **Author's claim:** as the crisis peaked, heads of state tried to draw back and the staffs' timetables pulled them on. [Source: Outbreak, [line 748](./source/source.txt#L748)] On 1 August the Kaiser, offered a hope of French neutrality, ordered the army east; Moltke refused. [Source: Ch 6, [line 790](./source/source.txt#L790), [line 800](./source/source.txt#L800)]
- **Reasoning:** German mobilisation was one interlocking railway movement of some 11,000 trains; Moltke foresaw chaos if it were reversed. [Source: Ch 6, [line 798](./source/source.txt#L798)]
- **Evidence:** the Staff kept an eastern deployment plan current until 1913; the head of the railway section later showed four armies could have gone east by 15 August; Moltke is reported as admitting the attack on France was a mistake. [Source: Ch 6, [line 802](./source/source.txt#L802), [line 806](./source/source.txt#L806)] She generalises the point. [Source: Ch 11, [line 1480](./source/source.txt#L1480)]
- **Boundary:** the claim that schedules dragged every government forward is shown for Germany only. Austria and Serbia are left out by design. [Source: Author's Note, [line 154](./source/source.txt#L154)] She doubts herself whether Germans could have held back from France. [Source: Ch 6, [line 828](./source/source.txt#L828)]
- **Application:** [Application] when told a commitment cannot be reversed, ask who checked, and whether an alternative was ever kept ready.

### 2. Germany's plan required Belgium

- **Author's claim:** Schlieffen wanted a quick annihilating victory over France before Russia could mobilise, which meant envelopment, which meant Belgium. The Staff treated military necessity as the only test. [Source: Ch 2, [line 402](./source/source.txt#L402), [line 416](./source/source.txt#L416)]
- **Reasoning:** French frontier forts ruled out a frontal attack and only the Belgian plain had room for millions of men. [Source: Ch 2, [line 406](./source/source.txt#L406)] The Germans expected Belgium to protest and stand aside. [Source: Ch 2, [line 430](./source/source.txt#L430)]
- **Evidence:** the ultimatum was drafted by Moltke on 26 July. [Source: Ch 8, [line 984](./source/source.txt#L984)] Belgium refused within hours. [Source: Ch 8, [line 1040](./source/source.txt#L1040)] The General Staff had counted on British entry and discounted it. [Source: Ch 9, [line 1130](./source/source.txt#L1130)] The Afterword calls Belgium the first and decisive if. [Source: Afterword, [line 3200](./source/source.txt#L3200)]
- **Boundary:** she also explains the decision by a century of German philosophy. [Source: Ch 2, [line 418](./source/source.txt#L418)] [Analysis] That passage has no documentary base comparable to the military account beside it.
- **Application:** [Application] price the political cost of a technically optimal route before it is the only route left.

### 3. France's plan refused the evidence

- **Author's claim:** the French Staff knew a German sweep through Belgium was possible and concluded that a strong German right meant a weak centre. [Source: Ch 3, [line 468](./source/source.txt#L468)] The authors of Plan 17 rejected evidence that argued for the defensive. [Source: Ch 3, [line 574](./source/source.txt#L574)]
- **Reasoning:** the offensive doctrine answered a weaker nation's need for confidence after 1870; and the army held that reservists were useless, so it assumed Germany would not put them in the line. [Source: Ch 3, [line 484](./source/source.txt#L484), [line 520](./source/source.txt#L520)]
- **Evidence:** General Michel's accurate 1911 forecast and his dismissal. [Source: Ch 3, [line 510](./source/source.txt#L510), [line 528](./source/source.txt#L528)] In the field, Lanrezac's warnings were answered with "so much the better". [Source: Ch 13, [line 1734](./source/source.txt#L1734)] On 24 August intelligence found reserve corps marching behind active corps. [Source: Ch 14, [line 2072](./source/source.txt#L2072)]
- **Boundary:** [Analysis] the overruled prophets are heard largely through their own later accounts. Michel's defensive plan was never tested.
- **Application:** [Application] be wary when every adverse report is welcomed as good news for the plan.

### 4. Everyone planned for a short war

- **Author's claim:** both Moltkes and Joffre foresaw a long war, and no staff planned for one. [Source: Ch 2, [line 422](./source/source.txt#L422); Ch 9, [line 1140](./source/source.txt#L1140)]
- **Reasoning:** a long war could not be planned scientifically and a short one could; economists and bankers agreed a long war was impossible. [Source: Ch 9, [line 1132](./source/source.txt#L1132)]
- **Evidence:** Germany entered with nitrates for six months; France left its Lorraine iron basin undefended. [Source: Ch 9, [line 1138](./source/source.txt#L1138)] Kitchener alone prepared for years of war. [Source: Ch 9, [line 1142](./source/source.txt#L1142)] The same belief kept the German fleet in port. [Source: Ch 18, [line 2490](./source/source.txt#L2490)]
- **Boundary:** [Analysis] the claims about the Lorraine ore are made in a sentence each.
- **Application:** [Application] plan for the outcome you privately expect, not only the one that is easy to plan.

### 5. The German right wing was whittled away

- **Author's claim:** by 28 August the armies meant to envelop the French were five corps short, the equivalent of a field army. [Source: Ch 19, [line 2684](./source/source.txt#L2684)]
- **Reasoning:** Moltke had moved strength left before the war; in August he let the left attack, committed it against the French fortresses and would not recall it. [Source: Ch 2, [line 448](./source/source.txt#L448); Ch 14, [line 1910](./source/source.txt#L1910); Ch 21, [line 2900](./source/source.txt#L2900)] Corps were left to watch Antwerp and the fortresses, and two were sent to East Prussia when OHL thought the West already won. [Source: Ch 16, [line 2260](./source/source.txt#L2260), [line 2262](./source/source.txt#L2262)]
- **Evidence:** Ludendorff said the eastern reinforcements were not needed. [Source: Ch 16, [line 2258](./source/source.txt#L2258)] Kluck, without reinforcements and believing the French beaten, turned southeast inside Paris. [Source: Ch 21, [line 2888](./source/source.txt#L2888), [line 2902](./source/source.txt#L2902)] Moltke's halt order of 4 September admitted the envelopment had failed. [Source: Ch 22, [line 3090](./source/source.txt#L3090)]
- **Boundary:** [Analysis] she faults the plan for rigidity, Moltke for departing from it, and Kluck for using his latitude. These are different diagnoses and the book does not choose. Whether two more corps would have changed the Marne is her inference.
- **Application:** [Application] a half-change to a single-minded plan can be worse than keeping it or replacing it.

### 6. The offensive broke; the Allies survived by leaving the plan

- **Author's claim:** from 20 to 24 August the French attacked in Lorraine, the Ardennes and on the Sambre and were beaten everywhere. [Source: Ch 14, [line 1886](./source/source.txt#L1886), [line 1894](./source/source.txt#L1894)]
- **Reasoning:** regulations assumed defenders needed twenty seconds to fire; machine guns needed eight. The French did not entrench and attacked without artillery preparation. [Source: Ch 14, [line 1888](./source/source.txt#L1888), [line 1934](./source/source.txt#L1934)]
- **Evidence:** Germany had 30 divisions west of the Meuse where the French counted 17 or 18; French casualties exceeded 140,000 in four days. [Source: Ch 14, [line 1968](./source/source.txt#L1968), [line 2058](./source/source.txt#L2058)] Lanrezac's unauthorised retreat saved his army; Smith-Dorrien's stand at Le Cateau saved the British left; the armies on the Moselle held. [Source: Ch 14, [line 2018](./source/source.txt#L2018); Ch 19, [line 2658](./source/source.txt#L2658), [line 2698](./source/source.txt#L2698)] The French retreated beaten and unbroken. [Source: Ch 19, [line 2560](./source/source.txt#L2560)]
- **Boundary:** she rates Mons as brave and small, and the idea that the BEF bore the weight of the blow as a myth. [Source: Ch 14, [line 2058](./source/source.txt#L2058); Ch 20, [line 2840](./source/source.txt#L2840)] [Analysis] Joffre is blamed for the plan and credited with the calm that saved the army; both come from the same refusal to doubt. [Source: Ch 20, [line 2800](./source/source.txt#L2800)]
- **Application:** [Application] separate failures of execution from failures of premise before assigning blame.

### 7. Terror made the war impossible to end

- **Author's claim:** German doctrine held that pressure on civilians shortens war; applied in Belgium it did the opposite. [Source: Ch 17, [line 2378](./source/source.txt#L2378)]
- **Reasoning:** reprisals were prepared in advance to save time and occupation troops. [Source: Ch 13, [line 1858](./source/source.txt#L1858); Ch 17, [line 2406](./source/source.txt#L2406)] Louvain was burned as a deterrent and announced as such. [Source: Ch 17, [line 2412](./source/source.txt#L2412), [line 2422](./source/source.txt#L2422)]
- **Evidence:** the shootings at Andenne, Tamines and Dinant, from German placards, gravestones and the commanders' memoirs. [Source: Ch 17, [line 2380](./source/source.txt#L2380), [line 2382](./source/source.txt#L2382), [line 2390](./source/source.txt#L2390)] The Pact of London and the war aims of both sides followed within the month. [Source: Ch 17, [line 2432](./source/source.txt#L2432), [line 2438](./source/source.txt#L2438)] Louvain turned Wilson against Germany. [Source: Ch 18, [line 2528](./source/source.txt#L2528)]
- **Boundary:** [Analysis] she explains German conduct both as cold doctrine and as panic and national psychology. Allied aims get a paragraph beside a page on the German ones. [Source: Ch 17, [line 2440](./source/source.txt#L2440)]
- **Application:** [Application] coercion intended to end resistance quickly should be judged by what it does to the will of everyone watching.

### 8. Britain was committed without deciding

- **Author's claim:** the joint plans were a continuation of policy whatever ministers said. [Source: Ch 7, [line 946](./source/source.txt#L946)]
- **Reasoning:** the BEF's place on the French left was fixed to the day, and the French fleet had moved on the strength of a British undertaking. [Source: Ch 4, [line 638](./source/source.txt#L638), [line 654](./source/source.txt#L654)]
- **Evidence:** twelve of eighteen ministers opposed supporting France on 1 August; only Belgium united the country. [Source: Ch 7, [line 908](./source/source.txt#L908); Ch 9, [line 1096](./source/source.txt#L1096)] The force went late, at four divisions, under instructions to support the French, avoid losses and stay independent. [Source: Ch 12, [line 1680](./source/source.txt#L1680), [line 1706](./source/source.txt#L1706)] On 30 August its commander told Kitchener he would retire behind the Seine; Kitchener went to Paris to stop him, and Joffre had to plead again on 5 September. [Source: Ch 20, [line 2852](./source/source.txt#L2852), [line 2872](./source/source.txt#L2872); Ch 22, [line 3176](./source/source.txt#L3176)]
- **Boundary:** [Analysis] the planning is seen mainly through Henry Wilson's diary. The case against Sir John French is made from his own letters; the motives she gives him are inference.
- **Application:** [Application] an arrangement others have relied on is a commitment, whatever the paperwork says.

### 9. The Marne decided that the war would go on

- **Author's claim:** the Marne was a German retreat and an incomplete Allied victory; the failures of both plans produced a deadlock that fixed the course of the war. [Source: Afterword, [line 3192](./source/source.txt#L3192), [line 3210](./source/source.txt#L3210), [line 3216](./source/source.txt#L3216)]
- **Reasoning:** Gallieni saw Kluck's exposed flank and forced the date; Joffre, who had planned a later battle behind the Seine, decided to fight; Moltke's order halting the right wing came hours too late. [Source: Ch 22, [line 3038](./source/source.txt#L3038), [line 3128](./source/source.txt#L3128), [line 3092](./source/source.txt#L3092)]
- **Evidence:** the two orders were signed two hours apart on 4 September. [Source: Ch 22, [line 3146](./source/source.txt#L3146)] Kluck's staff had no idea an offensive was coming. [Source: Ch 22, [line 3158](./source/source.txt#L3158)] The Afterword gives the commanders' later verdicts and French casualty figures. [Source: Afterword, [line 3196](./source/source.txt#L3196), [line 3218](./source/source.txt#L3218)]
- **Boundary:** the narrative ends on the eve of battle; the fighting is one paragraph of the Afterword. [Source: Afterword, [line 3194](./source/source.txt#L3194)] She says the credit between Joffre and Gallieni was disputed and the record tampered with. [Source: Ch 21, [line 2964](./source/source.txt#L2964); Ch 22, [line 3166](./source/source.txt#L3166)] [Analysis] The step from the deadlock to the peace and the next war is one sentence.
- **Application:** [Application] an outcome can be decisive by failing to decide.

## Critical assessment

All of this section is analysis.

- **Strongest contribution:** the paired demonstration that plans made as instruments became constraints, with documents for the key points (the eastern deployment plan, the dated ultimatum, the reserve corps, the count of missing German corps), and the chapter on Belgium, where German commanders' own words and neutral witnesses carry the case.
- **Weakest claims:** national character as cause, for the Schlieffen plan ([line 418](./source/source.txt#L418)) and for the reprisals ([line 2402](./source/source.txt#L2402)); and the long chains hung on single events: the Goeben to the fall of the Ottoman Empire ([line 1438](./source/source.txt#L1438)) although Turkey had already allied with Germany ([line 1290](./source/source.txt#L1290)), and the deadlock to the next war ([line 3210](./source/source.txt#L3210)).
- **Hidden assumptions:** that the war's origins can be explained without Austria and Serbia; that memoirs written in self-defence can establish motives and states of mind; that a different plan would have produced a different war.
- **Meaningful counterargument:** if the plans were as binding as she says, the decisive choices were political ones made earlier, in Vienna and Berlin in July, which the book leaves out. And her criticisms of the German command, too rigid, not faithful enough to Schlieffen, too free in the field, pull against each other.
- **Unanswered question:** was the Schlieffen plan ever workable with the divisions Germany had? She notes Schlieffen lacked them for his full design ([line 414](./source/source.txt#L414)) and does not follow it through. The Marne counterfactuals accumulate without being ranked ([line 3200](./source/source.txt#L3200)).
- **On sources:** she describes the participants' record as "this forest of special pleading" ([line 3228](./source/source.txt#L3228)) and often flags a doubtful witness in the text. The narrative still depends on those witnesses, and the judgments here about how she uses them were made without reading her endnotes.

## Action layer

The book is history and prescribes nothing. These are uses a reader might draw from it, and each is application.

- **To try now:** [Application] for any plan in hand, write down the assumptions it makes about what other parties will do, and what evidence would show each one false.
- **Conditions required:** [Application] someone with standing to say the plan should change, and a kept alternative. Germany had the alternative on file and no one willing to use it.
- **Warning signs:** [Application] "it cannot be altered"; adverse reports welcomed as confirming the plan; the person who was right being removed; a commitment everyone relies on and no one has approved; reports of a beaten opponent with few prisoners to show for it.
- **Seven-day experiment:** [Application] take one decision described as irreversible and find out what reversing it would cost in fact.

## Reading recommendation

**Read selected chapters.** For the argument: the Afterword first, then chapters 2, 3, 6, 14, 17, 21 and 22, with 9, 13, 16 and 19 if time allows. For the book's reputation as narrative: chapters 1, 8 and 10. The rest can be skimmed. Pair it with a later history of 1914: the July crisis is absent, the Schlieffen plan and the Belgian atrocities have been much argued over since, and nearly every scene here comes from someone with a reputation to defend.
