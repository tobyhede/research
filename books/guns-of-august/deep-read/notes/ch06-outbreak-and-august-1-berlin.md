# ch06: Outbreak and August 1: Berlin (pp. 67–77)

Includes the half-page introduction to Part II, "Outbreak" (pp. 67-68), which is the book's whole treatment of the July crisis.

## Question this chapter answers

On the day Germany mobilised, could the attack on France have been stopped, and why was it not?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Faithful support | pp. 67-68 | Germany's 5 July assurance to Austria; in Tuchman's telling the signal that released the onrush of events. |
| Pull of military schedules | p. 68 | Her explanation of late July: heads of state tried to back away and the timetables of the general staffs dragged them forward. |
| Kriegesgefahr | p. 68 | Danger of War, the preliminary state declared on 31 July. |
| Mobilization | pp. 69-70 | An automatic machine once started: two million men, 11,000 trains, timed to the axle. |
| Lichnowsky telegram | p. 71 | The London ambassador's mistaken report of 1 August that England would stay neutral and guarantee French neutrality if Germany did not attack France. |
| "It cannot be altered" | pp. 73-74 | Moltke's reply to the Kaiser; for Tuchman the phrase that marks military plans dictating policy. |
| Der Tag | p. 72 | The Day for which German energies had been gathered. |
| Trois Vierges | p. 76 | The Luxembourg railway junction seized at 7 p.m. on 1 August, the first frontier crossed. |
| War guilt | p. 77 | Germany's wish to make Russia appear the aggressor, for home opinion and to hold Italy, undercut by its own declaration of war. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] The July crisis is told in one paragraph: assassination, the German assurance of 5 July, the Austrian ultimatum, mobilisations, the German ultimatum to Russia of 31 July (pp. 67-68).
- [interpretation] Governments tried to draw back at the brink and the staffs' timetables pulled them on (p. 68).
- [narrative] German envoys had assured Berlin that Russia would not fight for fear of revolution (pp. 68-69).
- [interpretation] The Kaiser never wanted a general war; he wanted power and prestige by frightening others, and shrank when battle came near (p. 70).
- [narrative] An offer of autonomy for Alsace might have kept France still; she grants the story may be apocryphal and says the option existed all the same (pp. 70-71).
- [narrative] Germany instead demanded that a neutral France hand over Toul and Verdun as a guarantee (p. 71).
- [narrative] On Lichnowsky's telegram the Kaiser ordered the whole army east; Moltke refused, saying the deployment of millions could not be improvised (pp. 72-74).
- [interpretation] The same rigid phrase lay behind every major German mistake, including Belgium and the submarine war (pp. 73-74).
- [narrative] The plan could have been altered: an eastern deployment plan was kept current until 1913, and the railway chief von Staab later showed four armies could have gone east by 15 August (p. 74).
- [narrative] Moltke later told Erzberger that attacking France first had been a mistake (p. 74).
- [narrative] The Kaiser halted the move into Luxembourg; the order arrived late; Moltke wept, refused to sign, and wrote that something in him broke (pp. 75-76).
- [interpretation] Whether Germans could temperamentally have held back from France is doubtful even if it was technically possible (p. 76).
- [narrative] Germany declared war on Russia although Tirpitz asked why it should take on the odium of the aggressor; Foreign Office jurists insisted on form (p. 77).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 69-70) mobilisation was a single interlocking railway schedule + (p. 73) reversing it risked chaos.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 73-74) Moltke treated the plan as unalterable.
  **Counter**: (p. 74) the eastern plan existed and Staab showed it feasible, so the limit was Moltke's nerve and the Staff's fixation, not the railways.
- **Premises**: (p. 77) Germany wanted Russia to bear the guilt + (p. 77) Germany declared war first.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 77) legal correctness defeated political interest. This is a small instance of the book's larger claim about German tone-deafness to opinion.

## Evidence offered

- Moltke's memoirs for the scene with the Kaiser, quoted at length (pp. 73-76).
- The Kaiser's marginalia on diplomatic telegrams (p. 70).
- Lichnowsky's telegrams and the Kaiser's wire to George V (pp. 71, 74-75).
- Von Staab's book and Erzberger's testimony (p. 74).
- Sazonov and Paléologue for the declaration of war at St. Petersburg; she notes that Pourtalès reported only a bare handing over of the note (pp. 76-77).
- Tirpitz's memoirs (p. 77).
- Sourcing: the endnotes rest on the Kautsky collection of German documents, Moltke's Erinnerungen, Tirpitz, Bülow and journalists' accounts of Berlin. The central scene has one main witness, Moltke, writing in self-defence after his dismissal. She is careful to mark the apocryphal Alsace proposal (p. 71) and the embroidered Sazonov scene (p. 77). (This paragraph rests on the attributions in the text and on a mechanical tally of the chapter's endnotes; the endnotes were sampled, not read line by line.)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- This is the hinge of the book's argument about plans. Ch02 said the plan lacked flexibility (p. 33); here that is shown at the moment of decision.
- The July crisis receives one paragraph, as the Author's Note warned (fm02, p. 12). The claim that schedules dragged statesmen forward (p. 68) is asserted for all powers and demonstrated only for Germany.
- Grey's elliptical manner, which caused the misunderstanding (p. 72), is the British side of the no-commitment habit in ch04 and is taken up in ch07.
- Moltke broken on day one (pp. 75-76) prepares his loss of control in ch21 and ch22.

## Open questions

- If Germany had gone east, would France have attacked under Plan 17 anyway, and would Britain have stayed out? She raises the question (p. 76) and leaves it.
- How much weight can Staab's after-the-fact demonstration bear?
