# ch04: "A Single British Soldier …" (pp. 47–57)

## Question this chapter answers

How did Britain come to have a detailed plan to fight beside France while its government denied any commitment?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Staff talks / military conversations | p. 49 | Anglo-French General Staff planning begun in 1906 as a precaution, authorised by Grey, Haldane and the Prime Minister without telling the Cabinet. |
| No commitment | pp. 49, 55 | The government's formula that joint planning did not bind Britain; Tuchman treats it as a blanket ministers pulled over their heads (p. 57). |
| Committee of Imperial Defence (C.I.D.) | p. 48 | The war-policy body created by the Esher committee; its secret meeting of 23 August 1911 settled British strategy. |
| Henry Wilson | pp. 50-51 | Director of Military Operations from 1910: francophile, energetic, single-minded; the man who built the plan. |
| Dubail-Wilson agreement | pp. 52-53 | Memorandum of 20 July 1911: six divisions and a cavalry division, about 150,000 men, landing at Havre, Boulogne and Rouen and concentrating near Maubeuge by M-13. |
| Plan W / l'Armée W | pp. 56-57 | The Staffs' name for the expeditionary force and its movement plan, complete to the last billet by spring 1914. |
| Navy plan | pp. 50, 54 | Fisher's alternative: no army in France; a landing on the Baltic coast and the holding of Antwerp. |
| Grey-Cambon letter | p. 55 | The 1912 Cabinet-approved letter stating both parties remained free to decide; a masterpiece of ellipsis. |
| Belgian neutrality (British condition) | pp. 56-57 | Britain could act only if Germany violated Belgium first; the Belgians would fire on anyone who entered uninvited. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] Russia's defeat by Japan in 1905 left France without an effective ally, and Germany tested the moment at Tangier (pp. 47-48).
- [narrative] A 1905 British war game assumed a German flanking movement through Belgium and concluded that only quick British arrival in strength could stop it (p. 48).
- [narrative] Staff talks began in 1906 with a written disclaimer; three ministers kept them from the rest of the Cabinet (p. 49).
- [narrative] Foch told Wilson that one British soldier would suffice, and France would see that he was killed (p. 51).
- [narrative] Wilson found an army ready to mobilise and no plan to move it; by March 1911 he had a timetable (p. 52).
- [narrative] At the C.I.D. meeting of August 1911 the army's plan defeated the navy's, and Churchill went to the Admiralty as a result (pp. 53-54).
- [narrative] Wilson predicted the envelopment correctly and, following French theory, put the force west of the Meuse at four divisions (pp. 53-54).
- [interpretation] The meeting decided British strategy and started the Cabinet split that mattered in 1914 (p. 54).
- [interpretation] The talks were natural but they were not informal; Esher told Asquith the plans had committed Britain to fight whatever the Cabinet wished (p. 54).
- [narrative] The 1912 naval arrangement moved the French fleet to the Mediterranean and left Britain an obligation to guard the Channel coast (p. 55).
- [interpretation] The Grey-Cambon letter satisfied everyone by saying nothing definite (p. 55).
- [narrative] Belgium refused any prior arrangement; British action depended on Germany violating neutrality first (pp. 56-57).
- [interpretation] While soldiers fixed the lines of battle, the politicians chose not to look (p. 57).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 53) the plan placed the BEF on the French left by a fixed day + (p. 55) the French fleet had moved on the strength of a British undertaking.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 54) Britain was committed in fact while free in form. The moral obligation is her inference, supported by Esher's remark.
- **Premises**: (p. 53) the navy would not guarantee ports north of Dover-Calais + (p. 56) Belgium barred prior landings.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 53) the BEF could only go to France as an extension of the French line, so independent action in Belgium was closed off.

## Evidence offered

- Wilson's diaries, quoted often and at length (pp. 52-54, 55-56).
- Repington's 1905 questions and the French replies (p. 49).
- Fisher's letters for the naval view (pp. 50, 54).
- Haldane's and Grey's memoirs for the ministers' reasoning (pp. 49, 55).
- Huguet's record of the French view (p. 53).
- Sourcing: the endnotes rely on Callwell's edition of Wilson's diaries, Haldane's Before the War, Grey's Twenty-Five Years, Esher's journals, Fisher's letters and Huguet. The chapter sees British planning through Wilson, an advocate, and through ministers' later memoirs. Asquith's own view is, as she says, unrecorded (pp. 54-55). ## Open questions

- Did ambiguity serve Britain badly? A clear alliance might have deterred Germany, or might have broken the Cabinet, as Grey feared (p. 55). She does not settle it.
- Wilson dominates the account. How far did the plan depend on one officer's conviction?
