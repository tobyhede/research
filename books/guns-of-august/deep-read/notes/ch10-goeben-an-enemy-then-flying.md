# ch10: "Goeben … An Enemy Then Flying" (pp. 118–138)

## Question this chapter answers

How did two German warships escape a superior British fleet in the Mediterranean, and what followed from their reaching Constantinople?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goeben and Breslau | pp. 118-119 | Germany's only warships in the Mediterranean: a battle cruiser as fast as Britain's and a light cruiser, under Admiral Souchon. |
| Sick Man / wrong horse | pp. 119-120 | The British images of Turkey that let its friendship lapse just when it was worth most. |
| Young Turks | p. 119 | The Committee of Union and Progress: Enver the pro-German, Talaat the calculating boss; divided on which side to join. |
| Requisition of the Turkish battleships | p. 121 | Churchill's seizure on 28 July of two ships Turkey had paid for by public subscription; Tuchman calls it piracy on the Tyne. |
| Turco-German alliance | p. 121 | Signed 3 August, the day of Grey's regrets; Turkey then declined to act on it. |
| Superior forces | p. 124 | The Admiralty's instruction of 31 July not to be brought to action against superior forces; meant for the Austrian fleet, read by Milne and Troubridge as covering the Goeben. |
| Temperament of the commander | pp. 124-125 | Her explanation of the failure: at the moment of live ammunition some commanders grow bold and some careful. |
| Six-mile limit | p. 129 | The order to respect Italian neutrality that kept Milne out of the Strait of Messina. |
| Sale of the ships | p. 136 | The Turkish fiction that the German ships had been bought, which answered the British seizure. |

## Leading propositions

- [interpretation] No other single exploit of the war had such long consequences as Souchon's seven-day voyage (pp. 118-119).
- [interpretation] Britain no longer took Turkey seriously and never considered that Turkish neutrality might be valuable (p. 120).
- [narrative] Britain refused a Turkish alliance in 1911 and seized the two battleships on 28 July 1914; Turkey signed with Germany on 3 August (pp. 120-121).
- [narrative] The Allied fleets saw the Goeben only as a threat to the French troop transports from North Africa (p. 122).
- [interpretation] British naval thinking credited the German navy with more audacity than it showed (pp. 122-123).
- [narrative] Milne did not send two battle cruisers to shadow the Goeben as ordered on 2 August (pp. 125-126).
- [narrative] On 4 August British battle cruisers passed the Goeben within range and could not fire because the ultimatum had not expired; the Cabinet refused to authorise action before midnight (p. 128).
- [narrative] Milne guarded the western exit from Messina with his battle cruisers and left one light cruiser to the east (p. 129).
- [narrative] Souchon, with orders cancelled, chose on his own to go to Constantinople in order to force Turkey into war with Russia (p. 130).
- [narrative] Troubridge with four armoured cruisers gave up the interception on 7 August, judging the Goeben a superior force (p. 132).
- [interpretation] The failure was political more than naval: no one imagined a political mission to the Dardanelles (pp. 127-128, 132-133).
- [narrative] A clerk's false signal that Austria had declared war cost Milne another twenty-four hours (pp. 133-134).
- [narrative] Enver alone ordered the ships admitted on 10 August (pp. 135-136).
- [narrative] Kitchener vetoed Churchill's proposal to send torpedo boats in after them (p. 137).
- [narrative] On 28 October Souchon shelled Russian Black Sea ports under the Turkish flag and Turkey was at war by 5 November (p. 137).
- [interpretation] The closing of the Black Sea, Gallipoli, the Middle Eastern campaigns and the breakup of the Ottoman Empire followed from the voyage (pp. 137-138).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 120) a century of contempt for Turkey + (p. 121) the seizure of the ships + (pp. 132-133) ignorance of Turkish politics.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 132-133) the chase lacked urgency because the British could not conceive where the Goeben was going.
- **Premises**: (p. 124) an ambiguous order + (pp. 124-125, 132) two cautious commanders.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 132) three chances to engage were lost. She adds a fourth lost to a clerical error (p. 133).
- **Premises**: (p. 137) the Goeben's guns commanded Constantinople + (p. 137) Souchon attacked Russia in Turkey's name.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 137-138) one ship brought the Ottoman Empire into the war and cut Russia off. This is the chapter's largest causal claim and it is stated more than argued.

## Evidence offered

- Admiralty signals to Milne, quoted in sequence (pp. 124-126, 128-129, 133).
- Churchill's World Crisis for the Admiralty's view and his later admissions (pp. 124, 128-129, 133, 136).
- Souchon's own account and a crew member's published narrative (pp. 127, 130).
- Fisher's letters on Milne, which she warns must be scaled down (p. 125).
- Ambassador Morgenthau's memoir for the scene at Constantinople; the witnesses were Tuchman's own mother and family, a connection the text does not state here (p. 135; see fm02, pp. 8-9).
- Trade figures: Russian exports down 98 per cent, imports 95 per cent (p. 138).
- Sourcing: the endnotes cite Churchill, Corbett's official naval history, Milne's self-defence, Souchon, Kopp, Djemal, Morgenthau, Kannengiesser and the German documents. Milne and Troubridge are judged mainly through their critics and Churchill, himself the author of the ambiguous order. She notes that the navy acquitted Troubridge for its own sake (p. 138). (This paragraph rests on the attributions in the text and on a mechanical tally of the chapter's endnotes; the endnotes were sampled, not read line by line.)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The delay in sending the British ultimatum (ch09, p. 115) is given a concrete cost here (p. 128).
- The only chapter that follows a story past August, to November; it is the episode she first wanted to write (fm02, pp. 8-9).
- Individual temperament as a cause (pp. 124-125) recurs for Sir John French (ch12), Lanrezac (ch13), Moltke and Kluck (ch21).
- The chain from one ship to the fall of the Ottoman Empire (pp. 137-138) sits uneasily with her own report that Turkey had already signed an alliance (p. 121) and that Enver wanted war (pp. 119-120).

## Open questions

- Would Turkey have entered the war without the Goeben? The alliance of 3 August suggests the direction was set.
- How fair is the treatment of Troubridge, given the gun ranges she reports (pp. 131-132)?
