# ch18: Blue Water, Blockade, and the Great Neutral (pp. 273–287)

## Question this chapter answers

Why did the German navy stay in port in August 1914, how did Britain turn command of the sea into a blockade, and how did that begin to draw in the United States?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grand Fleet | pp. 273-274 | Britain's main fleet at Scapa Flow under Jellicoe: a vital necessity that could not be risked, and nervous about submarines and an unready base. |
| Fleet in being | p. 278 | The role the Kaiser gave the High Seas Fleet: stay in a fortified position as a standing threat. Mahan had come to think its value overstated. |
| Luxury fleet / Mahan | pp. 273, 276 | Germany's navy, built under the spell of Mahan's sea-power doctrine; in her view it made Germany weaker by adding Britain to its enemies. |
| Tirpitz | p. 276 | The fleet's builder, who believed in fighting with it and was shut out of its direction; he found on 30 July that there was no plan. |
| Distant blockade | p. 280 | Britain's 1912 change from close blockade of ports to interception far out at sea, which raised the legal question of destination. |
| Declaration of London | pp. 279-280 | The 1908 code favouring neutral trade; never ratified by Britain. |
| Continuous voyage | p. 281 | The old British doctrine that the ultimate destination of goods decides; revived by the Order in Council of 20 August. |
| Order in Council of 20 August | p. 281 | Made conditional contraband seizable on any sufficient evidence of enemy destination; in effect made everything contraband. |
| The great neutral | p. 279 | The United States; Wilson meant neutrality as the basis for acting as mediator. |
| Dog in the night | p. 286 | Her image for the German navy: the decisive thing at sea was what it did not do. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] The British navy's chief fear was interruption of trade; two-thirds of Britain's food was imported (p. 273).
- [narrative] Scapa Flow had no fixed defences; Jellicoe twice moved the fleet away in alarm, leaving the North Sea open had the Germans known (pp. 274-275).
- [interpretation] A German naval offensive in those weeks might have had startling results (p. 275).
- [narrative] The BEF crossed in 137 passages from 14 to 18 August without a German ship appearing (p. 276).
- [interpretation] The German navy, a chief cause of British enmity, had no active role planned for it when war came (p. 276).
- [interpretation] Germany would have been stronger without a navy than with one, since without it Britain might have stayed neutral and German seaborne supply stayed open (pp. 276-277).
- [narrative] The Kaiser ordered a defensive attitude for the fleet; he could not bear to risk his ships and reserved their direction to himself (pp. 277-278).
- [interpretation] The fleet was held back for reasons of hope: that England could be bought off, that the fleet would be a bargaining counter, and above all that the army would win before blockade mattered (pp. 278-279).
- [narrative] By the end of August German merchant shipping had left the oceans; 670 steamers were laid up in neutral ports (p. 279).
- [narrative] The Order in Council of 20 August was passed for a military reason, to stop supplies reaching the German army through Rotterdam, with little discussion (pp. 281-282).
- [interpretation] Its authors did not foresee the chain that led to unrestricted submarine warfare and American entry (pp. 281-282).
- [interpretation] After Mons and Charleroi the Allies saw a long war, in which they would need American supply and would have to cut Germany off from it; the two aims were incompatible (pp. 282-283).
- [interpretation] Wilson held to neutrality in order to play mediator, and could not be neutral in thought (pp. 283-285).
- [narrative] American trade with the Central Powers fell from $169 million in 1914 to $1 million in 1916, and with the Allies rose from $824 million to $3 billion (p. 284).
- [interpretation] The greatest Allied asset in America was German folly more than the British fleet; Louvain muffled resentment of the blockade (pp. 284-285).
- [narrative] Heligoland Bight on 28 August cost Germany three light cruisers and over 1,000 men, and the Kaiser forbade further risks (p. 286).
- [interpretation] German naval policy went underwater by default, and the U-boats fulfilled Wilson's fear (p. 287).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 276-277) the navy made Britain an enemy + (p. 279) the short-war belief made blockade seem unimportant + (p. 278) the Kaiser would not risk the ships.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 286-287) Germany paid the political price of a fleet and got no use from it, and blockade was established unopposed.
- **Premises**: (p. 281) distant blockade needs a destination rule + (p. 281) the Order supplied one of unlimited reach + (p. 287) Germany's only answer was the submarine.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 281-282, 287) a Cabinet decision of 20 August, taken as an odd end, led toward American belligerency. She marks this as unforeseen and argues it by sequence.
- **Premises**: (p. 284) trade followed existing ties + (p. 285) Belgium and Louvain turned Wilson against Germany.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 284-285) American alignment with the Allies came from sympathy and German conduct, with economics as a consequence. She sets this against economic determinism (p. 284).

## Evidence offered

- Churchill's World Crisis and Jellicoe's own account for British naval anxiety (pp. 274-276).
- Tirpitz's memoirs and letters, including the admission that there was no plan (pp. 276, 279, 286-287).
- The preamble of the German Navy Law of 1900 on the effect of blockade (p. 277).
- Asquith's diary entry on a long Cabinet about coal and contraband (p. 282).
- Trade figures (p. 284); counts of ships and tonnage (p. 279).
- House's diary and Spring-Rice's report on Wilson's private views (p. 285).
- Sourcing: I did not read this chapter's endnotes. The German naval story rests on Tirpitz, an embittered and excluded witness, with Bülow for court gossip. The American part looks ahead to 1917 and is the chapter's most summary section. The counterfactual about a navy-less Germany (pp. 276-277) is her own.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The short-war belief (ch02, ch09 p. 106) is again the cause of a failure to prepare: here it idles the fleet (p. 279) and Moltke refuses an economic staff (p. 282).
- British naval caution (pp. 274-275) is the background of the Goeben failure (ch10, pp. 124-125); the fleet-in-being idea appeared there too (ch07, p. 85).
- Louvain's effect on Wilson (p. 285) joins this chapter to ch17 (p. 270).
- Schlieffen had wanted a quick decision before British blockade could tell (ch02, p. 32); this chapter shows the blockade starting.
- The chapter reaches furthest beyond August of any, to 1916 trade and 1917 submarines. It supports the Afterword's claim that the war spread until both hemispheres were entangled (af01, p. 369).
- Tension: Britain is shown bending law for military necessity (p. 281) in the same book that condemns the German appeal to necessity; she notes the manner without drawing the comparison.

## Open questions

- Could the High Seas Fleet have done real damage in August, or does she take Tirpitz's later claim (pp. 286-287) at face value?
- How direct is the line from the Order in Council to American entry? She says it was not thought of at the time (pp. 281-282) and gives it as the result all the same.
