# ch14: Debacle: Lorraine, Ardennes, Charleroi, Mons (pp. 196–223)

## Question this chapter answers

What happened in the four battles of 20 to 24 August, why did the French offensive fail everywhere, and what did the failure settle?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Battle of the Frontiers | pp. 196-197 | The four linked battles of 20 to 24 August: Lorraine, the Ardennes, Charleroi and Mons; in numbers and rate of loss the greatest battle of the war (p. 223). |
| Morhange | p. 198 | The Lorraine battlefield where, in her words, the doctrine of the offensive died and the power of the defence showed itself. |
| Pursue direction Epinal | p. 199 | OHL's order of 22 August committing the German left wing to a frontal attack on the French fortress line instead of feeding the right. |
| Case 3 | p. 199 | The contingency in the German plan allowing an attack across the Moselle on OHL's express order. |
| Numerical superiority in the centre | p. 200 | GQG's belief about the Ardennes, false because German reserve corps marched behind active corps under the same numbers (pp. 205, 222). |
| Army of Lorraine | pp. 204-205 | Maunoury's force of reserve divisions formed on 21 August; an improvisation she credits as the saving flexibility of the French army. |
| Battle of Charleroi | p. 212 | Lanrezac's Fifth Army against Bülow and Hausen on the Sambre and Meuse, 21 to 23 August. |
| Another Sedan | pp. 214-215 | Lanrezac's fear of encirclement, which decided him to retreat and preserve the army. |
| Mons | p. 220 | The BEF's nine-hour defensive action on the canal on 23 August; brave, small, and later inflated into a victory. |
| Limogés | pp. 221-222 | Generals dismissed by Joffre, who blamed execution and never the plan. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] In Lorraine on 20 August the French attacked fortified positions without artillery preparation and were thrown back; Castelnau retreated to the Grand Couronné and Dubail had to conform (pp. 197-198).
- [interpretation] The slaughter at Morhange ended the doctrine of the offensive and revealed the defensive power that turned the war into four years of position warfare (p. 198).
- [narrative] Success in Lorraine revived OHL's hope of a double envelopment, and Moltke ordered the left wing to attack toward Epinal (pp. 198-199).
- [interpretation] That order locked two German armies against the French fortress line when they could have reinforced the right wing (p. 199).
- [narrative] Joffre launched the Ardennes offensive on 21 August knowing the Belgians and British would not support the left, and forbade infantry reconnaissance for the sake of surprise (pp. 199-201).
- [narrative] In fog the armies collided; the Germans dug in and the French charged with the bayonet into machine guns (p. 203).
- [narrative] The Germans in the Ardennes outnumbered the French, having reserve corps the French had not counted (p. 205).
- [interpretation] Method and training beat nerve; the Briey ore stayed German and fed the long war (p. 205).
- [narrative] German strength west of the Meuse was 30 divisions where GQG and Wilson counted 17 or 18; overall superiority was one and a half to one, on the right nearly two to one (pp. 207-208).
- [interpretation] What a staff makes of evidence depends on what it wants or fears to believe (p. 207).
- [narrative] Bülow ordered Hausen to attack westward and so left Lanrezac's line of retreat open (p. 213).
- [narrative] On 23 August, with Namur fallen, the Fourth Army retreating and Saxons across the Meuse at Onhaye, Lanrezac ordered a general retreat without consulting GQG (pp. 214-215).
- [interpretation] With that order the last French hope of winning a short war vanished; GQG later made Lanrezac the scapegoat (p. 215).
- [narrative] At Mons 70,000 British with 300 guns faced 160,000 Germans with 600; Kluck, held in by Bülow, attacked frontally with two corps and lost heavily (pp. 217-218).
- [narrative] Mons cost 1,600 British casualties and delayed Kluck one day; French casualties in the four days exceeded 140,000 (p. 220).
- [interpretation] Joffre stayed unshaken by blaming commanders and absolving the plan, and that confidence was what the following days required (p. 221).
- [narrative] GQG's note of 24 August ordered entrenchment and artillery coordination; the same day intelligence found the reserve corps (p. 222).
- [interpretation] German possession of Belgian and northern French industry blocked any compromise peace and prolonged the war four years; she labels this hindsight (p. 223).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 197) regulations assumed twenty seconds before defenders could fire + (p. 197) machine guns needed eight + (pp. 203, 208) the French did not entrench.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 198, 222) the offensive failed from too much nerve, the opposite of Joffre's diagnosis.
- **Premises**: (p. 205) uncounted reserve corps in the centre + (pp. 207-208) thirty divisions on the right.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 205) the Germans were strong in both places at once, so the premise of Plan 17 was false. This closes the argument opened in ch03 (pp. 46-47).
- **Premises**: (p. 213) Hausen was diverted from the line of retreat + (pp. 214-215) Lanrezac withdrew in time + (p. 218) Kluck's flanking corps were not up at Mons.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 213, 218) German command errors let two Allied armies escape annihilation.
- **Premises**: (p. 199) the left wing was committed at Epinal.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 199) the right wing would not be reinforced. Krafft is quoted calling it one of the most consequential decisions of the war.

## Evidence offered

- Field Regulations timings against machine-gun timings (p. 197).
- Krafft's and Tappen's accounts of the Epinal telephone call (p. 199).
- Diaries of a French sergeant and a German officer for the Ardennes fighting (pp. 203-204).
- Strength figures for both sides west of the Meuse and at Mons (pp. 207-208, 217).
- Lanrezac's own account of his decision; she remarks that one line has the ring of memoirs written afterwards (p. 215).
- Wilson's diary, used against him (pp. 218-220).
- Joffre's postwar testimony that he still thought the theory right (p. 222).
- An anonymous British official verdict that Lanrezac's retreat spared another Sedan (pp. 222-223).
- Sourcing: I did not read this chapter's endnotes line by line. In the text she warns that Joffre's memoirs were staff-written and semi-official (p. 200), that Ruffey's and de Langle's complaints were made after the battle (pp. 202-203, 204-205), and that Spears's account is coloured by resentment (p. 219). The case for Lanrezac rests heavily on Lanrezac.

## Open questions

- How far did the reserve-corps discovery (p. 222) really surprise GQG, given the 1913 intelligence in ch03 (p. 47)?
- Is the claim that Germany could not have fought a long war without Briey (p. 205) supported anywhere beyond assertion?
- She says if all four of Kluck's corps had attacked together history might have been different (p. 218). The book collects many such moments; how many can each be decisive?
