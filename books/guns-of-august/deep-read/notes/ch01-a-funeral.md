# ch01: A Funeral (pp. 13–25)

## Question this chapter answers

What was the European order on the eve of the war, how had Britain, France and Russia come together against Germany, and what was the temper of Germany's rulers?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Encirclement (Einkreisung) | p. 13 | The Kaiser's and Germany's reading of the ententes as a plot, with Edward VII as arch plotter. Tuchman treats it as a reaction Germany provoked by its own threats (pp. 17-18). |
| Understandings / Entente | p. 15 | Britain's attachments to France (1904), Russia (1907) and Japan: commitments short of alliance, because England dislikes the definitive. |
| Uncle of Europe | p. 15 | Edward VII, literally related to most ruling houses; a sociable king whose personal diplomacy eased a policy he did not make. |
| The Kaiser | p. 13 | William II: impulsive, envious, craving recognition, owner of the least inhibited tongue in Europe; a different goal every hour (p. 17). |
| Need for recognition | pp. 17-18 | The national mood Tuchman ascribes to Germany: a sense of entitlement to rule and of being cheated of acknowledgment. |
| Treaty of Björkö | p. 20 | The Kaiser's 1905 personal treaty with the Czar, void because it contradicted the Franco-Russian alliance; her example of dynastic diplomacy failing. |
| The Great Illusion | pp. 20-21 | Norman Angell's book arguing that economic interdependence made war unprofitable and so improbable; a cult, with Lord Esher as disciple. |
| Germany and the Next War | p. 21 | Bernhardi's 1911 book: war as biological necessity, world power or downfall, the duty to strike first and crush France. |

## Leading propositions

- [interpretation] The funeral of Edward VII in May 1910 was the last great assembly of the old order, a sunset (p. 13).
- [narrative] In Edward's nine-year reign Britain left isolation for understandings with France, Russia and Japan (p. 15).
- [interpretation] Edward neither initiated nor directed policy; he was the instrument of the new alignments, and his charm made them possible (pp. 15, 22).
- [narrative] Germany could have had its own English entente but rebuffed Chamberlain's overtures in 1899 and 1901 out of unfocused suspicion (pp. 16-17).
- [interpretation] Bismarck's successors lacked his clear aims: Holstein suspected everyone, Bülow had no principles, the Kaiser changed goals hourly (p. 17).
- [interpretation] Germany as a nation shared the Kaiser's craving for recognition, sought esteem by threats, and when other nations answered with alliances called it encirclement (pp. 17-18).
- [narrative] Dropping Bismarck's Reinsurance Treaty with Russia was the first and worst blunder of William's reign; it produced the Franco-Russian alliance of 1892 (p. 19).
- [narrative] The Kaiser tried to detach the Czar by personal letters and by the Björkö treaty, which Russian ministers killed (pp. 19-20).
- [narrative] In 1910 Europe was calm and prosperous; Angell's argument that war could not pay was widely believed in Britain (pp. 20-21).
- [interpretation] Bernhardi's doctrine of necessary, offensive war is offered as the opposite and equally influential current in Germany (pp. 21-22).
- [narrative] At the funeral dinner the Kaiser proposed to the French foreign minister that France side with Germany against England, then denied it (p. 23).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 17-18) Germany pursued esteem by naval building and menace + (pp. 16-17) it refused agreement with England when offered.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 18) the ententes were a response to German behaviour, so encirclement was self-inflicted.
- **Premises**: (pp. 20-21) informed British opinion held war economically irrational + (pp. 21-22) German military thought held it a duty.
  **Conclusion**: implied, not stated: the two sides did not share a premise about war, so reassurance on one side was no restraint on the other.
- **Premises**: (p. 19) dynastic ties and letters + (p. 20) Björkö failed on contact with ministers.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 20) the personal diplomacy of sovereigns no longer governed state policy.

## Evidence offered

- The Times and Le Figaro for the ceremony and public mood (pp. 13, 22, 23).
- The Kaiser's letters, marginalia and reported table talk, including the remark that Edward was Satan and the margin note that Edward wants war (pp. 13, 20).
- Eckardstein's memoirs for the 1902 dinner; she flags that he does not say how he overheard (p. 17).
- Bernhardi quoted at length from his own book (pp. 21-22).
- Esher's lectures and diary (pp. 21, 25).
- Sourcing: the endnotes for this chapter lean on English-language memoirs and biographies (Lee's life of Edward, Roosevelt's letters, Bülow's memoirs in translation, the Willy-Nicky letters), on British diplomatic documents and on newspapers. German national character is argued from Bernhardi and a handful of slogans. No German source defends the encirclement view. ## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Bernhardi's necessity (pp. 21-22) returns as Schlieffen's military necessity in ch02 and as the justification for Belgium in ch08.
- Albert of Belgium is singled out at the funeral as the one king who would achieve greatness (p. 14); ch08 and ch11 make the case.
- Angell's short-war economics (pp. 20-21) is taken up in ch02 (p. 30) and ch09 as the shared belief that a long war was impossible.
- The chapter covers 1890 to 1910 in twelve pages and says almost nothing of Austria-Hungary or the Balkans, in line with the Author's Note (fm02).

## Open questions

- A whole nation's psychology is inferred from its emperor and one militarist author. How representative was Bernhardi of German policy as opposed to German pamphleteering?
- Was encirclement wholly imagined? The chapter itself reports three ententes forming around Germany in nine years (p. 15).
