# ch11: Liège and Alsace (pp. 138–164)

Covers 4 to 16 August on the two extreme wings: the German assault on Liège and the French raid into Upper Alsace, with GQG and Belgian headquarters in between.

## Question this chapter answers

What happened when each side's opening move met a real enemy, and did either high command let the result change its plan?

## Key terms

## Leading propositions

- [interpretation] The two opening moves were made regardless of the enemy; the French one into Alsace was more sentimental than strategic (p. 138).
- [narrative] Liège was the first objective on which the whole German right wing depended (p. 139).
- [narrative] The Germans, expecting Belgium to yield or resist only in token, had not brought up their siege guns; Krupp's road model was not due until 1 October (pp. 140-142).
- [narrative] The Belgian General Staff had made no arrangement to deploy on the Meuse; Albert could only keep one division each at Liège and Namur (pp. 142-143).
- [interpretation] Existing plans outweigh the impulse to change them; she names the Kaiser and Moltke, Kitchener and Wilson, Lanrezac and Joffre as parallel cases (p. 143).
- [narrative] Belgian will outran means: 117,000 field troops, no heavy field artillery, half the German proportion of machine guns (pp. 143-144).
- [narrative] From the first day German troops, told the Belgians would not fight, shot civilians and priests, took hostages at Warsage and burned Battice (pp. 145-147).
- [interpretation] The priest-conspiracy story was a pretext for German consumption; the executions were deliberate terror; this began the process that made Belgium Germany's nemesis (pp. 146-147).
- [narrative] The infantry assaults of 5 August failed with heavy loss; Ludendorff took over the 14th Brigade, broke through between the forts and took the Citadel's surrender on 7 August (pp. 147-148, 151).
- [interpretation] The prodigal spending of lives that led to the Somme and Verdun began on the second day at Liège (p. 147).
- [narrative] Leman withdrew the 3rd Division on 6 August to preserve the Field Army; the city fell, the forts held (p. 149).
- [narrative] Joffre refused to shift a brigade to help Belgium and asked the Belgians to fall back on Namur; Albert chose Antwerp (pp. 150-151).
- [narrative] Lanrezac's letter and Ruffey's warning about the German right were dismissed; GQG's Operations bureau refused Intelligence reports that would require changing the plan (pp. 154-156).
- [narrative] The VIIth Corps took Mulhouse on 8 August and was driven out by 10 August; Bonneau was dismissed and an Army of Alsace formed under Pau (pp. 158-159).
- [narrative] Sordet's cavalry found nothing west of the Meuse because it came too early and was screened; this confirmed GQG, which also explained away the Huy report and relieved Fournier (pp. 160-162).
- [narrative] The siege guns opened fire on 12 August; eleven forts fell by 16 August and Loncin blew up with Leman inside (pp. 162-164).
- [interpretation] Liège delayed the German advance by two days, not the two weeks the world believed; its real gift was a cause and an example (p. 164).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 139, 141) the German timetable needed Liège open before the right wing could move and allowed no siege + (pp. 140, 145) the Germans assumed Belgium would not fight and left the guns behind.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 147, 152-153) the forts could not be taken by assault; the army had to wait for the guns and Kluck's start slipped from the 10th to the 13th.
  **Qualification**: (p. 164) the net delay against the schedule was two days.
- **Premises**: (pp. 145-146) troops told to expect no fight met live fire + (p. 146) memory of 1870 + (p. 148) the aim of intimidating a people who should have let them pass.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 146-147) reprisals on civilians from day one, which turned Belgium into a moral liability for Germany.
- **Premises**: (p. 155) Joffre held that will, not the plan, decides + (p. 158) doctrine rated initiative above appreciation of enemy strength + (p. 156) Operations discounted Intelligence.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 161-162) every report of a German mass west of the Meuse (Lanrezac, Fournier, Huy, Albert) was explained away, and on 10 August GQG felt confirmed that the main German move would not be in Belgium.
- **Premises**: (p. 149) Albert's fixed purpose was an army in being on Belgian soil + (pp. 150-151) Namur risked being cut off from Antwerp.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 151) Belgium would not act as a wing of French strategy; no combined offensive.

## Evidence offered

- Technical data on the forts and guns: calibres, weights, ranges, emplacement times, Krupp's timetable (pp. 139-142).
- Ludendorff's own account for the night attack and the Citadel (pp. 147-148, 151).
- A German officer's description of burned Battice (Bloem) and Moltke's letter to Conrad of 5 August calling the advance brutal (pp. 146-147).
- A Belgian officer's account of the dead piled before the forts (p. 147).
- Lanrezac's letter to Joffre and Joffre's reaction; Ruffey and Dubail at the 3 August meeting (pp. 154-155).
- GQG's replies to Lanrezac, quoted from Joffre's memoirs (pp. 161-162).
- Demblon's eyewitness account of the 420 hauled through Liège (p. 163).
- Allied press and communiqué claims of German rout, set against the facts (pp. 149, 153).
- Sourcing: per the endnotes (pp. 393-395) the Belgian side rests on Galet, van der Essen and Cammaerts, all partisans of Albert; the German side on Ludendorff's memoir chapter and the Reichsarchiv history, which she notes gives twelve pages to the assault and one to the guns; the guns on Schindler and US artillery journals. French material is Joffre, Lanrezac, Dubail, Poincaré, Gallieni, Engerand and the postwar Briey inquiry, mostly memoirs written during the Plan 17 controversy. The Ludendorff scenes rest on Ludendorff alone. The priest executions rest on one line in Prince Bülow's memoirs; the hostage and village reports on Hanotaux and Bloem. The Belgian officer's account comes via the wartime Times History, and the kidnap attempt on Leman via van der Essen and a 1914 newspaper report, so both carry propaganda risk. The claim that executions were policy, not panic, is her inference.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The sentence on the impetus of plans (p. 143) restates the thesis of ch06 (Moltke and the Kaiser) and anticipates ch12 (Kitchener and Wilson) and ch13 (Lanrezac and Joffre).
- The two-day figure (p. 164) corrects the legend of Liège, yet she still credits the siege with pushing Kluck's start from the 10th to the 13th (p. 152). The two statements fit only if the 15 August general start had slack built in; she does not spell this out.
- The terror theme opened here (pp. 145-147) is developed in ch16 and ch17; the nemesis claim depends on those chapters.
- GQG's dismissal of Lanrezac and the under-rating of German reserves continue ch03 and are paid off in ch13 and ch14.
- The Joffre and Gallieni relationship is flagged (pp. 156-157) for the Marne chapters.
- Joffre is shown both as rigid about the plan and as holding that the plan matters less than will (p. 155); she treats these as one temperament.

## Open questions

- How far were the first-day executions ordered policy and how far panic? She asserts design (p. 146) on thin evidence in this chapter.
- Was the Alsace raid of any strategic use in pinning German forces, as Joffre intended (p. 159), or purely sentimental (p. 138)?
- If the delay was two days, did Liège affect the campaign's outcome at all, other than morally?
