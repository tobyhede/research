# ch07: August 1: Paris and London (pp. 77–89)

## Question this chapter answers

How did France make sure Germany would appear the aggressor, and why could the British government still not say whether it would fight?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ten-kilometer withdrawal | p. 77 | The French order of 30 July pulling troops back from the whole German frontier: a military risk taken for its political effect on England. |
| Covering troops | p. 79 | The frontier corps Joffre demanded be deployed before mobilisation; he said that without them Germany would enter France without firing a shot. |
| Carnet B | p. 80 | The list of agitators to be arrested on mobilisation; the Cabinet declined to use it, and most on it later volunteered. |
| Franco-Russian military terms | pp. 80-81 | The 1892 text, never shown to the French Parliament, obliging both to mobilise and attack Germany at once. |
| Little Englanders | p. 83 | The majority of the Liberal Cabinet, heirs of Gladstone, opposed to war for France. |
| Liberal Imperialists | p. 83 | Asquith, Grey, Haldane and Churchill, who held that Britain's interest required preserving France. |
| United government | p. 83 | Britain's prime necessity: with no conscription, a split Cabinet would wreck recruiting. |
| War Station | pp. 84-85 | Scapa Flow; Churchill sent the fleet there on 28 July, before any decision for war. |
| Precautionary Period | pp. 85-86 | The British device allowing preparations without Cabinet approval. |
| Legal fiction of no commitment | p. 87 | Her verdict on the British position: war plans are a continuation of policy or they are nothing. |

## Leading propositions

- [interpretation] One aim governed French policy: to enter the war with England as an ally, which required that Germany be seen to attack (p. 77).
- [narrative] The Cabinet ordered the ten-kilometer withdrawal on 30 July and reaffirmed it on 1 August under threat of court-martial (pp. 77-78, 81-82).
- [narrative] Joffre pressed for mobilisation daily, warning that each day's delay would cost fifteen to twenty kilometers of territory (p. 81).
- [narrative] France answered the German demand with the formula that she would act in accordance with her interests, and mobilised at 4 p.m. on 1 August (pp. 81-82).
- [interpretation] Britain's difficulty came from a split within the Cabinet and between the parties, dating from the Boer War (p. 83).
- [interpretation] Grey's sentence about German domination being disagreeable contains all of British policy (p. 83).
- [narrative] On 1 August twelve of eighteen ministers opposed assuring France of support (p. 84).
- [narrative] Churchill kept the fleet together on 26 July and sent it to Scapa Flow on the 28th, acting ahead of his government (pp. 84-85).
- [narrative] The City was aghast at war and opposed intervention (pp. 86-87).
- [interpretation] The Anglo-French plans differed from other alliances only by a legal fiction; those who disliked the policy shut their eyes (p. 87).
- [narrative] Cambon protested that France had left her coasts open on the strength of the naval arrangement (p. 87).
- [interpretation] Grey knew the peace group would move only for Belgium, and expected Germany to supply the occasion (pp. 87, 89).
- [narrative] On the night of 1 August Churchill mobilised the fleet against the Cabinet's decision, and Grey promised to bar the German fleet from the Channel (pp. 88-89).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 83) no conscription + (p. 83) a seceding peace party would hurt recruiting.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 83) Asquith had to wait for an event that would unite the Cabinet. Delay was policy, not drift.
- **Premises**: (p. 87) nine years of detailed joint planning + (p. 87) the French fleet's redeployment.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 87) Britain was bound in honour while denying it. This is Tuchman's own judgment, in her own voice.
- **Premises**: (p. 77) France needs England + (p. 83) England needs Belgium as the cause.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 89) both waited on a German act that the Schlieffen plan made certain.

## Evidence offered

- Messimy's and Viviani's memoirs and Poincaré's for the French Cabinet (pp. 78-82).
- Joffre's memoranda and ultimatum to the Cabinet (pp. 79, 81).
- Morley's memorandum on the Cabinet split, Asquith's diary, Churchill's World Crisis, Grey's memoirs (pp. 83-89).
- Cabinet and caucus vote counts, and a verse from Punch (p. 84).
- Cambon's words to Opposition leaders and to The Times (pp. 87-88).
- Sourcing: the endnotes draw almost entirely on participants' memoirs and diaries: Messimy, Poincaré, Joffre, Morley, Churchill, Grey, Asquith, Bertie, Nicolson's biography. The British Cabinet is seen mostly through Morley, a dissenter, and Churchill, an activist. She notes one disputed point of timing in Grey's promise and declines to resolve it (pp. 88-89). ## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Contrast with ch06: France subordinated its military plan to a political aim; Germany did the opposite.
- The no-commitment formula of ch04 (p. 55) is here called a fiction (p. 87).
- Joffre unshaken by the withdrawal (p. 78) is set against Moltke broken (ch06, pp. 75-76).
- She presents French policy as purely reactive. The Russian alliance terms (pp. 80-81) show France was bound to attack Germany if Russia was attacked, a point she reports without weighing it against the image of France as the attacked party.

## Open questions

- Would an earlier, plain British declaration have changed German calculations? The chapter implies Grey could not give one; it does not ask whether that mattered in Berlin.
- How much did the City's opposition (pp. 86-87) weigh with Lloyd George?
