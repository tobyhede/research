# ch12: BEF to the Continent (pp. 164–174)

## Question this chapter answers

Why did the British Expeditionary Force go to France late, smaller than planned, and under orders that pulled two ways?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| War Council | p. 164 | The Committee of Imperial Defence sitting on 5 August; unlike the continental plans, the British plan needed its approval before it could run. |
| Kitchener | pp. 164-165 | The colossus made Secretary for War for his prestige; the one man who foresaw a long war and the German sweep west of the Meuse, and who despised the plan. |
| BEF / Expeditionary Force | p. 165 | Six regular divisions and cavalry, planned by Wilson to stand on the French left at Maubeuge; in Kitchener's eyes too small to decide anything and too precious to lose. |
| Tack on | p. 165 | Kitchener's scorn for attaching the British Army to the tail of French strategy. |
| Armies of millions | pp. 165-166 | Kitchener's forecast: seventy divisions, full strength in the third year, hence a war of at least three years. |
| Territorials | p. 166 | The volunteer home-defence force, which Kitchener rated at zero, unjustly in her view. |
| Sir John French | pp. 168-169 | Commander in Chief chosen for rank and reputation; brave, unintellectual, mercurial, swayed by people and moods. |
| Henry Wilson | pp. 165, 170-171 | Author of the plan and its defender; closest to the French, has French's ear, and feuds with Kitchener. |
| Haig | pp. 169, 172 | Polished corps commander who doubts French's fitness and thinks himself the better choice. |
| Amiens versus Maubeuge | pp. 167-168 | The dispute over concentration: Kitchener wants to stage seventy miles back; the generals and the French insist on the forward position. |
| Invasion scare | p. 170 | The overnight alarm that cut the force from six divisions to four. |
| Kitchener's instructions | pp. 173-174 | Orders to support the French while keeping losses to a minimum and remaining an entirely independent command. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] British disagreement delayed the covering of Lanrezac's left; the plan had to be approved by the War Council on 5 August (p. 164).
- [interpretation] Having had no share in the prewar planning, Kitchener could see the BEF in proportion and the war whole (p. 165).
- [narrative] Kitchener told the Council to prepare armies of millions for several years; it did not believe him (pp. 165-167).
- [narrative] He wanted the regulars preserved as the training nucleus of a mass army and thought throwing them away at once criminal folly (pp. 165-166).
- [interpretation] He got the War Office for his prestige, not his opinions, which no one else held (pp. 166-167).
- [narrative] He foresaw the German envelopment west of the Meuse and proposed concentrating at Amiens instead of Maubeuge (pp. 167-168).
- [narrative] Sir John French was nominally responsible for the joint plans, yet the French plan of campaign was practically unknown to him, as was the German (p. 168).
- [interpretation] French's leading trait was responsiveness to pressures, people and prejudices, against Joffre's solidity (p. 169).
- [narrative] On 5 August French proposed Antwerp; Churchill vetoed it on naval grounds; six divisions were agreed, then cut to four the next day after an invasion scare (pp. 169-170).
- [narrative] The force of about 80,000 crossed from 9 August unescorted and unobserved; the Germans did not learn where it was until Mons (pp. 171-172).
- [narrative] On 11 August French first learned of the German use of reserves from Intelligence, too late to change anything (p. 172).
- [narrative] On 12 August Kitchener argued for three hours against six British and French officers, gave way, and Asquith backed the staffs (pp. 172-173).
- [interpretation] This was another victory for the momentum of predetermined plans (p. 173).
- [interpretation] Kitchener's instructions set a policy and a mission that could not be reconciled, and cancelled unity of command; given French's temperament they practically nullified the order to cooperate (pp. 173-174).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 165) Kitchener stood outside the planning + (pp. 165-166) he reckoned in seventy-division armies and years.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 167-168) he read both the length of the war and the German right wing correctly, where the planners, caught in timetables, did not.
- **Premises**: (p. 169) rail and shipping arrangements could not be shifted without serious consequences + (p. 173) the plan had stood for five years and the troops were already at sea + (p. 173) Asquith deferred to united expert opinion.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 173) the plan went forward unchanged in destination because there was no time to make another.
- **Premises**: (p. 173) Kitchener kept direction of the war effort and wanted to limit liability + (pp. 173-174) he ordered both support of the French and minimum loss under independent command + (p. 169) French was mercurial.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 174) cooperation with the French was undermined from the start, with effects that outlasted both men.
- **Premises**: (pp. 170-172) Kitchener and Wilson detested each other + Haig distrusted French, Murray and Wilson.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 170-171) personal antipathies would not aid the fortunes of the BEF.

## Evidence offered

- Kitchener's statement to the Council and Grey's later puzzlement at how he reached it (pp. 165-167).
- Establishment figures: six regular divisions and one cavalry division at home, fourteen Territorial divisions, a Reserve of about 300,000 (p. 166).
- Wilson's and Haig's diaries for both Councils and for their opinions of colleagues (pp. 169-173).
- George V, Esher and Grey on French's character (pp. 168-169).
- Callwell's story of the Intelligence chief producing reserve divisions like goldfish (p. 172).
- The text of Kitchener's instructions to French (pp. 173-174).
- Embarkation figures and the official historian's verdict on the quality of the force (p. 171).
- Officers' recollections of the crossing and the march to Mons (pp. 171, 174).
- Sourcing: the endnotes (p. 395) give Edmonds's official history for facts and the Wilson and Haig diaries for quotation, with Churchill, Haldane, Esher, Huguet and Arthur for the Councils and Magnus's biography for Kitchener's words. The Council scenes are therefore seen largely through Wilson, a hostile witness to Kitchener, and Haig, an interested critic of French; Tuchman reverses Wilson's verdicts while using his facts. Kitchener left no account, so his reasoning is reconstructed from biographers (Magnus, Arthur, Esher) and from his later vindication. The instructions are a primary document from Edmonds's appendix.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The plan's momentum (p. 173) is the British case of the rule stated in ch11 (p. 143) and shown for Germany in ch06.
- Ch04 described Wilson's planning with the French and the absence of political commitment; here the cost appears: a plan no minister had examined and that could not be altered.
- She compares Kitchener's contradictory orders to Churchill's to the Mediterranean fleet in ch10 (p. 173).
- The reserves point (p. 172) repeats the French error of ch03 and ch11.
- Kitchener is praised for foresight and blamed for the instructions (p. 174). Both follow from the same aim of saving the army; she holds the two judgments together without resolving them.
- The instructions and French's temperament set up the retreat and the crisis of ch19 to ch21.

## Open questions

- Was Kitchener's foresight reasoned, as she asserts (pp. 167-168), or is that reconstruction after the event? Contemporaries could not say.
- Would Amiens have been the better concentration point, given that the BEF's forward position at Mons later mattered to Lanrezac's flank?
- How much did the cut from six to four divisions matter in the first battles?
