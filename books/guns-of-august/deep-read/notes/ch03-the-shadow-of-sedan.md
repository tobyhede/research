# ch03: The Shadow of Sedan (pp. 34–47)

## Question this chapter answers

Why did France, knowing a German sweep through Belgium was possible, plan an offensive into Lorraine and leave most of the Belgian frontier unguarded?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sedan / "Again" | pp. 34-35 | The defeat of 1870 and the loss of Alsace-Lorraine; the wish to reverse it was the most basic factor in French policy for forty years. |
| Élan vital | pp. 36-37 | Bergson's all-conquering will, adopted as the weapon that would offset Germany's larger population. |
| Offensive à outrance | p. 38 | Grandmaison's doctrine of attack to the limit: seize the initiative, impose one's will, discard the defensive. |
| Sureté | p. 38 | Foch's term for protection and the practical side of war; the half of his teaching that Grandmaison dropped. |
| Field Regulations of 1913 | p. 39 | The army's governing doctrine: no law but the offensive; battle as a struggle of morale; no mention of firepower. |
| Cran | p. 39 | Nerve or guts; the banner under which the French army went to war. |
| Plan 17 | pp. 38-39, 45 | Adopted May 1913: a plan of deployment for two offensives either side of Metz, with no stated objective or timetable; flexible in everything except the intention to attack. |
| Les réserves, c'est zéro | p. 40 | The officer corps' dogma that reservists were unfit for the line, which led the Staff to assume the Germans would not use theirs. |
| Michel's plan | pp. 39-40 | The 1911 proposal to defend a line Verdun-Namur-Antwerp with a million men, using reserves; rejected as insane and its author removed. |
| Joffre | p. 43 | The engineer chosen as chief in 1911: silent, phlegmatic, politically safe, supremely confident. |
| The chapel | p. 45 | The clique of Foch's disciples around Joffre who wrote Plan 17. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] The French Staff knew of a possible German right wing through Belgium and reasoned that a strong right meant a weak centre, which they would break (p. 34).
- [interpretation] The offensive doctrine grew out of the need to recover from 1870: a weaker nation needed a weapon of spirit (pp. 36-37).
- [interpretation] Foch taught both will and prudence; his followers took the will and left the prudence, and that set a trap for France (p. 38).
- [narrative] Grandmaison's 1911 lectures led to the 1913 Field Regulations and Plan 17 (pp. 38-39).
- [interpretation] French doctrine changed and French geography did not; the frontier still called for a defensive (p. 39).
- [narrative] General Michel in 1911 forecast the German line of attack accurately and proposed to meet it with reserves in the line; Messimy and the War Council dismissed him (pp. 39-42).
- [interpretation] Being right and overruled is not forgiven; Michel paid for his foresight (p. 42).
- [narrative] The army refused to give up red trousers, a decision Messimy later blamed for heavy losses (p. 42).
- [narrative] Plan 17 put five armies between Belfort and Hirson and left the frontier from Hirson to the sea undefended (pp. 45-46).
- [narrative] A German staff officer had sold France an early version of the Schlieffen plan in 1904, with the true route (p. 46).
- [narrative] Joffre and Castelnau judged a strong German force west of the Meuse impossible, counting twenty-six corps with no reserves in the line (p. 46).
- [narrative] By 1913 French intelligence had evidence that the Germans would use reserves as active troops (p. 47).
- [interpretation] The authors of Plan 17 rejected that evidence because their hopes and training were fixed on the offensive (p. 47).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 40) reserves are worthless + (pp. 40-41) the Germans think so too + (p. 46) so Germany has only about twenty-six corps for the front.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 46-47) Germany cannot be strong on the right and in the centre at once, so a wide sweep helps France. The first premise was dogma and the second was false (ch02, p. 32).
- **Premises**: (pp. 36-37) France is weaker in numbers + (p. 37) will can offset material.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 39) attack is the only law. Tuchman's counter is geography (p. 39) and the missing firepower (p. 39).
- **Premises**: (p. 47) evidence for the German right wing existed + (p. 47) it argued for the defensive.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 47) the Staff disbelieved it for that reason. This is her explanation of motive, backed by Castelnau's reported phrase.

## Evidence offered

- Castelnau's interview with General Lebas over Lille, used to open and close the chapter (pp. 34, 47).
- The text of the Field Regulations and of Plan 17's five-sentence directive (pp. 39, 45).
- Messimy's memoirs for Michel's dismissal, the red trousers and the choice of Joffre (pp. 41-43).
- The 1904 purchase of the German plan (p. 46); the Belgian attaché Melotte's report and Moltke's 1913 critique (p. 47).
- Dissenters outside the Staff: Jaurès and Colonel Grouard (pp. 46-47).
- Sourcing: the endnotes cite the French official history (Les Armées Françaises) and Joffre's memoirs for the plan, and the postwar Briey parliamentary inquiry plus Messimy's Souvenirs for the Michel affair. Messimy and Michel were both defending their records after the disaster, so the story of the overruled prophet comes largely from interested witnesses. ## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Mirror of ch02: Germany had a rigid plan with a fixed timetable; France had a fixed intention and no plan of operations (p. 45).
- Both staffs shared the short-war, decisive-battle premise (ch02, p. 27; here p. 38).
- The mistaken count of German corps (p. 46) is the root of the defeats in ch13 and ch14 and of Lanrezac's warnings.
- Michel reappears as Military Governor of Paris (p. 42) and is replaced by Gallieni in ch19 and ch20.
- She presents Foch as two-sided (pp. 37-38) and blames the doctrine's excess on Grandmaison, which protects the later hero of the Marne.

## Open questions

- Would Michel's defensive line have served better, or does hindsight make his plan look sounder than it was? No one tested his mixing of reserves.
- How much of the cult of the offensive was doctrine and how much army politics against the nation in arms (p. 40)?
