# ch02: "Let the Last Man on the Right Brush the Channel with His Sleeve" (pp. 25–34)

## Question this chapter answers

Why did Germany plan to attack France through neutral Belgium, what did the plan assume, and how had it changed by 1914?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Schlieffen plan | pp. 26-27 | The 1906 plan: six weeks and seven-eighths of the army to destroy France by a right-wing envelopment through Belgium, one-eighth to hold Russia meanwhile. |
| Belgian neutrality | p. 25 | The 1839 treaty, Palmerston's work, guaranteed by England, France, Russia, Prussia and Austria; to Schlieffen an unimportant obstacle. |
| Military necessity | p. 26 | The General Staff's sole criterion; whether a step was wise for policy or opinion was treated as irrelevant (pp. 28-29). |
| Decisive battle | p. 27 | Clausewitz's first object in offensive war: a quick annihilating victory. Its opposite, attrition, was the thing most feared. |
| Envelopment / Cannae | pp. 27-28 | Hannibal's double envelopment as Schlieffen's model; under him envelopment became the fetish and frontal attack the anathema of the Staff. |
| Right wing | p. 28 | The mass crossing Belgium on both sides of the Meuse and descending on Paris by the Oise valley; everything was to go to strengthen it. |
| The sack | p. 28 | The deliberately weak left wing in Alsace-Lorraine meant to draw the French forward between Metz and the Vosges. |
| Reserves in the front line | p. 32 | Schlieffen's innovation: about twenty reserve divisions marching with the active army, which made the numbers for envelopment possible. |
| Moltke (the younger) | p. 33 | Schlieffen's successor: melancholy, cautious, a man who foresaw a long war and did not act on it; he moved strength from right to left. |
| M-day schedule | p. 33 | The fixed timetable: Liège roads open by day 12, Brussels by M-19, the French frontier M-22, Paris and victory by M-39. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] The alliance system guaranteed Germany a two-front war against France and Russia (p. 26).
- [narrative] Schlieffen chose France first because Russia could retreat into its space and would need six weeks to mobilise, by German arithmetic (pp. 26-27).
- [narrative] French frontier fortresses ruled out a frontal attack, and millions of men needed the roads and room of Belgium to get round them (p. 27).
- [interpretation] The reasoning was sound in purely military terms and ignored everything else (pp. 27, 28-29).
- [interpretation] The decision was a product of a century of German philosophy and national egoism more than of Clausewitz (p. 29).
- [interpretation] Armies prepare for the last war; Germany staked everything on a decisive battle modelled on 1866 and 1870 (p. 29).
- [narrative] Both Moltkes foresaw a long national war, yet no staff in any country planned for one (pp. 29-30).
- [interpretation] A constant of 1914 was the refusal to prepare for the harder alternative or act on what was suspected to be true (pp. 29-30).
- [narrative] The Germans expected Belgium to protest and not fight, a belief formed under Leopold II and kept under Albert (pp. 30-31).
- [narrative] The French plan forbade entering Belgium before Germany did, because they weighed the moral cost that Schlieffen ignored (p. 30).
- [narrative] Schlieffen expected British entry and wanted a quick decision before blockade could tell (p. 32).
- [narrative] Moltke kept the plan and each year shifted strength from the right wing to the left; by 1914 the split was 16 corps right, 11 centre, 8 left (p. 33).
- [interpretation] The plan provided for every contingency and lacked flexibility (p. 33).
- [narrative] In May and June 1914 Moltke said delay reduced Germany's chances and that sooner was better (p. 34).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 27) decisive battle requires speed + (p. 27) the fortress line blocks a frontal attack + (p. 27) only Belgium has the room.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 28-29) envelopment through Belgium became a necessity to the Staff, and necessity closed the question.
- **Premises**: (p. 30) Clausewitz counted material success as the way to win opinion + (p. 30) he left out the cost of moral failure.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 30) the plan carried a political hazard its authors did not price: a new enemy in Belgium and a cause for England.
- **Premises**: (pp. 29-30) a long war could not be scientifically planned + (pp. 29-30) a short war could.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 29-30) staffs planned for the war they could plan, not the one some of them expected.

## Evidence offered

- Schlieffen's memoranda and his book Cannae, quoted directly (pp. 26-28, 32).
- Moltke's 1906 statement to the Kaiser on a long wearisome struggle (p. 29).
- The Kaiser's 1904 interview with Leopold II, from the Kaiser's own report to Bülow and a Belgian aide's recollection (pp. 30-31).
- Corps and manpower figures and the day-by-day timetable (p. 33).
- Sourcing: the endnotes rest on Gerhard Ritter's study of the Schlieffen plan, Foerster, Schlieffen's own writings, Clausewitz, Bülow's memoirs, the French intelligence officer Dupont and Conrad's memoirs. The technical account follows German sources. The national-philosophy paragraph (p. 29) has no comparable documentary base; it is the author's judgment. ## Open questions
