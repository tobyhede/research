# ch17: The Flames of Louvain (pp. 260–273)

## Question this chapter answers

How did the first weeks of war, and above all German conduct in Belgium, change what people thought they were fighting about and close off a negotiated peace?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enormous hope | pp. 261, 272-273 | The widespread feeling in August 1914 that the war would purify, settle things and lead to something better; held on both sides. |
| Theory of terror | p. 263 | The German doctrine, traced to Clausewitz and applied in 1870, that pressing the civil population shortens war. |
| Schrecklichkeit | p. 267 | Frightfulness: licensed pillage, arson and shooting, intended as a substitute for the occupation troops Germany could not spare. |
| Hostages | p. 264 | Taken systematically in every town and shot when sniping was reported; the numbers demanded kept rising. |
| Franc-tireur | pp. 266-267 | To the German mind a heretic against order; to the Western mind a hero. |
| Orders from above | p. 266 | The German conviction that civilian resistance must have been organised by the Belgian government, which made every reprisal the Belgians' fault. |
| Louvain | pp. 267-268 | The university city burned from 25 to 30 August, with its library of 230,000 volumes; the climax of Belgium in world opinion. |
| Manifesto of the ninety-three | p. 271 | German intellectuals' denial that Belgium had been violated or Louvain destroyed. |
| Pact of London | p. 271 | The Allied undertaking of 4 September to make no separate peace. |
| Erzberger memorandum | pp. 271-272 | German war aims of 2 September: permanent control of the Continent, Belgium, the Channel coast, Briey-Longwy, colonies and vast reparations. |

## Leading propositions

- [interpretation] In its first twenty days the war created passions and issues that fixed its future course (pp. 260-261).
- [interpretation] The supposed deterrents, socialist brotherhood and economic interdependence, failed; nationhood swept them aside (p. 261).
- [narrative] Many on both sides welcomed the war as cleansing: Rupert Brooke, Thomas Mann (p. 261).
- [narrative] Private Germans in August already spoke of a new map of Europe with Germany at its centre (pp. 261-262).
- [interpretation] Hatred of Germany was not yet fixed in early August; what changed it was Belgium (pp. 262-263).
- [interpretation] German terror was theory put into practice, and 1870 had already shown that it deepens resistance and lengthens war (p. 263).
- [narrative] At Andenne 110 by the German count and 211 by the Belgian were shot; at Tamines about 400; at Dinant 612 bodies were identified (pp. 263-265).
- [interpretation] The more terror was applied, the more seemed to be required (p. 264).
- [interpretation] Germans overlooked their own breach of law in being in Belgium and fixed on the illegality of resistance (p. 266).
- [interpretation] The German soldier feared the civilian sniper as disorder itself; atrocity stories in the German press prepared him to run wild at a cry of snipers (p. 267).
- [narrative] Louvain burned for six days after a night panic among German troops on 25 August; who fired first was never established (pp. 267-268).
- [interpretation] The cause did not matter, since the city was burned as a deterrent and a display of might, as the German authorities themselves announced (pp. 268-270).
- [interpretation] The gesture meant to frighten the world convinced it that no settlement with such an enemy was possible (p. 270).
- [narrative] Erzberger admitted the plea of military necessity was insufficient against Belgium (p. 270).
- [interpretation] After the Pact of London both sides were locked into fighting for total victory, each needing gains to justify its losses (p. 271).
- [narrative] German aims as set down on 2 September and Allied aims as stated by Sazonov on 20 August were each beyond compromise (pp. 271-272).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 263) terror is meant to shorten war + (pp. 263-265) it was applied systematically + (p. 270) it turned neutral and Allied opinion.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 263, 270) terror lengthened the war it was meant to shorten. This is the chapter's central claim.
- **Premises**: (p. 271) the Allies bound themselves to no separate peace + (pp. 271-272) each side's aims required the other's defeat.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 271) a negotiated peace was closed off within the first month. This supports the Afterword's trap (af01, p. 369) from the side of opinion and aims.
- **Premises**: (p. 267) Goethe's remark on injustice and disorder + (p. 267) a state founded on obedience.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 267) Germans could not conceive resistance without orders. This is national psychology argued from a quotation and an anecdote.

## Evidence offered

- Verhaeren's dedication (p. 260); Brooke and Mann (p. 261); Shaw and Wells (p. 262).
- Bülow's own placard announcing the shooting at Andenne (pp. 263-264); gravestone counts at Tamines (p. 264).
- Hausen's memoirs for Dinant, quoted against him (pp. 265-266).
- Walter Bloem, a reserve officer in Kluck's army, on hostage-taking and atrocity rumours; she calls his account invaluable (pp. 264, 267).
- American witnesses at Louvain: Whitlock, Gibson, Richard Harding Davis, Cobb (pp. 264, 268-269).
- The German statements that Louvain was punished by destruction (pp. 268-270).
- The Kriegsbrauch, the German war manual, on destroying the enemy's material and intellectual resources (p. 270).
- The Erzberger memorandum and Paléologue's record of Sazonov (pp. 271-272).
- Sourcing: I did not read this chapter's endnotes. The massacres are documented from German commanders' own words and from neutral American observers, which is the strongest evidence in the book. The attribution of terror to Clausewitz (p. 263) and the account of the German mind (p. 267) are interpretation. She does not take up the Allied atrocity propaganda that later made these events disputed.

## Open questions

- Was the terror centrally directed or the sum of local panics licensed from above? Her evidence shows both (pp. 264, 267-268).
- How far were Allied war aims in August (p. 272) as uncompromising as the German? She says both, and gives the German list far more space.
