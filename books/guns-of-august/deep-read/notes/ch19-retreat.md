# ch19: Retreat (pp. 287–313)

## Question this chapter answers

From 24 to 28 August, after the frontiers, how did the French and British armies fall back without breaking, who took charge of Paris, and why did the German advance begin to thin?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General Order No. 2 | p. 287 | Joffre's order of 25 August: form a new Sixth Army on the left from Lorraine, hold on the Somme, resume the offensive by 2 September. |
| Fight in retreat | p. 288 | The order of the day: rearguard actions to delay the enemy and keep a continuous front. |
| Sixth Army | pp. 287, 297 | Maunoury's new force, built round the Army of Lorraine and reserve divisions and railed to Amiens. |
| Gallieni | p. 293 | Named Military Governor of Paris on 26 August: sixty-five, ill, beyond ambition; he demanded three active corps to defend the city beyond its perimeter. |
| Three active corps | pp. 294-295 | The army Gallieni required and Messimy ordered Joffre to supply; Joffre ignored the order. |
| Zone of the Armies | pp. 294-295 | The area where the 1913 decrees gave the Commander in Chief absolute power; Messimy had conceded Joffre the whole military conduct of the war. |
| Le Cateau | pp. 301-303 | Smith-Dorrien's stand of 26 August against orders, which saved the BEF's left wing at a cost of over 8,000 men. |
| Wheel inwards | p. 306 | Kluck's idea of 28 August to turn against the French Fifth Army's flank; OHL's order of the same day allowed for it. |
| Foch Detachment | p. 309 | Three corps under Foch to fill the thirty-mile gap between the Fourth and Fifth Armies; later the Ninth Army. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] A million Germans entered France on 24 August on a front seventy-five miles wide; the left wing made no breakthrough in Lorraine (p. 287).
- [interpretation] In the twelve days that followed the Germans came close enough to victory to touch it (pp. 287-288).
- [interpretation] The French fought better in retreat than in the offensive: on their own soil, entrenching, with rearguards (pp. 288, 291).
- [narrative] Paris's defences existed on paper; work had been put off out of faith in the offensive (pp. 291-292).
- [narrative] Gallieni told Messimy to expect the Germans before Paris in twelve days (p. 292).
- [narrative] Messimy, who had ceded Joffre full military authority, ordered him to send three corps to Paris; Joffre ignored it and Messimy was forced out of office (pp. 294-297).
- [interpretation] To GQG Paris was a town like any other; Joffre held that only the field army could defend it (pp. 294, 297).
- [narrative] Sir John French decided after Mons that the campaign was lost and thought only of saving the BEF (pp. 298-299).
- [narrative] Haig's alarm at Landrecies split the two British corps for seven days (p. 300).
- [narrative] Smith-Dorrien stood at Le Cateau because he could not get away; Kluck's flanking corps were held off by French Territorials and cavalry (pp. 301-302).
- [narrative] BEF casualties in its first five days were just under 15,000 (p. 304).
- [narrative] OHL announced on 27 August that the enemy was beaten along the whole line (pp. 304-305).
- [narrative] The right wing was by 28 August five corps short: two left in Belgium, two sent to Russia, one investing fortresses (p. 307).
- [interpretation] OHL kept the left wing at twenty-six divisions, about equal to the whole diminished right wing, which was the opposite of Schlieffen's proportion (p. 307).
- [narrative] Castelnau and Dubail held and counterattacked at the Charmes gap from 24 to 27 August (p. 308).
- [narrative] Joffre ordered Lanrezac to turn and attack toward St. Quentin; Lanrezac called it almost insane and Joffre threatened to relieve him (pp. 309-311).
- [narrative] On 28 August Sir John French ordered ammunition thrown away to speed the retreat and moved his base toward St. Nazaire (pp. 311-313).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 307) five corps detached + (p. 307) the left wing kept whole + (p. 307) the same ground to cover.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 307) the right wing had to thin or open gaps. This is the arithmetic behind Kluck's turn (ch21, p. 334) and the Afterword's ifs (af01, p. 366).
- **Premises**: (pp. 288, 291) French troops entrenched and fought rearguards + (p. 288) the line was pierced and not broken.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 288) this was a defeated army and not a routed one, contrary to the German communiqués (pp. 304-305).
- **Premises**: (pp. 294-295) Messimy had given Joffre the whole conduct of operations + (p. 297) Joffre treated the Paris order as interference.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 297) the civil government could name a governor for Paris and could not give him an army.

## Evidence offered

- Diaries of French infantry and cavalry officers and of a German officer for the retreat (pp. 288-290).
- Messimy's account of his own night composing the order, and of the Cabinet scene (pp. 295-296).
- Gallieni's diary and words to Messimy (pp. 293-294).
- Smith-Dorrien's account of his decision; Sir John French's dispatch crediting him, set against his later vendetta (pp. 301, 303-304, 313).
- Huguet's telegrams on the state of the BEF, which she says reflect British headquarters and not the troops (pp. 303, 310).
- OHL's communiqué and order of 27 and 28 August; Tappen on the hoped-for encirclement (pp. 304-307).
- Hausen's memoirs on his billets, quoted for comic effect (p. 305).
- Sourcing: I did not read this chapter's endnotes. The Paris story rests on Messimy and Gallieni, both at odds with Joffre. The British headquarters is seen through Smith-Dorrien, Spears-era staff recollections and Wilson's own words; French is judged harshly and by his enemies. The count of five missing corps is hers, assembled from the German accounts.

## Quotes

- "the fortifications existed on paper but nothing had been done on the ground" (pp. 291-292)
