# af01: Afterword (pp. 365–370)

Tuchman's own summing-up. The Preface names its closing paragraph as her best statement of the war's meaning (fm02, p. 10). It is the one place where the thesis is stated outright.

## Question this chapter answers

What did the Battle of the Marne decide, why did it turn out as it did, and what did the first month fix for the rest of the war and after?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Miracle of the Marne | pp. 365-366 | The popular name for the battle that turned the German advance; she denies it was a miracle and assigns it to the errors and commitments of the first month. |
| The ifs | p. 366 | Her list of contingencies: the two corps sent east, the overstrong German left, the right wing outrunning supply, Kluck's position, and first of all the march through Belgium. |
| The gap | p. 365 | The opening between the German First and Second Armies made when Kluck turned to face Maunoury. |
| Eastern door | pp. 366-367 | The Moselle front held by Castelnau and Dubail for eighteen days, which freed divisions for the Marne. |
| Incomplete victory | pp. 365, 368 | The Marne stopped Germany and failed to break it, so the war continued. |
| Deadlock | p. 368 | The trench stalemate from Switzerland to the Channel, produced jointly by the failure of Plan 17 and of the Schlieffen plan. |
| Second Round | p. 368 | The war of 1939, whose conditions she traces to the deadlock. |
| Disillusion | p. 369 | The dominant result of the war: the great words of the pre-1914 world could not be restored. |
| The trap | p. 369 | Her closing image: indecisive battles in the first thirty days left the nations in a war they could not leave. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] The Marne ended in a German retreat; in the four days left of their schedule the Germans lost the decisive victory (p. 365).
- [interpretation] The tragedy of the Marne was that it fell short of the victory it might have been (p. 365).
- [narrative] The issue turned on whether the Germans could crush Maunoury and Foch before Franchet d'Esperey and the British exploited the gap; the British entered it slowly, Hentsch counselled retreat, and the Germans withdrew in time (p. 365).
- [interpretation] It was the ifs, errors and commitments of the first month that decided the battle, not a miracle (p. 366).
- [interpretation] German command faults counted as much as French recovery (p. 366).
- [interpretation] The two corps withdrawn for the Russian front might have filled the gap and overwhelmed Foch; Russia's unready offensive drew them away (p. 366).
- [interpretation] The first and decisive if was the plan's dependence on Belgium, which cost German divisions and added five British ones to the Allied line (p. 366).
- [interpretation] The Marne was made possible on the Moselle, where the French First and Second Armies held (pp. 366-367).
- [interpretation] Joffre's confidence kept the armies whole in retreat; Gallieni saw the opportunity; Lanrezac's decision at Charleroi saved France from Plan 17 (p. 367).
- [narrative] About 600 taxis carried some 6,000 men to the Ourcq (pp. 367-368).
- [narrative] The Aisne, the race to the sea, Antwerp and Ypres followed, then trench warfare for four more years (p. 368).
- [interpretation] Plan 17 was responsible for the Germans' presence deep in France, an error that could never be repaired (p. 368).
- [interpretation] The deadlock set the course of the war and, through it, the peace terms, the inter-war period and the conditions of the next war (p. 368).
- [interpretation] The Marne was decisive because it determined that the war would go on (p. 369).
- [narrative] French casualties in August were 206,515 by the official history, believed nearer 300,000; war dead per head of population were 1 in 28 for France and 1 in 32 for Germany (pp. 369-370).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 366) the plan required Belgium + (p. 366) Belgium cost divisions, time, British entry and opinion + (p. 366) Russia's early attack drew off two corps.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 366) the German right arrived at the Marne too weak, so the six-week victory failed for reasons built into the plan.
- **Premises**: (p. 368) Plan 17 let the Germans penetrate too far to be dislodged + (p. 368) the Schlieffen plan failed to finish France.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 368) two failed offensives produced a front neither side could move.
- **Premises**: (p. 368) the deadlock consumed Allied resources and doomed back-door efforts + (p. 369) no battle of the first thirty days was decisive.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 368-369) the first month fixed the war's length and, at one remove, the peace and the next war. The second half of this chain is asserted in one sentence and not argued.

## Evidence offered

- Moltke's letter to his wife and Kluck's postwar verdict on the French soldier's power of recovery (p. 366).
- Colonel Dupont's tribute to the Russian sacrifice (p. 366).
- Foch on Joffre (p. 367).
- Casualty figures from the French official history, with her caution that lists were not published and that other belligerents counted differently (pp. 369-370).
- Sourcing: the judgments on German failure rest on the losers' own later explanations (Moltke, Kluck) and on French testimony. The counterfactuals are hers. The casualty note is careful about its limits.

## Quotes

- "the tragedy of the Marne was that it fell short of the victory it might have been" (p. 365)
- "it was no miracle but the inherent ifs, errors, and commitments of the first month that determined the issue at the Marne" (p. 366)
- "If there was a miracle of the Marne, it was made possible on the Moselle." (pp. 366-367)
- "Without Joffre no Allied line would have existed to bar the German path." (p. 367)
- "Failure of Plan 17 was as fatal as failure of the Schlieffen plan, and together they produced deadlock on the Western Front." (p. 368)
- "because it determined that the war would go on" (p. 369)
- "a trap from which there was, and has been, no exit" (p. 369)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Pays off ch02 (the weakened right wing, p. 33) and ch03 (Plan 17) by naming both plans as joint causes of the deadlock (p. 368).
- The transfer of two corps east (p. 366) links the Western story to East Prussia (ch15, ch16) and to the Russian promise of ch05 (p. 58).
- Belgium as the first if (p. 366) links to ch02 (pp. 28-31), ch08 and ch09 (p. 110).
- The battle itself is summarised in a single paragraph here (p. 365).
- Massie's foreword says she drew no moral conclusions (fm01, p. 6); the trap passage (p. 369) is a conclusion.
- Tension: the Afterword praises Joffre as indispensable (p. 367) and blames Plan 17, his plan, as an irreparable error (p. 368).

## Open questions

- How much weight can single counterfactuals carry? Each if is plausible alone; she stacks five.
- The line from the deadlock of 1914 to the conditions of 1939 (p. 368) skips everything between. It is a thesis about the book's importance more than a finding of the book.
- The 1 in 107 figure for Russia (p. 370) sits oddly beside Russian losses described elsewhere; she flags that the bases of counting differ.
