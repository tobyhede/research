# ch05: The Russian Steam Roller (pp. 57–67)

## Question this chapter answers

What did the Allies and Germany expect of Russia, what had Russia promised France, and what was the real condition of the Russian army and regime?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steam roller | p. 57 | The myth of Russia's irresistible mass: slow to start, then unstoppable through sheer numbers; a comfort to France and Britain and a dread to Germany. |
| M-15 offensive | p. 58 | The French demand, accepted by Russia in Staff talks from 1911, to attack East Prussia by about the fifteenth day of mobilisation with whatever was ready. |
| This insane regime | pp. 59-60 | Witte's phrase for Czarist government: an idle autocrat, court favourites, secret police and an irresponsible bureaucracy. |
| Sukhomlinov | p. 61 | Minister of War 1908 to 1914: lazy, corrupt, hostile to fire tactics and to reform; the personification of the regime's unreadiness. |
| Fire tactics | p. 61 | The doctrine that firepower decides modern battle, which Sukhomlinov called a vicious innovation. |
| Grand Duke Nicholas | p. 64 | The admired reforming soldier, anti-German and pro-French, hated at court; designated to command against Germany. |
| Two-pronged invasion | p. 65 | The Russian plan for East Prussia: First Army north and Second Army south of the Masurian Lakes, converging behind the Germans. |
| Hoffmann | pp. 66-67 | The German Staff's Russia specialist: indolent, quick, irreverent; he worked out the likely Russian plan. |
| Schlieffen's eastern formula | p. 67 | Strike with all available strength at the first Russian army that comes within reach. |

## Leading propositions

- [interpretation] Belief in Russian invincibility survived a record of defeats; numbers alone inspired awe (p. 57).
- [narrative] France needed Russia to draw off German forces from the first moment so that Plan 17 could break the German centre (p. 58).
- [narrative] Russia agreed in 1911 to attack on M-16, and in 1912 and 1913 improved on that promise, though its railways and munitions output could not support it (p. 58).
- [interpretation] The Russian Staff, like the French, had caught the doctrine of the offensive as compensation for defeat (p. 58).
- [narrative] Russian soldiers travelled on average 700 miles to mobilise, four times the German distance, on a tenth of the rail density (pp. 58-59).
- [interpretation] The Allies believed that getting the giant moving was all that mattered, whatever its efficiency (pp. 58-59).
- [interpretation] The army had been partly reformed after 1905 and the regime had not (p. 59).
- [narrative] Sukhomlinov stopped reform, subordinated the General Staff, went through six Chiefs of Staff in six years and did not spend the munitions budget (pp. 63-64).
- [narrative] Russia began the war with 850 shells per gun against 2,000 to 3,000 in Western armies, and 60 heavy batteries against Germany's 381 (pp. 63-64).
- [narrative] The plan for East Prussia depended on two armies timing their advance so that neither could be engaged alone (p. 65).
- [narrative] East Prussia's railways and prepared positions gave the defender mobility between the two wings (p. 66).
- [narrative] The Germans had deduced the Russian plan from the terrain and meant to strike one wing before the other (p. 67).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 58) France needs an early second front + (p. 58) Russia cannot be ready by M-15 + (p. 58) Russia promises it anyway.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 58-59) Russia was committed to an offensive begun before concentration was complete. The cost is shown in ch15 and ch16.
- **Premises**: (p. 65) the Russian pincer needs exact timing + (p. 59) systematic organisation was not a Russian strength + (p. 66) the defender has interior railways.
  **Conclusion**: implied: the plan was set up to fail in the way it did.
- **Premises**: (pp. 59-60) government by whim and favourites + (pp. 61-63) a war minister chosen for amusing the Czar.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 63-64) the army's material shortages were the regime's product, not accidents.

## Evidence offered

- Manpower, railway and artillery statistics (pp. 57, 58-59, 63-64).
- The Dubail mission and the annual Staff talks, with the Czar's signed agreement (p. 58).
- Witte, Kokovtsov and Sazonov on the Czar and on Sukhomlinov (pp. 59-61).
- Sukhomlinov's finances, the Altschiller and Myasoedev spy cases and the 1917 trial (pp. 62-63).
- Paléologue's diary for court scenes (pp. 61-62, 65).
- Hoffmann's memoirs, which she calls not always wholly serious (p. 67).
- Sourcing: endnotes draw on memoirs of Russian ministers written in exile (Witte, Kokovtsov, Sazonov), on the French ambassador Paléologue, on Danilov and Agourtine for the army, and on Hoffmann. Almost every witness to Sukhomlinov is an enemy or a post-revolution court. The picture of Russia is drawn mainly from French, British and émigré eyes. (This paragraph rests on the attributions in the text and on a mechanical tally of the chapter's endnotes; the endnotes were sampled, not read line by line.)

## Quotes

- "dread of the Slav at their backs haunted the Germans" (p. 57)
- "the Russians agreed, with more valor than discretion, to launch an offensive simultaneously with France" (p. 58)
- "simply to get the Russian giant in motion, regardless of how he functioned, was all that mattered" (pp. 58-59)
- "Systematic attention to detail was not a notable characteristic of the Russian Army." (p. 59)
- "Warnings that war would be largely a duel of firepower Sukhomlinov treated with contempt." (pp. 63-64)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Completes the set of four plans (ch02 to ch05). Each depends on an assumption about another power that the reader has just seen to be false.
- The premature offensive (p. 58) explains Tannenberg (ch15, ch16) and, in her argument, the German transfer of corps from the West (ch16).
- The chapter gives more space to scandal than to the army; the account of ordinary Russian military capacity is thin beside the portraits.
- Germany's assumption that Russia would need six weeks (ch02, p. 27) is contradicted by the M-15 promise here.

## Open questions

- How good were the reformed parts of the Russian army? She says many officers were brave and able (p. 59) and does not follow it up.
- Was the early offensive a French imposition or a Russian choice? Both appear (p. 58).
