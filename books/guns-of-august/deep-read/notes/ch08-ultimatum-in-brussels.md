# ch08: Ultimatum in Brussels (pp. 89–100)

## Question this chapter answers

What did Germany demand of Belgium, why did Belgium refuse knowing it would be overrun, and what did the refusal cost Germany?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The ultimatum | pp. 91-92 | The German note of 2 August, first drafted by Moltke on 26 July: free passage in return for a guarantee of independence, with war as the alternative; twelve hours to answer. |
| Benevolent neutrality | p. 92 | What Germany asked of Belgium: to let the army through. |
| Call-up without deployment | p. 90 | Belgian mobilisation of 31 July: neutrality forbade pointing the army at any one enemy. |
| King Albert | p. 93 | Reserved, studious, energetic; the one sovereign who had foreseen invasion and who led the refusal. |
| Galet | p. 94 | Albert's military adviser, a critic of the offensive doctrine; attack needs superior means. |
| Galet memorandum | p. 97 | The 1913 policy: declare war at once on any violator and fight on until a general peace. |
| Imaginary bombings | p. 99 | Reports of French air raids near Nuremberg, used to justify declaring war on France; none took place. |
| Machinery of transport | p. 99 | Moltke's reason for refusing to delay entry into Belgium. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] The ultimatum was sealed and sent on 29 July, and Below was told to pretend the instructions had just arrived (p. 89).
- [narrative] Moltke drafted it on 26 July, before Austria declared war on Serbia and before any mobilisation (pp. 91-92).
- [narrative] It alleged a French advance through Belgium for which no evidence existed (p. 92).
- [interpretation] Belgium would be occupied either way; to yield would lose honour as well (p. 92).
- [narrative] Belgium had neglected its army out of faith in the treaty: six divisions against thirty-four, no finished plan of concentration, heavy guns on order from Krupp and undelivered (pp. 93, 94-95).
- [narrative] In November 1913 the Kaiser and Moltke told Albert in Berlin that war with France was inevitable and near (pp. 95-97).
- [narrative] Albert opened the Council of State on 2 August by saying the answer must be no, whatever the consequences (p. 97).
- [interpretation] Germany's late anxiety was less about neutral opinion than about the delay Belgian resistance would cause its timetable (p. 98).
- [narrative] The Belgian reply refused on grounds of national honour and duty to Europe (pp. 98-99).
- [narrative] In Berlin the same night Tirpitz objected to declaring war on France; Moltke would allow nothing to disturb the transport schedule (p. 99).
- [narrative] Albert ordered the Meuse bridges and frontier tunnels blown, and withheld the appeal to the guarantors until invasion had occurred (pp. 99-100).
- [interpretation] Belgian neutrality was a collective act of the powers that almost succeeded (p. 100).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 92) refusal means destruction by a force ten times larger + (p. 92) acceptance means complicity, occupation and little chance of evacuation.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 92) refusal was the only course that kept anything. She presents the decision as near unanimous and immediate.
- **Premises**: (p. 98) a Belgian army that fights must be masked or besieged + (p. 98) it can destroy railways and bridges.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 98) resistance threatened the one thing the German plan could not spare, time.
- **Premises**: (pp. 91-92) the ultimatum predates the crisis's military phase + (p. 99) war was declared on France because Germany meant to cross Belgium.
  **Conclusion**: implied: the violation was planned policy, and the stated reasons were pretexts.

## Evidence offered

- The text of the ultimatum, including the deleted offer of French territory (p. 92).
- Bassompierre's record of the scene at the Foreign Office (pp. 91-92).
- Beyens's and Melotte's reports of the 1913 Berlin conversations (pp. 95-97).
- Galet's account of Belgian military planning (pp. 94-95, 97).
- Tirpitz's memoirs for the Berlin meeting of 2 August (p. 99).
- Sourcing: the endnotes rely on Belgian participants writing after the war (Bassompierre, Galet, Beyens, Crokaert, Cammaerts's life of Albert) and on the German documents and Tirpitz. The Belgian side is told by its own actors, who had every reason to stress unanimity and resolve; the hesitations appear only as an aged minister's mumbling (pp. 97-98).
