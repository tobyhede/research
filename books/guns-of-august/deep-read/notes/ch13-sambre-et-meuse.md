# ch13: Sambre et Meuse (pp. 174–196)

## Question this chapter answers

Between 15 and 20 August, as the armies closed for the first great battles, how did each command respond to evidence that the enemy was not doing what its plan assumed?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GQG | p. 176 | French General Headquarters at Vitry: Joffre with Belin and Berthelot; fixed on the offensive through the centre and closed to evidence about the German right. |
| OHL | p. 181 | German Supreme Headquarters, moved to Coblenz on 16 August; Moltke's command post, hampered by bad communications and staff friction. |
| The sack | p. 175 | Schlieffen's design for Lorraine: Rupprecht retreats, draws the French forward, and defeats them strategically while they win tactically. |
| Lanrezac | pp. 176-177 | Commander of the Fifth Army: a quick strategist who saw the envelopment coming, hectored GQG and lost faith in it. |
| Special Instruction No. 10 | p. 179 | Joffre's order of 15 August moving the Fifth Army into the angle of Sambre and Meuse, a partial concession that kept the Ardennes offensive. |
| Order No. 13 | p. 189 | The 18 August signal for the central offensive, giving Lanrezac two contradictory alternatives. |
| So much the better | pp. 177, 190 | GQG's standing answer: the stronger the German right, the weaker the centre France would break. |
| Modern Alexander | p. 181 | Schlieffen's ideal commander directing by telephone from an office; in practice Moltke, who could not get messages through. |
| Waiting attitude | p. 185 | Sir John French's sudden reluctance to commit the BEF. |
| Collective responsibility | p. 193 | The German policy of hostages, shootings and burned villages for any civilian act of hostility, printed in advance and outlawed by the Hague Convention. |
| Franc-tireur | p. 192 | The civilian sniper; the alleged cause of German reprisals. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] The French attacked in Lorraine from 14 August against prepared positions, relying on élan and the 75 and having almost no heavy field artillery (p. 175).
- [narrative] Rupprecht's Sixth and Seventh Armies fell back for four days as planned; the French took Château Salins and Sarrebourg (p. 176).
- [narrative] Lanrezac reported masses of Germans west of the Meuse; GQG said the figures must be wrong and told him his fears were premature (pp. 177-178).
- [narrative] Gallieni reached the same conclusion from War Ministry reports and was brushed off (p. 178).
- [narrative] On 15 August Joffre moved the Fifth Army toward the Sambre without giving up the Ardennes offensive, and scraped up three Territorial divisions for the gap to the sea (pp. 179-180).
- [interpretation] No report could shake GQG's conviction that a large German right meant a thin centre (p. 180).
- [interpretation] German communications in hostile country were a friction the Staff had not planned for; OHL's messages took eight to twelve hours (p. 181).
- [interpretation] From the opening of the French attack in Lorraine, Moltke's resolve to rely wholly on the right wing slipped; once the doctrine was questioned there was no return to it (p. 182).
- [narrative] After three days of argument OHL refused to forbid Rupprecht's counterattack, and the sack manoeuvre was abandoned on 18 August (pp. 183-184).
- [interpretation] Sir John French lost his will to fight on landing; she lists possible causes and declines to judge (p. 185).
- [narrative] French and Lanrezac met on 17 August without a common language and parted in anger; French said he would not be ready until the 24th (pp. 186-188).
- [narrative] King Albert, told by Joffre that the Germans west of the Meuse were a screen, withdrew the Belgian army to Antwerp on 18 August and saved it (pp. 188-190).
- [narrative] OHL, acting on a false report of British landings at Ostend, put Kluck under Bülow; Kluck resisted and the order was rescinded after ten days (pp. 190-192).
- [narrative] Belgian resistance delayed the march and cut supply and communications (p. 192).
- [interpretation] The reprisals were prepared policy meant to save time and men; they were not spontaneous (p. 193).
- [narrative] Aerschot saw 150 civilians shot on 19 August, Dinant 664; Brussels was occupied on 20 August by Kluck's 320,000 men (pp. 192, 194-195).
- [narrative] On 20 August Rupprecht's counteroffensive struck, Pau was withdrawn from Alsace, and Joffre ordered the Ardennes offensive that night (pp. 195-196).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 177) Lanrezac's reconnaissance + (p. 178) GQG's own intelligence estimate + (p. 178) Gallieni's reading.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 180) the evidence was available and GQG's doctrine turned it into encouragement. This repeats, in the field, the prewar refusal of ch03 (p. 47).
- **Premises**: (p. 182) the French massed in Lorraine + (p. 182) a double envelopment tempted OHL + (p. 184) a royal army commander demanded to attack.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 182, 184) Germany gave up the logic of its own plan on the left, committing strength to a frontal attack the plan forbade.
- **Premises**: (p. 193) proclamations printed beforehand + (p. 193) speed was essential.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 193) terror was an instrument of the timetable.

## Evidence offered

- Lanrezac's own account for his warnings and his visit to Vitry (pp. 177-178, 180).
- Rupprecht's and Krafft's records of the telephone arguments with OHL, quoted as dialogue (pp. 183-184).
- Spears's eyewitness account of the French-Lanrezac meeting; she twice says his explanations are inadequate (pp. 180, 186-187).
- Poincaré's diary and Sir John French's letters to Kitchener (pp. 185-186).
- Kluck's and Hausen's memoirs, quoted against themselves on reprisals (pp. 192-194).
- The text of German proclamations (p. 193); American correspondents for the occupation (pp. 193-194).
- Density figures per mile of front for the three German armies (pp. 191-192).
- Sourcing: I did not read this chapter's endnotes line by line. From the attributions in the text, the French command dispute rests on Lanrezac, Messimy, Gallieni and Spears, all critics of GQG; Joffre's side appears mostly in his orders. The German reprisals are documented from the commanders' own words, which is strong evidence for intent.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Both headquarters depart from their plans in the same week, in opposite ways: GQG clings (p. 180) and OHL wavers (p. 182). She criticises both, which sits beside ch02's complaint that the German plan lacked flexibility (p. 33).
- The abandoned sack (p. 184) is one of the ifs of the Afterword: too much strength committed to the left wing (af01, p. 366).
- Kluck placed under Bülow and the fear of a gap (pp. 191-192) prepare the gap at the Marne (af01, p. 365).
- The Belgian army preserved at Antwerp (pp. 190-191) is the cost of Belgium counted in af01 (p. 366).
- Lanrezac's judgment here is vindicated in the Afterword (af01, p. 367).
- The battles these orders launch are ch14.

## Open questions

- How sound were Lanrezac's numbers at the time, as opposed to in his later book? The figure of perhaps two million (p. 177) was wrong on the high side.
- Was the reprisal policy uniform across armies, or does she generalise from Kluck and Hausen?
