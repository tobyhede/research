# fm01: Foreword (Robert K. Massie) (pp. 1–7)

Everything here is Massie's account of Tuchman and her book, written for a later edition. It is a secondary voice and is not evidence for the events of 1914.

## Question this chapter answers

Why did this book, a military history of one month, earn its reputation, and how did its author work?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Four qualities | p. 2 | Massie's list of what distinguishes the book: vivid detail, clear and witty prose, detached moral judgment, and suspense that makes the reader forget the known outcome. |
| Popular history | p. 6 | The label academic reviewers applied to Tuchman's work, which Massie reports stung her; he treats it as a sneer at sales, not a finding about accuracy. |
| The why | pp. 4-5 | Tuchman's stated method as Massie quotes it: establish what happened and arrange it in narrative first; causes surface from the sequence. |
| Systems / systemizers | p. 4 | Historians who put a theory first; Massie says Tuchman had little use for them. |
| Folly | p. 2 | Massie's word for what the book shows: human folly viewed with skepticism, in contrast to villainy viewed with outrage. |

## Leading propositions

- [interpretation, Massie] The book is one of the finest histories written by an American in the twentieth century (p. 1).
- [narrative, Massie] It was an immediate success in January 1962; Kennedy gave a copy to Macmillan, saying statesmen must avoid the pitfalls that led to August 1914 (pp. 1-2).
- [narrative, Massie] The Pulitzer history prize was barred to a non-American subject by the donor's will, so the committee gave her the prize for general nonfiction (p. 2).
- [interpretation, Massie] The book is essentially military history of the war's first month, and its distinctive achievement is suspense: the reader forgets that the Germans will not reach Paris (p. 2).
- [narrative, Massie] Tuchman came from the Morgenthau and Wertheim families; her grandfather Henry Morgenthau Sr. was ambassador to Turkey during the war (pp. 2-3).
- [narrative, Massie] She had no doctorate, worked at The Nation, reported from Madrid, and had written Bible and Sword and The Zimmermann Telegram before this book (pp. 3, 5).
- [narrative, Massie] Her research method was immersion in letters, diaries, memoirs, cabinet papers and orders in the major libraries, plus a summer driving the Belgian and French battlefields (p. 4).
- [interpretation, Massie] Her aim was to find out what happened and how it felt to those present, letting the narrative force the causes to the surface (pp. 4-5).
- [interpretation, Massie] She judged with tolerance; the example given is her refusal to decide why Sir John French held back the BEF (p. 6).
- [interpretation, Massie] Though she did not write to draw morals, the book carries a lesson: incompetent leaders stumbled into a war nobody wanted, which unfolded like a Greek tragedy (pp. 6-7).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 2) the reader knows the outcome + (p. 2) the narrative nonetheless restores the uncertainty the participants felt.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 2) the book's hold on readers comes from narrative craft, on top of its factual detail.
- **Premises**: (p. 4) she distrusted systems + (pp. 4-5) she held that sequence reveals cause.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 6-7) the book's lessons are implied by the story and are not argued as a thesis. This is Massie's framing; the Preface (fm02) shows Tuchman accepting that the book has a thesis.

## Evidence offered

- Tuchman's own essays and speeches collected in Practicing History, quoted throughout (pp. 3-6).
- Her character sketches of the Kaiser, Franz Ferdinand, Schlieffen, Joffre and Sukhomlinov, offered as samples of the vivid specific fact (p. 4).
- Family recollection from her daughters (p. 7).
- Sourcing: no notes. This is an appreciation by a fellow narrative historian. It reports no criticism of the book's accuracy beyond the popular history label, so it is one-sided as a guide to reception.

## Quotes

- "one of the finest works of history written by an American in our century" (p. 1)
- "essentially a military history of the first month of the First World War" (p. 2)
- "she persuades the reader to suspend any foreknowledge of what is about to happen" (p. 2)
- "her quarry was always the vivid, specific fact" (p. 4)
- "Foolish monarchs, diplomats, and generals blundered into a war nobody wanted" (pp. 6-7)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Massie says she wrote without drawing moral conclusions (p. 6); the Afterword (af01) and the Preface's endorsement of Fadiman (fm02, pp. 9-10) show a definite thesis about what August decided.
- The "war nobody wanted" summary (pp. 6-7) is Massie's. The body of the book shows Germany's General Staff wanting a short war on its own schedule (ch02, ch06), so the phrase flattens her account.
- The French passage he quotes on Sir John French comes from ch12.

## Open questions

- How did military historians judge the book's accuracy? Massie does not say.
- Does the method of letting narrative surface the cause hide the selection that the narrator makes?
