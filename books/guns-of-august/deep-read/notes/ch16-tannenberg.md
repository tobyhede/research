# ch16: Tannenberg (pp. 245–260)

This note was written before ch15 was read. Ch15 sets the chapter up (the Russian advance, Gumbinnen, the replacement of Prittwitz by Hindenburg and Ludendorff); see that note for those events.

## Question this chapter answers

How was the Russian Second Army destroyed between 25 and 30 August, who and what won the battle for Germany, and what did it cost the German effort in the West?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intercepted wireless | p. 245 | Russian orders sent in clear, giving Rennenkampf's and Samsonov's movements; to Hoffmann the real victor of the battle (pp. 258-259). |
| Double envelopment | p. 246 | The German plan for 26 August: Mackensen and Below on Samsonov's right, François on his left, Scholtz holding the centre. |
| François's disobedience | pp. 245, 254 | The Ist Corps commander's refusal to attack before his artillery arrived and, later, to turn inward; it closed the trap. |
| Reinforcements from the West | p. 247 | OHL's offer of three corps and a cavalry division on 26 August; two corps and the cavalry were sent, taken from the right wing at Namur. |
| Jilinsky | pp. 248-249 | Commander of the Russian North-West Front, 180 miles to the rear, who drove Samsonov forward on the strength of Rennenkampf's fantasy reports. |
| Stavka | p. 250 | Russian General Headquarters at Baranovichi under the Grand Duke. |
| Hindenburg myth | p. 259 | The public's making of the nominal commander into a titan. |
| Sacrifice for an ally | p. 260 | The Grand Duke's phrase, which she adopts: the premature Russian offensive failed and still drew German strength from the West. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] On 25 August the Germans intercepted uncoded orders showing that Rennenkampf would not be near enough to interfere and that Samsonov thought he was pursuing a beaten enemy (p. 245).
- [interpretation] Hoffmann's story of a personal feud between the two Russian generals is doubtful; she questions whether he believed it himself (pp. 245-246).
- [narrative] Ludendorff lost his nerve on 26 August over Rennenkampf and was steadied by Hindenburg, by both men's accounts (pp. 246-247).
- [narrative] On 26 August Tappen telephoned to offer three corps; Ludendorff said they were not positively needed and would arrive too late (p. 247).
- [interpretation] The decision came from panic at OHL when Russia attacked in two weeks instead of six, eased by the belief that the decisive battle in the West was already won (pp. 247-248).
- [narrative] Two corps from the junction of the Second and Third Armies and a cavalry division left the Western Front on 26 August; a third corps was recalled (p. 248).
- [narrative] Samsonov's men were starving and exhausted; Jilinsky refused his request to shift west and called caution cowardice (pp. 248-249).
- [narrative] The Russian VIth Corps on the right was broken on 26 August after contradictory orders; the Ist Corps on the left fled under François's bombardment on the 27th (pp. 251, 252-253).
- [narrative] François ignored Ludendorff's order to turn inward on the 28th and drove east, cutting the line of retreat (p. 254).
- [narrative] Rennenkampf never made contact with Samsonov (p. 254).
- [narrative] Samsonov cut his communications, rode to the front, ordered retreat on the 28th and shot himself in the forest on the night of the 29th (pp. 254-257).
- [narrative] The Germans took 92,000 prisoners and 300 to 500 guns; dead and missing were estimated above 30,000; two corps ceased to exist (p. 257).
- [interpretation] Ludendorff's victory owed much to Hoffmann, François, Hindenburg and above all the Russian wireless (pp. 258-259).
- [narrative] Russia's simultaneous victory over Austria at Lemberg inflicted 250,000 casualties and crippled the Austrian army (p. 259).
- [interpretation] Tannenberg exposed Russian training, command and organisation and strengthened those who wanted a separate peace (pp. 259-260).
- [interpretation] The sacrifice achieved what France wanted: the two corps that came too late for Tannenberg were absent from the Marne (p. 260).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 247) the German plan allowed Russia six weeks + (p. 247) Russia attacked in two + (pp. 247-248) OHL believed the West was won.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 248) Moltke weakened the right wing at the height of the offensive. This is the link between the Russian promise of ch05 and the Afterword's first if after Belgium (af01, p. 366).
- **Premises**: (p. 245) orders broadcast in clear + (pp. 249, 251) a front commander acting on reports from an army out of contact + (pp. 248-249, 252) troops without rations.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 259-260) the defeat came from organisation and command, as ch05 had prepared the reader to expect.
- **Premises**: (pp. 245, 254) François disobeyed twice + (p. 253) his artillery won Usdau.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 258) the victory credited to Ludendorff was made by subordinates defying him.

## Evidence offered

- Hoffmann's memoirs and diary, used throughout and repeatedly marked as self-serving or malicious (pp. 245-247, 258-259).
- Ludendorff's and Hindenburg's own accounts of the crisis of nerves (pp. 246-247).
- Tappen's report of OHL's reasoning (pp. 247-248).
- Major Knox, the British attaché with Samsonov, for the Russian headquarters and Samsonov's farewell (pp. 252, 255).
- General Martos's account of his capture (p. 256); Potovsky for Samsonov's death (pp. 256-257).
- Prisoner, gun and casualty figures, with her note that no agreed figure for the dead exists (p. 257).
- Sourcing: I did not read this chapter's endnotes. From the text, the German side comes from the three rival memoirists (Hoffmann, Ludendorff, Hindenburg), each claiming credit, which she plays against one another, for example over who named the battle (p. 258). The Russian side comes through a British observer and survivors. The claim that the two corps would have mattered at the Marne is hers and the Afterword's.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Pays off ch05: the M-15 promise (p. 58), the neglect of munitions (pp. 63-64) and Hoffmann's reading of the Russian plan (p. 67).
- Supplies the evidence for one of the Afterword's ifs (af01, p. 366). Note that Ludendorff said the corps were not needed (p. 247), so the transfer was OHL's error and East Prussia did not require it.
- Lemberg (p. 259) is the Austrian front she chose to omit (fm02, p. 12); it appears here in one paragraph as a victory larger in numbers than Tannenberg.
- The Liège hero losing his nerve (p. 246) and the disobedient subordinate being right (p. 254) repeat the book's interest in temperament (ch10, pp. 124-125).
- Tension: she treats the Russian offensive as both a predictable failure and a successful sacrifice (p. 260).

## Open questions

- Would two corps and a cavalry division have changed the Marne? The Afterword says they might have (af01, p. 366). Ch19 (p. 307) and ch22 (p. 354) say where they were missed.
- How much of Hoffmann can be trusted, given how often she warns against him while relying on him?
