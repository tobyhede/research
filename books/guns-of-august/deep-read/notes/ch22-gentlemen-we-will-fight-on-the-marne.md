# ch22: "Gentlemen, We Will Fight on the Marne" (pp. 347–365)

This note was written before ch19 and ch20 were read (the retreat, Guise, Gallieni's appointment, Kitchener's visit to Sir John French); see their notes. The chapter ends on the eve of battle, 5 September; the battle itself is told only in the Afterword (af01, p. 365).

## Question this chapter answers

Between 3 and 5 September, who decided that the retreat would end and the armies would turn, and how did the Germans come to be caught off balance?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Army of Paris | p. 347 | Gallieni's force, with Maunoury's Sixth Army as its core; since 2 September formally under Joffre. |
| Flank attack | pp. 347-348 | Gallieni's plan to strike the rear of Kluck's southeastward march from Paris, and by doing so force a general offensive. |
| Planned retreat to the Seine | p. 356 | GQG's intention, defended by Berthelot: fall back to a strong line, let the Germans come deeper, wait for reinforcements. |
| Order of 4 September (OHL) | p. 354 | Moltke's order halting the right wing and turning the First and Second Armies to face Paris; an admission that the plan had failed. |
| General Order No. 6 | p. 361 | Joffre's order of 4 September, 10 p.m., for a general offensive on 6 September against the German First Army. |
| Battle fought on the telephone | p. 355 | Gallieni's phrase for his pressure on GQG on 4 September. |
| Limogés | p. 351 | Joffre's dismissals: two army commanders, ten corps commanders and thirty-eight divisional generals in five weeks. |
| Hentsch | p. 362 | OHL's Chief of Intelligence, sent with full powers to make Kluck comply. |
| Prematurely | p. 363 | The word in Joffre's telegram blaming Gallieni in advance; later Gallieni's proof of his own role. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] On the evening of 3 September Gallieni decided to attack Kluck's flank at once and to press Joffre to end the retreat (p. 347).
- [narrative] The Sixth Army was exhausted and mostly reservists; one division had lost two-thirds of its officers (pp. 347-348).
- [narrative] Kluck crossed the Marne on 4 September against orders, ahead of Bülow, having outrun his supply and his heavy artillery; OHL was out of contact with him for two days (p. 348).
- [interpretation] Kluck took no precautions because he thought he faced pursuit and believed the French incapable of turning (p. 348).
- [narrative] Joffre dismissed Lanrezac on 3 September and gave the Fifth Army to Franchet d'Esperey (pp. 350-351).
- [interpretation] Lanrezac's offence was having been right aloud; his retreat at Charleroi saved the French left and, as Hausen later admitted, upset the German plan (pp. 350-351).
- [interpretation] The dismissals did some injustice and improved the army (p. 351).
- [narrative] On 4 September Moltke said the Germans had success without victory, pointing to the lack of prisoners and guns (p. 353).
- [narrative] Moltke wanted reinforcements from the left wing; the Kaiser backed Rupprecht against it and Moltke did not insist. He halted the right wing instead (pp. 353-354).
- [interpretation] The order was correct and late; it might still have been in time but for Gallieni's haste (p. 354).
- [narrative] Gallieni ordered Maunoury to prepare before he had Joffre's consent, and telephoned Poincaré to force Joffre's hand (pp. 354-355).
- [interpretation] Gallieni judged from Paris without knowing the state of the field armies; the plan was bold to the point of rashness (p. 355).
- [narrative] GQG's staff was divided; Joffre thought alone through the afternoon, queried Franchet d'Esperey and Foch, and decided before their answers came (pp. 356-359).
- [narrative] Franchet d'Esperey and Henry Wilson agreed a joint plan at Bray; d'Esperey's reply set the times and places Joffre adopted (pp. 358-359).
- [interpretation] D'Esperey's immediate boldness was a deciding factor and would probably not have come from Lanrezac (p. 359).
- [narrative] Gallieni insisted by telephone on attack north of the Marne on 6 September, and Joffre gave way; Order No. 6 was signed two hours after Moltke's halt order (pp. 360-361).
- [narrative] Sir John French declined to commit himself and the BEF marched a further night in retreat; Joffre drove to Melun on 5 September and won his word (pp. 360-361, 364).
- [narrative] Kluck ignored Moltke's order on the morning of 5 September, learned from Hentsch that evening that the left and centre were deadlocked, and began to pull back (pp. 361-362).
- [interpretation] Had Kluck stayed in echelon, or halted that morning, he could have met the flank attack with his whole army (p. 362).
- [narrative] Joffre drew three corps from his right to gain the numerical superiority he had lacked on the frontiers (p. 363).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 348, 352-353) German commanders believed their own reports of a beaten enemy + (p. 348) OHL could not reach Kluck + (pp. 353-354) the left wing kept its troops.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 354, 362) the right wing was halted too late and with no reserves. This is the evidence for the Afterword's ifs about Kluck's position and the overcommitted left (af01, p. 366).
- **Premises**: (p. 356) Joffre's own plan already included a flank attack by the Sixth Army + (pp. 354-355, 360) Gallieni forced the date and place + (p. 359) d'Esperey supplied the workable plan.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 360-361) the counter-offensive was Joffre's decision, made earlier than he wished because of Gallieni and feasible because of d'Esperey. She divides the credit and does not award it.
- **Premises**: (pp. 350-351) Lanrezac was right and intolerable + (p. 359) his successor answered at once.
  **Conclusion**: both Lanrezac's earlier judgment and his removal served the outcome, a point the Afterword states (af01, p. 367).

## Evidence offered

- Joffre's aide Muller's record of the staff debate and of the telephone call with Gallieni (pp. 355-356, 360).
- Gallieni's and Clergerie's accounts of the Paris side (pp. 347, 354-355).
- Rival versions of Lanrezac's dismissal, Joffre's and Lanrezac's, set side by side (p. 351).
- Moltke's words to Helfferich on prisoners (p. 353); the text of his 4 September order (p. 354); Falkenhayn's diary (p. 354).
- German officers' diaries on exhaustion (pp. 348-349).
- Kuhl on the complete surprise (p. 362).
- Joffre's telegram with the word prematurely, later recovered by Briand (p. 363).
- Spears-style eyewitness detail of the Melun meeting, with Wilson translating (p. 364).
- Sourcing: I did not read this chapter's endnotes. She states that Joffre later tried to minimise Gallieni's part and to expunge records (p. 363) and that Joffre avoided the telephone because he had an eye on history (p. 355). Where the two camps conflict she gives both versions. The German side rests on Kluck, Kuhl, Bülow and Tappen writing after defeat.

## Quotes

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The two corps sent east are said here to be missed (p. 354), which is ch16's closing claim (p. 260) and the Afterword's (af01, p. 366).
- Moltke, broken on 1 August (ch06, pp. 75-76), again fails to insist (p. 354); she ties the two moments together.
- Foch's single British soldier (ch04, p. 51) is recalled when the BEF nearly withdraws (p. 361); Cambon's appeal to English honour (ch07, p. 87) returns as Joffre's (p. 364).
- Joffre's numerical superiority (p. 363) reverses the frontiers (ch14, p. 205).
- The book's narrative stops before the battle. What the Afterword says of the fighting, the gap and Hentsch's second mission (af01, p. 365) has no chapter behind it.
- Tension: Moltke sees clearly and cannot act; Joffre and Kluck act confidently, one rightly and one wrongly. She notes that confidence can veil the eyes (p. 353) while crediting Joffre's with saving the army (af01, p. 367).

## Open questions

- Would Joffre's later battle behind the Seine have succeeded? She thinks it might have come too late (af01, p. 367) and shows Berthelot's case for it only briefly (p. 356).
- How much did Hentsch's visit of 5 September, as opposed to Maunoury's attack, turn Kluck around (p. 362)?
