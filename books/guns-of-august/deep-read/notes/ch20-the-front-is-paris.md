# ch20: The Front Is Paris (pp. 313–333)

## Question this chapter answers

Between 28 August and 1 September, how was Paris prepared, what did the Battle of Guise achieve, and how close did the British commander come to leaving the Allied line?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entrenched camp | p. 314 | Gallieni's idea of Paris as a base of operations for an army fighting beyond the forts, as opposed to a city holding out under siege. |
| Open city | pp. 314, 324 | The alternative pressed by those who wanted Paris spared; encouraged, she says, by GQG. |
| Battle of Guise (St. Quentin) | pp. 316-320 | Lanrezac's ordered counterattack of 29 August: a failure toward St. Quentin and a tactical success at Guise that checked Bülow. |
| Wonderful calm | p. 321 | Foch's phrase for Joffre's composure, which she credits with holding the army together. |
| Amiens dispatch | pp. 326-327 | The Times report of 30 August on a retreating and broken army, passed by the censor as a recruiting aid; origin of the belief that the BEF bore the weight of the blow. |
| Phantom Russians | pp. 327-329 | The rumour of Russian troops crossing Britain to the Western Front, which reached the Germans and worried them. |
| Retirement behind the Seine | p. 329 | Sir John French's stated intention on 30 August to pull the BEF out of the line and west of Paris. |
| Kitchener's visit | pp. 331-332 | His night journey to Paris on 1 September to order French to stay in the line. |

## Leading propositions

- [narrative] Gallieni drove the defence works through by decree, impressed labour and requisitioned transport, including taxis (pp. 314-315).
- [narrative] Haig offered to attack with Lanrezac on 29 August; Sir John French forbade it (p. 316).
- [narrative] Joffre sat silently beside Lanrezac for three hours to make sure he fought (pp. 316-317).
- [narrative] The attack toward St. Quentin failed; Lanrezac shifted his weight to Guise, where Franchet d'Esperey's corps drove back the German Guard (pp. 319-320).
- [interpretation] Guise was a tactical victory that left Lanrezac alone and exposed; its effect on Bülow was not visible to the French at the time (pp. 320-321).
- [narrative] Bülow reported a success to OHL and told Kluck his army was too exhausted to pursue (pp. 321-322).
- [interpretation] Joffre did not panic, waver or despair as others did; if his calm came from want of imagination, that was fortunate for France (p. 321).
- [narrative] On 29 August Joffre abandoned the Somme line and ordered a further retreat and the removal of GQG (pp. 321-322).
- [interpretation] To Joffre Paris would stand or fall with the field army's battle; he also wanted the government gone because its departure enlarged his authority (pp. 322-323).
- [narrative] Gallieni told the Cabinet on 30 August that Paris could not hold without an army of three or four corps (pp. 323-324).
- [narrative] News of Tannenberg reached Joffre together with evidence that at least two German corps had gone east (p. 325).
- [interpretation] Joffre removed Ruffey as a scapegoat for the Ardennes (p. 326).
- [interpretation] The Amiens dispatch founded a British myth; the BEF never faced more than three German corps out of over thirty in the first month (p. 327).
- [interpretation] German worry about some 70,000 imaginary Russians counted at the Marne as much as the 70,000 real men sent east (pp. 328-329).
- [narrative] French wrote to Kitchener that his confidence in the French was waning and that he would retire behind the Seine; his own operation order the same day recorded the French success at Guise (p. 329).
- [interpretation] He was not dismissed, perhaps for want of an agreed successor; everyone handled him with tact and no one trusted him (p. 330).
- [narrative] Kitchener ordered him to remain in the fighting line and then left the manner to his judgment (p. 332).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 320) the Guard Corps fell back at Guise + (pp. 321-322) Bülow could not pursue + (ch21, p. 336) Bülow then asked Kluck to wheel inward.
  **Conclusion**: the forced counterattack that Lanrezac thought mad helped produce Kluck's turn. She states the first two links here and the third in ch21.
- **Premises**: (p. 318) officers of the BEF said the troops needed only food and rest + (p. 329) French's own order acknowledged the French success.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 318-319, 329) his claim of a shattered army was false, and his motive was fear for his army and his reputation.
- **Premises**: (p. 327) the dispatch said the BEF bore the weight of the blow + (p. 327) three corps out of thirty.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 327) the British account of the first month is a myth. This is her own judgment, stated bluntly.

## Evidence offered

- Gallieni's memoirs and Hirschauer for the Paris measures (pp. 314-315).
- A French sergeant's account of the attack at St. Quentin (pp. 319-320).
- Lanrezac's telephone exchange with Belin (p. 320).
- Poincaré's diary for the Cabinet and the senator on the roof (pp. 322-324).
- The text of the Amiens dispatch (pp. 326-327) and American travellers' reports of the Russian rumour (p. 328).
- Sir John French's letters and telegrams to Kitchener, quoted against his own operation order and his later book (pp. 329, 331).
- Kitchener's telegrams (pp. 330, 332); she notes French's version of the private meeting is unreliable and gives only its result (p. 332).
- Sourcing: I did not read this chapter's endnotes. The case against Sir John French is made from his own documents, which is strong; the motives she assigns (fear, reputation) are inference. Joffre's reasons for wanting the government out of Paris are her reading. The figure of three corps out of thirty is stated without a source in the text.

## Open questions

- Was Guise decisive or incidental? She calls it a tactical victory (p. 320) and lets the consequence emerge later.
- Did the phantom Russians really weigh on German decisions at the Marne (pp. 328-329)? The claim is made in one sentence.
