# ch05: Exercising Power (pp. 94–117)

## Question this chapter answers

How did fascist regimes actually govern once in office (Paxton's Stage Four), and how far did they change the societies they ruled? Paxton's answer: not as a monolith obeying one will, but as an unstable coalition of four elements (leader, party, state apparatus, civil society) locked in permanent rivalry, held together by shared enmity to liberalism and the Left. The German and Italian regimes contained the same elements and differed in the relative weight each achieved. Rule rested on selective terror plus wide accommodation, and the fascist "revolution" transformed citizenship and the public/private boundary while leaving property and social hierarchy largely intact.

## Key terms

| Dual state | p. 96 | Ernst Fraenkel's model of Nazi Germany: a *normative state* (legally constituted authorities, traditional civil service, due process, bureaucratic recruitment) coexisting with a *prerogative state* (the party's parallel organizations, bound by no rule but the leader's whim, militants' gratification and the people's supposed destiny) (p. 96). | Normative state | p. 96 | The sector that continues to apply law by due process and to staff itself by competence and seniority (p. 96). Never formally abolished in Germany; encroached on and contaminated over time, near-eclipsed after war began (p. 96). Stronger in Italy (p. 97). |
| Prerogative state | p. 96 | The sector of arbitrary, rule-free power exercised by party agencies (p. 96). In Italy: the OVRA, controlled press, economic baronies such as the IRI, African fiefdoms (p. 97). |
| Islands of separateness | p. 97 | Friedrich and Brzezinski's term for parts of civil society that survive inside a totalitarian dictatorship, for example Catholic parishes; Paxton borrows the metaphor without accepting the totalitarian model whole (p. 97). |
| Gleichschaltung | p. 97 | Nazi euphemism for coordination or leveling: the absorption of independent organizations. Paxton insists it was neither inevitable nor unilinear and could involve two-way negotiation and appropriation from within (p. 97); also applied to Hitler's bringing his conservative colleagues into line in 1933 (p. 102). |

| Parallel organizations / parallel structures | p. 96, p. 98 | Party agencies duplicating state and social institutions (militia beside army, party secretary beside prefect, party foreign-policy office, youth and leisure bodies). An asset to the leader for outflanking conservatives and a power base for radicals against him (p. 98); the main cause of shapelessness (p. 99). |
| Shapelessness | p. 98 | Chaotic, overlapping lines of authority characteristic of fascist rule, distinguishing it from military dictatorship or authoritarian rule (p. 99). Endnote 31 traces the word to Neumann via Arendt and Broszat (p. 233). |
| Charisma | p. 100 | The basis of the fascist leader's legitimacy, claimed neither by election nor conquest: a direct, unmediated bond with the people as incarnation of its will and destiny. Explains radicalization under an indolent Hitler, the leader's immunity from grumbling, the brittleness of the regime, and the absence of any fascist succession (p. 100). |
| Polyocracy | p. 100 | Interpretation of fascist governance as rule by multiple relatively autonomous power centers in unending rivalry, with the leadership principle cascading down into many petty leaders (pp. 100-101). Paxton accepts it with two warnings (p. 101). |
| Intentionalists / structuralists (functionalists) | p. 100, p. 101 | The 1980s debate: the dictator's will as the centre of explanation versus the claim that his will could act only through many links with state and society (p. 101). Paxton rejects the extreme of each (p. 101). |
| Weak dictator | p. 101 | Hans Mommsen's label for Hitler: not a denial of unlimited power, but an observation that the regime was not rationally organised and its murderous energy did not come from Hitler's diligence (p. 101). |

## Leading propositions

- The image of the leader ruling alone is a product of fascist propaganda, Allied wartime awe, and postwar conservative self-exculpation; no dictator rules without the cooperation or acquiescence of the military, police, judiciary, senior civil servants and social and economic powers (p. 94).
- Because fascists reached office through conservative elites, they were obliged to share power with them; this makes fascist dictatorships different in origin, development and practice from Stalin's (p. 95).
- There has never been an ideologically pure fascist regime, and each generation of scholars (Neumann, Bracher, Broszat, Mommsen; Salvemini, Aquarone, Schieder, Petersen, Legnani, even Gentile) has found some pact or alliance at the base of the regimes (p. 95).
- Composite regimes are not static: conservatives pull toward cautious traditional authoritarianism, fascists toward leveling populist dictatorship, and the leader resists both (pp. 95-96).
- The two regimes moved in opposite directions: Italy decayed toward conservative authoritarian rule, Germany radicalized toward unbridled party license (p. 96).
- Fraenkel's dual state is a fruitful description of both regimes, with the prerogative state steadily encroaching in Germany and far more weight left to the normative state in Italy (pp. 96-97).
- The dual state is incomplete: the contest also involves civil society, which the regimes could not always swallow, and Gleichschaltung was a negotiated, two-way and sometimes subverted process (pp. 97-98).
- Four elements (leader, party, state apparatus, civil society) are in permanent tension because none can dispense with the others (p. 98).
- Parallel organizations cut both ways and are a principal source of the shapelessness that sets fascist rule apart from military dictatorship or authoritarianism (pp. 98-99).
- Opening the parties to opportunists diluted them (p. 99).
- Fascist leadership rests on charisma more nakedly than any other kind of rule, which is why it is brittle and why no fascist regime has passed power to a successor (p. 100).
- Neither extreme intentionalism nor extreme structuralism is tenable; the convincing account is two-way, with officials working toward the leader and the leader setting goals and removing limits (p. 101).
- Hitler mastered his conservative allies by summer 1933, and they thereafter fought only rearguard actions for institutional autonomy, losing the army and Foreign Office in 1938 (pp. 102-103).
- At every crucial decision most German conservatives swallowed their doubts in favour of overriding common interests; their postwar emphasis on opposition overstates it (p. 103).
- Italy had the same contending elements but a different apportionment; calling the result merely a tougher Liberal Italy underestimates the party's innovations and the latent tension (p. 104).
- Leader and party are in permanent tension because the leader must neglect early promises to win allies; fascist regimes face a problem traditional dictators do not, of keeping party energy boiling without upsetting order and allies (pp. 104-105).
- Party-state conflict was the most easily and definitively settled; civil servants, police, judges and doctors mostly cooperated, and leaders almost never let militants displace career officials (pp. 105-107).
- The Italian police stayed under a career civil servant while the German police passed to the party via the SS: one of the most profound differences between the regimes (p. 106).
- Terror was indispensable but selective; because it fell on groups many disliked, it gratified more than it frightened, and consensus was high in both countries (pp. 107-108).
- The popularity/terror dichotomy is too rigid; the interesting ground lies between, in accommodation (p. 108, p. 112).
- Workers were contained by terror, division, concessions and integration; youth were especially won; women and intellectuals mostly accommodated or quietly escaped the roles assigned (pp. 108-111).
- Popular approval tracked success: Mussolini's from the Lateran accords of 1929 to Ethiopia in 1936, Hitler's on full employment and bloodless foreign victories (pp. 109-110).
- Fascist regimes left the distribution of property and the social hierarchy largely intact (p. 112).
- They did transform citizenship: individual rights lost autonomous existence, the state of law vanished, and the regime could imprison, despoil and kill at will (p. 113).
- The effort to have the public sphere absorb the private is a fundamental point of difference from authoritarian conservatism and from liberalism (pp. 114-115).
- Economic policy is where the leaders conceded most to conservatives and where practice departed furthest from early programs; it was subordinate to political ends and driven by war preparation (p. 115).
- Business did not choose fascism first, mostly preferred it to the alternatives, objected to parts of it, adapted well, and got far more than the party radicals did (p. 116).
- Fascism was not a developmental dictatorship (p. 117).
- The German and Italian cases are distinguished by the relative weight of leader, party and traditional institutions, not by different ingredients (p. 117).

## Arguments

**A. Against the monolith: obligatory power sharing (pp. 94-96)**

- **Premises**: (p. 94) Any dictator needs the agencies of rule and the powerful social forces. (p. 95) Fascists in particular entered office by the gift of conservative elites and so could not casually discard them. (p. 95) Successive scholars of both regimes, including one most committed to the totalitarian reading of Italy, describe a cartel, alliance, coalition or composite. (pp. 95-96) The partners want different things. (p. 96) Constitutional restraints and the rule of law have collapsed and a social Darwinist climate prevails.
- **Unstated premises**: that agreement among historians of different politics is itself evidence about the regimes and not only about a historiographical fashion; that the manner of arrival in power fixes the structure of rule for the regime's life.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 96) Fascist rule is a never-ending struggle for preeminence within a coalition, and never static.

**B. The dual state, and why it is not enough (pp. 96-98)**

- **Premises**: (p. 96) Fraenkel's two sectors coexisted in conflict-ridden but workable cooperation, giving the mix of legalism and arbitrary violence. (p. 96) In Germany the prerogative sector encroached on and contaminated the normative one and after the war began came close to total dominance. (p. 97) In Italy the state, not the party, was placed at the centre, though prerogative elements existed. (p. 97) But organisations outside the state (unions, youth clubs, professional bodies, churches) were also targets and sometimes survived, bargained, or turned regime institutions to their own use. (pp. 97-98) Examples: Gestapo denunciations for private ends, German fraternities, the Catholic Church in Italy, the GUF and the Dopolavoro.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 98) The contest has four parties, not two; civil society must be added to leader, party and state.

**C. Why the tension is permanent and why it produces shapelessness (pp. 98-99)**

- **Premises**: (p. 98) Conservatives fear that removing the leader readmits the Left or the liberals. (p. 98) The leaders need the conservatives' economic and military resources. (p. 98) The leaders cannot weaken the party too far without losing their own independent base. (p. 98) Parallel organizations let the leader outflank conservative bastions but also give radicals a base against him. (p. 99) Duplicated agencies mean competing lines of authority.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 98-99) No contender can eliminate the others, so the struggle continues; the characteristic result is febrile activism combined with shapelessness, which marks fascist rule off from military dictatorship and authoritarianism.

**D. Charisma (p. 100)**

- **Premises**: (p. 100) The leader's legitimacy comes from neither election nor conquest but from a claimed direct bond with the people. (p. 100) Stalin shared the role of bearer of destiny with the Communist Party, so succession was possible; fascist leaders shared it with no one. (p. 100) An inactive leader frees subordinates to compete in radicalism. (p. 100) The leader is exempt from grumbling aimed at the administration. (p. 100) High promises of a special relation to history raise the cost of failure.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 100) Fascist rule is more dependent on charisma than any other kind, which helps explain both the absence of any fascist succession and the speed with which a failing leader loses his hold, as Mussolini did in July 1943. Paxton hedges the succession point with "may help explain" (p. 100).

**E. Between intentionalism and structuralism (pp. 100-101)**

- **Premises**: (p. 101) Polyocracy alone cannot explain why the regimes were dynamic and not stalemated, and in extreme form loses sight of the leader's supremacy. (p. 101) Intentionalism works best in foreign and military policy, where both leaders were hands-on. (p. 101) Hitler's working habits (inaccessibility, late rising, no cabinet meetings after February 1938) do not fit a picture of a will diligently applied. (p. 101) Mommsen's weak-dictator thesis concerns the mode of exercising power, not its extent.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 101) Neither extreme is tenable; the best 1990s work shows competition from below to anticipate the leader's wishes together with the leader's indispensable role in setting goals, removing limits and rewarding zeal. The full account of radicalization is deferred to ch06 (p. 101).

**F. Fascists versus conservatives: the German and Italian outcomes (pp. 101-104)**

- **Premises**: (p. 102) Von Papen's circle expected to manage Hitler; the Comintern expected a swing back to the Left. Both were wrong. (p. 102) Hitler's advantages were audacity, drive, tactical agility, manipulation of the communist terror scare to suspend the rule of law, and willingness to murder. (p. 102) After the one-party law of July 14, 1933 open legal opposition was impossible. (pp. 102-103) Von Papen's Marburg speech was answered by the arrest and then murder of his associates; Hindenburg died on August 2, 1934. (p. 103) In February 1938 the army command and the Foreign Office were taken over. (p. 103) Conservatives hampered only one policy, euthanasia; individual and late resistance (Kreisau, July 20, 1944) came from their ranks. (p. 104) In Italy Mussolini distrusted his party, subordinated it to the state, and had to share the summit with king and Church.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 103) German conservatives had real differences with the Nazis and suffered real defeats, yet at each ratcheting of anti-Jewish repression, loss of liberties, foreign aggression and economic subordination most of them went along. (p. 104) In Italy the same elements produced a different apportionment of power, though not simply a harsher Liberal Italy.

**G. Leader versus party (pp. 104-105)**

- **Premises**: (p. 104) The leader breaks early promises in order to make the alliances that bring power, disappointing radicals. (p. 104) In Germany the second-revolution agitation of 1933 threatened the state's monopoly of violence and alarmed business and the army. (p. 105) Hitler settled it by murder in June 1934; later outlets were domestic and foreign success, war, spoliation of occupied Europe and the murder of the Jews. (p. 105) Mussolini faced more open and durable challenges from the ras, was pushed by them in 1921 and in December 1924, appointed Farinacci in February 1925 and dismissed him a year later, then used pliable secretaries.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 105) Fascist regimes face a problem unknown to traditional dictators: how to keep the party's energy alive without disturbing order and conservative allies. Hitler solved it by control and outlets; Mussolini by subordinating the party to himself and the state.

**H. Party versus state (pp. 105-107)**

- **Premises**: (p. 106) Leaders almost never granted militants' demands to replace career officials (SA sacrificed to the army; Milizia kept out of the army's sphere). (p. 106) Civil servants shared the regimes' preference for authority and order and liked freedom from legal restraint. (p. 106) German police resented Weimar and were flattered by the regime; Himmler rose from Bavarian political police chief in April 1933 to chief of all German police in June 1936. (p. 106) German judges were already conservative and traded a limited invasion of their sphere for an enhanced role; Italian judges were used to political interference. (p. 107) Doctors and child welfare professionals found in Nazism things they already wanted.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 107) This was the most easily and definitively settled of the tensions; the Nazi state in particular functioned vigorously to the end.

**I. Selective terror and broad accommodation (pp. 107-112)**

- **Premises**: (p. 107) The terror model has prevailed partly because it is an alibi for the peoples concerned. (pp. 107-108) Nazi violence was public and aimed at Jews, Marxists and asocial outsiders whom many Germans were glad to see removed; only at the end did it turn on ordinary Germans. (p. 108) Italian violence was heaviest before power and comparatively mild afterwards, and likewise aimed at designated national enemies. (p. 108) The Gestapo managed with roughly one officer per ten to fifteen thousand citizens because citizens supplied denunciations. (pp. 108-109) Workers were contained by Mason's four means. (p. 109) Small-group peer pressure did much of the work (Haffner). (pp. 109-110) Church accommodation and foreign success produced real approval. (pp. 110-112) Youth, women, intellectuals and figures such as Furtwängler accommodated where regime aims overlapped with theirs and alternatives looked worse.
- **Unstated premise**: that plebiscite results, police ratios and the later public-opinion literature measure consent and not merely the absence of visible dissent under a dictatorship.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 107, p. 112) Terror was selective and consensus high; most citizens accepted things as they were, and by accepting partial accommodation the regimes kept the loyalty of nationalists and conservatives who did not agree with everything.

**J. What kind of revolution (pp. 112-117)**

- **Premises**: (p. 112) Early fascist programs never attacked wealth and capitalism as directly as a hasty reading suggests, and the leadership principle reinforced hierarchy. (pp. 112-113) Many radicals had already been shed on the way to power, and priorities shifted from assembling the discontented to mobilising national energies. (p. 113) Fascists needed armies, productive capacity, order and property for national aggrandizement. (p. 113) But the mission required subordinating the individual to the community: no autonomous rights, no state of law, no representation. (p. 114) Unity had to be manufactured by state action, hence youth organisations, leisure organisations and the invasion of the private sphere. (p. 115) Economic policy was a means; it followed political priorities and war preparation. (p. 116) Business and regime had overlapping but distinct interests. (p. 117) Fascist economies never matched postwar or even pre-1914 European growth, nor the total war mobilisation of some democracies.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 117) Fascism was revolutionary in its conception of citizenship and counterrevolutionary toward individual liberties, human rights, due process and international peace; it was neither an agent of capitalists nor a developmental dictatorship.

**Position versus argument.** What Paxton holds: the two regimes are one kind of thing, a fascist-conservative amalgam (p. 117). Why he says he holds it: the same four elements contended in both, and only the outcome differed (p. 104, p. 117). The claim that the Italian outcome followed largely from Mussolini's fear of his own militants (p. 117) sits beside an earlier admission that the reason is uncertain (p. 97).

**Concessions and qualifications Paxton makes**

- He is not sure why Mussolini subordinated party to state and offers several possible explanations: less leeway, less drive, less luck, the surviving king, fear of party chieftains (p. 97).
- Even Hitler was not free until 1942 of the need to placate munitions owners, officers, experts, religious leaders and public opinion (p. 100).
- The polyocracy approach is worthy but risks making fascist energy inexplicable and obscuring the leader's supremacy (p. 101).
- German conservatives and Nazis had authentic differences and conservatives suffered real defeats; there were honourable exceptions (p. 103).
- Mussolini's dictatorship must not be treated as comic: the Rosselli and Matteotti murders and colonial ruthlessness count against that view (p. 108).
- Public enthusiasm was never as total as fascists promised their allies (p. 112).
- Fascist regimes did make some breathtaking changes, and the fascist new men posed some threat to inherited position (p. 112).
- Cultural homogeneity was not achieved; cultural life stayed a patchwork of official, tolerated and illicit activity (p. 115).
- The female vote for Hitler is impossible to measure precisely (p. 110), and Heisenberg's role remains obscure (pp. 111-112).
- Nazi medicine was not mere sadism and did real public health research (p. 107).

## Evidence offered

- **Historiographical consensus** (the main support for the coalition thesis): Neumann's cartel of party, industry, army and bureaucracy; Bracher's alliance; Broszat's coalition partners; Mommsen's alliance of ascending fascist elites and traditional leadership groups; for Italy Salvemini, Aquarone, Schieder and Petersen, Legnani, and Gentile's concession that the regime was composite (p. 95). This is testimony of interpreters, not primary evidence; its force lies in its range across generations and politics.
- **Borrowed models**: Fraenkel's dual state (p. 96); Friedrich and Brzezinski's islands of separateness (p. 97); Broszat and Hüttenberger's polyocracy, with the term's history in the endnote (p. 234); Mommsen's weak dictator (p. 101), which the endnote glosses as unlimited power chaotically exercised (p. 235); Kershaw's working toward the leader (p. 101, p. 235); Mason's four means of containment (p. 108); Hayes on business (p. 116); Durkheim's two solidarities (p. 114).
- **Narrative fact, Germany**: Hitler never formally abolished the Weimar constitution and refused a euthanasia law so as not to be bound (p. 96); one-party law of July 14, 1933 (p. 102); Goering replaces von Papen in Prussia on April 7, 1933; Marburg speech June 17, 1934; Night of the Long Knives June 30, 1934 (pp. 102-103); Hindenburg dies August 2, 1934 (p. 103); Blomberg and Fritsch removed and von Neurath replaced by Ribbentrop in February 1938 (p. 103); cabinet ceases to meet after February 1938 (p. 101); Himmler's ascent April 1933 to June 1936 (p. 106); SA auxiliary police ended August 2, 1933 (p. 106); Wagener displaced by Kurt Schmitt of Allianz in June 1933 (p. 116).
- **Narrative fact, Italy**: the prefect declared the highest authority of the state over party leaders (pp. 98-99); PNF rolls opened in 1933 and membership required for civil-service jobs (p. 99); Farinacci named secretary February 1925, dismissed after a year following eight killings in Florence in October 1925 and a plagiarism revelation (p. 105); Lateran Treaties February 1929 (p. 109); Labor Charter 1927 and Chamber of Corporations 1939 (p. 109); lira revalued at 90 to the pound in December 1927 over the finance minister's objection (pp. 115-116); the king deposes Mussolini in July 1943 (p. 97, p. 100).
- **Statistics**: Nazi Party membership up by 1.6 million between January and May 1933 (p. 99); Police Day extended from one day to seven by 1937 (p. 106); about ten thousand serious opponents imprisoned in Italy and nine death sentences between 1926 and 1940 (p. 108), with the endnote giving the Special Tribunal's caseload and noting that most death sentences involved Croat and Slovene separatists and that Italy ran more than fifty camps in 1940-43 (p. 237); Gestapo ratio of one officer per ten to fifteen thousand citizens (p. 108), the endnote citing sixty-nine officers for Cologne in 1942 (p. 237); 98 percent for the single Fascist list on March 24, 1929 (p. 110); Nazis' 44 percent in March 1933 as baseline (p. 110); 90 of the signers of Croce's manifesto writing for the Enciclopedia italiana in 1931 and 11 of 1,200 professors refusing the oath (p. 111); close to 70 percent of six-to-twenty-one-year-olds in Turin, Genoa and Milan in Fascist youth bodies, far fewer in the south (p. 114); Hitler Youth share of ten-to-eighteen-year-olds from 1 percent to 87 percent between end 1932 and early 1939 (p. 114); 90 percent of Nazi-era films light entertainment (p. 115); private insurers keeping 85 percent of the business (p. 117).
- **Cases and anecdotes**: German fraternities surviving through old-boy protection and student apathy (pp. 97-98); the Dopolavoro appropriated by ordinary Italians (p. 99); Haffner's retreat for apprentice magistrates ending with his own salute (p. 109); Furtwängler as the accommodating non-member (p. 112); I. G. Farben adapting from export to rearmament (p. 116); the Hermann Goering Werke forced on steelmakers (p. 116); the insurance industry's defeat of the party's nonprofit schemes (pp. 116-117); a suspect acquitted in court rearrested at the courthouse door (p. 113); Ley's remark that only a sleeper was a private individual (p. 114); Hitler's December 4, 1938 Reichstag speech on never giving the young back (p. 114).
- **Contemporary misjudgments used as evidence of contingency**: von Papen's expectation of managing Hitler and the Comintern's forecast of a revolutionary tide (p. 102).
- **Comparison as method**: Germany against Italy on nearly every point (police, judiciary, party-state balance, pattern of violence, treatment of intellectuals); fascism against Stalinism on succession and power sharing (p. 95, p. 100); fascism against traditional dictatorship on workers and party energy (p. 105, p. 109, p. 115); the Gestapo against the Stasi (p. 108).
- **Weaker points in the evidence (my assessment)**: consent is inferred from sources that a dictatorship shapes (single-list elections, oath-taking, membership figures); the chapter itself reports that Italian party membership became a career necessity (p. 99), which cuts against reading enrolment as enthusiasm. The claim that Germans often felt more gratified than threatened by violence (p. 108) rests on recent scholarship cited in notes, not shown in the text. The growth comparison (p. 117) is asserted without figures in the text; the endnote points to one study of Italian growth rates (p. 240).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch04**: the whole chapter follows from the claim that fascists were let into office by conservative elites (p. 94, restated p. 95); the Reichstag fire powers and the Matteotti crisis are recalled, not re-argued (p. 96, p. 105).
- **Depends on ch03**: the parallel organizations were built during the stage of taking root (p. 98), and the conflicts seen then sharpen in power (p. 95).
- **Feeds ch06**: the chapter repeatedly postpones the question it raises. Why polyocracy produced energy and not stalemate (p. 101), the Holocaust and the intentionalist debate (p. 101), euthanasia (p. 103), the eastern occupation (p. 105), Mussolini's alternation of normalization and radicalization (p. 105), and the Italian racial laws (p. 111) are all deferred to the next chapter. ch05 describes the structure; ch06 has to supply the motor.
- **Feeds ch07**: the test used there for postwar and non-European cases (a mass party sharing power with conservatives, charisma, giving up free institutions) is the regime anatomy set out here; mechanical versus organic solidarity (p. 114) returns at p. 163, and developmental dictatorship (p. 117) returns in the verdict on Latin America (p. 157).
- **Feeds ch08**: the rejection of the emergency-capitalism and developmental-dictatorship readings (pp. 115-117) and the qualified use of the totalitarian model (p. 97, p. 104) anticipate the survey of interpretations; an endnote here points forward to ch08 on developmental dictatorship (p. 240).
- **Tension (my observation)**: the four-way model treats the two regimes as the same amalgam with different weights (p. 117), but the differences Paxton records are large: party-run versus civil-service police (p. 106), nine death sentences against omnipresent violence (pp. 107-108), a deposable leader against one who never was (p. 100). Whether a difference of weight this large is still a difference of degree is not argued; it is settled by the choice of model.
- **Tension (my observation)**: Paxton says the cause of Mussolini's subordination of the party is uncertain (p. 97) and later states it as largely fear of his own militants (p. 117).
- **Tension (my observation)**: the leader is constrained enough that pure fascism is hardly possible (p. 95) yet supreme enough that structuralism must not lose sight of him (p. 101). The working-toward formula reconciles these, but it is taken over from the German literature and illustrated only for Hitler; no Italian equivalent is shown, and Mussolini is described as the desk-bound opposite of Hitler (p. 101).
- **Tension (my observation)**: fascism is said to differ from Stalinism by obligatory power sharing (p. 95) while the Nazi prerogative state reaches something approaching total dominance in wartime (p. 96). The contrast therefore holds for origins and peacetime more firmly than for the end state.
- **Tension (my observation)**: the consensus thesis and the atomisation of workers sit uneasily together. Workers are described as demoralised and afraid to confide in anyone (p. 109) and also as part of a population whose consent was high (p. 107). Paxton's phrase for the middle ground is accommodation (p. 112), which is weaker than consent; the section heading's three words (accommodation, enthusiasm, terror) are not given relative weights.
- **Comparison point for other books**: the denial that fascist regimes attacked property (pp. 112-113) and the primacy of politics over economics (p. 115) are the places to test Paxton against accounts that treat Nazism as a form of socialism or of planning (Hayek in this repo) and against economic histories of the Nazi war economy (Tooze in this repo).

## Open questions

- If conservatives could not dispense with the leader for fear of the Left (p. 98), what changed in Italy in July 1943 beyond military defeat, and why did no equivalent coalition form in Germany before July 1944? The chapter gives the king as the institutional difference (p. 97) but not a general condition.
- How is high consensus (p. 107) to be measured under a regime that punishes dissent, and how much weight can the plebiscite of 1929 or the Gestapo staffing ratio bear? Paxton refers the reader to the bibliographical essay for the opinion studies (p. 234).
- Does the four-element model apply to any regime other than these two? ch07 will need it for cases that never reached Stage Four.
- Is the absence of a fascist succession (p. 100) explained by charisma, or simply by the fact that both regimes ended in military defeat within a generation? Paxton's wording is cautious.
- The claim that early fascist programs never attacked capitalism as directly as they seem to (p. 112) points back to ch01; check how that squares with the anti-capitalist items listed in ch02-ch03 notes.
- Where does the SS economic empire, mentioned only as a rival to business (p. 102) and as an insurer in conquered territory (p. 117), fit the verdict that property was left intact?
- Paxton says that for some observers the swallowing of the private by the public is the very essence of fascism (p. 114). Does ch08's own definition adopt or reject that?
