# ch07: Other Times, Other Places (pp. 137–164)

## Question this chapter answers

Is fascism confined to Europe between 1919 and 1945, or can it exist after 1945 and outside Europe? Paxton's answer: classical fascism in its original dress is finished, but a functional equivalent remains possible. Stage One movements exist in every democracy; since 1945 Stage Two has been reached in western Europe only by parties that normalised themselves, and nowhere outside Europe did a fully authentic fascist regime appear. Recurrence is to be detected by how a movement works (rooting, alliance with frightened conservatives, giving up free institutions), not by shirts, salutes or rhetoric.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classical fascism | p. 137 | The historic interwar fascisms of 1922-45, with their specific symbols, programs and European setting; distinguished from possible later equivalents (pp. 137-138). |
| Functional equivalent | p. 139 | A movement or regime that performs the same functions as fascism (mass mobilization for the reunification, purification and regeneration of a troubled group, at the cost of free institutions) under different names and symbols (p. 138, p. 139). The chapter's test. Applied to skinheads if elite-backed (p. 139), Milosevic's Serbia (p. 152), American movements (p. 161), religion (p. 162), and the Israeli far right (p. 163). |
| Giving up free institutions | p. 138 | Salvemini's phrase, used as an essential criterion: fascism is a malady of sick democracies, so it can occur only where democracy or representative government has been tried (p. 152). Excludes Third World tyrannies (pp. 152-153) and Islamic fundamentalist movements (p. 163). |
| Stage One / Stage Two / Stage Three | p. 139 | The book's stages applied as a diagnostic. Stage One: founding of radical Right movements with some link to fascism, found in every industrial mass-politics society (p. 139). Stage Two: becoming rooted as a significant player and bearer of important interests, with elites cultivating or tolerating the movement (p. 139, p. 146). Stage Three: approach to power through alliances and complicities among frightened elites (pp. 146-147). |
| Legacy fascism / legacy neofascism | p. 139, p. 141 | Movements created after 1945 by unreconstructed former Nazis and fascists and their heirs: SRP, DRP, NPD, MSI, Jeune Nation, British groups (pp. 139-142). Expected to die with the generation (p. 142). |
| Second generation (of the far Right) | p. 144, p. 146 | Radical Right parties of the 1980s and 1990s feeding on new grievances (structural unemployment, immigration, identity, antipolitics), more successful than legacy movements (pp. 143-146). |
| Normalization | p. 139 | A far Right party's effort to present itself as an outwardly moderate party distinguishable from the centre Right only by awkward friends and verbal excesses; the precondition of Stage Two since 1945 (p. 139, p. 147). |
| Antipolitics | p. 144 | Public disaffection from the whole political Establishment, which the extreme Right could exploit better than the far Left after 1989 (p. 144). |
| Postfascist | p. 146 | Self-description of the Alleanza Nazionale after 1994-95: Europe has entered an era in which Mussolinian nostalgia is irrelevant (p. 147). |
| Cryptofascism | p. 147 | The press's charge against Le Pen, Haider and Fini, based on slips, coded phrases and the lineage of supporters (pp. 147-148). |
| Fascism from above / from below | p. 154, p. 158 | From above: fascist-style measures imposed by existing rulers without a mass movement (Uriburu, p. 154; imperial Japan, p. 158). | Emperor-system fascism | p. 158 | The Japanese scholarly reading of the 1930s militarised dictatorship as a distinctive fascism; Paxton reports it and does not adopt it (p. 158, p. 160). |

## Leading propositions

- The early boundary of fascism is easy to fix (mass democracy plus World War I and the Bolshevik Revolution); the outer time limit is harder (p. 137).
- Classical fascism's revival after 1945 was blocked chiefly by the repugnance it inspired, and also by prosperity and globalization, individualistic consumerism, the reduced availability of war to large nations, and the fading of a revolutionary threat (p. 137).
- Developments of the 1990s reopened the question: Balkan ethnic cleansing, exclusionary nationalism in the east, skinhead violence, the entry of Alleanza Nazionale and the Freedom Party into governments, Le Pen in 2002, Fortuyn (p. 138).
- Whether fascism can recur depends on one's understanding of it; Paxton reports three positions: loose alarm, Nolte's categorical denial, and the common view that fascists remain but interwar conditions are gone (p. 138).
- The inoculation of 1945 is temporary; a future fascism would call itself something else, use fresh symbols and need not target Jews (pp. 138-139).
- A new fascism would dress in the mainstream patriotic clothing of its own place and time; there is no test by costume (p. 139).
- The stages give a better test: Stage One is everywhere; Stage Two is the stringent historical test, and it requires elite cultivation or toleration, not replicas of 1920s rhetoric (p. 139).
- Since 1945, outside the former Soviet sphere, Stage Two has been reached, if at all, only by parties that normalised themselves (p. 139).
- Legacy neofascism in Germany stayed weak and divided; in Italy the MSI was more substantial but quarantined; in Britain and France the radical Right had little electoral success for thirty years yet influenced immigration policy (pp. 140-142).
- The radical Right grew again in the 1980s and 1990s on new grievances produced by deindustrialisation, structural unemployment, postcolonial immigration, identity anxieties and the failure of mainstream parties, and after 1989 had no serious rival as the voice of the losers (pp. 142-144).
- The Front National found the formula first and entered Stage Two; the MSI/AN and the Freedom Party entered national governments where a discredited governing bloc faced no credible mainstream opposition (pp. 145-146).
- In western Europe a fascist look and electoral success are inversely related (p. 147).
- The successful parties echo some classical themes (decadence, identity, unassimilable foreigners, authority) but lack others: the attack on market liberty, the attack on democratic constitutions, and expansion by war (pp. 148-149).
- Circumstances differ even more than programs; western Europe offers no significant opening for parties overtly affiliated with classical fascism (pp. 149-150).
- Post-Soviet eastern Europe and the Balkans hold the most virulent radical Right; most such movements are weak, kept so for the moment by the pull of the European Union (pp. 150-151).
- Milosevic's Serbia is a functional equivalent though it arrived by a different route and showed no fascist trappings but brutality (p. 152).
- Outside Europe, the criterion of a failed or sick democracy excludes mere tyrannies (pp. 152-153).
- Latin America came closest to genuine fascist regimes, but Vargas and Perón are better classed as national-populist developmental dictatorships with fascist trappings (p. 153, p. 157).
- Imperial Japan is better understood as an expansionist military dictatorship with state-sponsored mobilization than as fascism (p. 160).
- Western client dictatorships are not fascist (p. 160).
- The United States has never been exempt; the danger lies in movements using authentically American themes, not in imported imitations (pp. 160-161).
- Rhetoric resembling early fascism within a subjugated minority cannot become fascism because it cannot wield power (p. 162).
- A religion-based fascism is conceivable where religious identity outweighs national identity, but al-Qaeda and the Taliban do not qualify because they are not reactions to a malfunctioning democracy (pp. 162-163).
- The possibilities for non-European fascism are probably greater than in the 1930s because failed democratic experiments have multiplied (p. 163).
- The real warning signals are political deadlock in crisis, threatened conservatives seeking tougher allies and ready to abandon due process, and conservatives borrowing fascist techniques and co-opting the fascist following (p. 164).

## Arguments

**A. Function, not form (pp. 137-139)**

- **Premises**: (p. 137) The specific conjuncture of 1890s pessimism, mass nationalization, World War I and Bolshevism is gone, and classical fascism is discredited. (p. 138) The discredit fades as eyewitnesses die. (p. 138) Postwar far Right parties are as broad as interwar ones and adept at a moderate public face with coded private signals. (p. 139) Historic fascisms were shaped by the political space and the alliances available to them; new ones would be too. (p. 139) Carbon copies have been too exotic or shocking to win allies since 1945.
- **Unstated premise**: that fascism is defined by a function (mobilising a troubled group for unity, purity and regeneration at the expense of free institutions, in alliance with elites) which can be separated from its historical content. This is the assumption the whole chapter runs on; the definition itself is postponed to ch08.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 139) Recurrence is possible as a functional equivalent, and must be judged by comparing how movements work, with the stage model supplying the questions.

**B. Why legacy neofascism stayed marginal (pp. 139-142)**

- **Premises**: (p. 140) Committed Nazi opinion in the American zone fell from 15-18 percent to about 3 percent by the early 1950s despite ten million expellees. (p. 140) German parties were banned when overt (SRP 1952), stayed near 1 percent in prosperity (DRP), and never passed the federal 5 percent threshold (NPD 4.3 percent in 1969). (pp. 140-141) The MSI averaged 4-5 percent, peaked after red scares, and was quarantined after the Tambroni affair of 1960. (pp. 141-142) In France and Britain decolonisation and immigration fed movements that won little at the polls.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 142) Legacy movements could be expected to die with the fascist generations; their electoral weakness did not stop them shaping national policy on race and immigration (p. 141, p. 142).

**C. The new space after 1973 (pp. 142-144)**

- **Premises**: (p. 143) The oil shocks and Asian competition turned industrial decline into long-term structural unemployment and a two-tier society. (p. 143) Unions, Marxist parties and proletarian neighbourhoods lost their power to defend and console. (pp. 143-144) Immigrants from former colonies, welcome in boom years, became unwelcome and were seen as a threat to identity. (p. 144) Governments could neither apply Keynesian remedies nor leave the European and global markets, and the state was losing authority upward. (p. 144) After the Soviet collapse the Marxist Left lost credibility as a protest vehicle.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 144) A set of linked enemies (globalization, foreigners, multiculturalism, taxes, incompetent politicians) and a widening antipolitics gave the radical Right an open field among the losers of postindustrial Europe.

**D. Stage Two reached only by normalised parties (pp. 144-148)**

- **Premises**: (p. 145) The FN bundled constituencies around immigration, refrained from threatening democracy directly, won cities and regional seats, rewarded militants with office, and forced conservatives to adopt its positions and sometimes ally with it locally. (pp. 145-146) In Italy and Austria a long-ruling bloc was discredited and no credible mainstream opposition existed, so outsiders filled the niche. (p. 147) The MSI renamed itself, declared a postfascist era and shed its die-hards; the overt British groups got nowhere. (p. 148) Old fascist clienteles had nowhere else to go and could be kept with hints and disavowals.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 145, p. 148) Movement into Stage Two in 1990s France, Italy or Austria required being firmly recentred on the moderate Right; Paxton adds that this was already true of La Rocque in 1930s France (p. 148).

**E. Are the second-generation parties fascist? (pp. 147-150)**

- **Premises**: (p. 148) They echo fears of decline, identity, the alien threat and the call for authority. (p. 148) Most voters chose them as single-issue anti-immigrant parties. (p. 148) They do not attack the market; they mostly favour less state intervention. (p. 148) None proposes single-party dictatorship; at most a stronger executive and less inhibited police. (pp. 148-149) Open celebration of violence is left to skinheads, with some quiet overlap of membership. (p. 149) None proposes expansion by war; border changes sought are secessionist. (pp. 149-150) Peace, prosperity, functioning democracy and the absence of a Bolshevik threat make the setting unlike interwar Europe.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 150) Western Europe has had legacy fascisms and, since 1980, normalised but racist extreme Right parties as minority partners in government, but no significant opening exists for parties overtly affiliated with classical fascism. Paxton does not give a flat yes or no on whether the normalised parties are fascist; the question he posed at p. 147 is answered by listing likenesses and differences.

**F. The east: virulence, weakness, and one equivalent (pp. 150-152)**

- **Premises**: (p. 150) Russia has a deep Slavophile antiliberal tradition (the Union of Russian People and its Black Hundreds), revived after 1991 by Pamyat and Zhirinovsky, whose party took nearly 23 percent in December 1993. (p. 150) Zhirinovsky faded mainly because Yeltsin held power and ignored parliament. (p. 151) In the successor states the appeal of European Union membership keeps integral nationalism marginal. (pp. 151-152) Milosevic, a sitting communist bureaucrat, adopted expansionist nationalism to consolidate personal rule, with enthusiastic public support, mass killing and expulsion. (p. 152) Serbia kept multiparty elections and had no militant party that rooted and then allied with the establishment.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 152) The label fascist explains nothing about how Milosevic's rule was built, but his Serbia should be recognised as a functional equivalent; Tudjman's Croatia, which revived Ustaša emblems, is its less-noticed counterpart.

**G. Latin America: similar tools, different bodies (pp. 153-157)**

- **Premises**: (p. 153) Much 1930s fascist decor in Latin America was fashion, and Depression remedies came as much from the New Deal as from Mussolini. (pp. 153-154) Brazil's Integralists were a genuine homegrown fascist movement, but Vargas ruled without them and closed them down. (p. 155) Perón came to power through a mass worker demonstration against a military junta, not at the head of a party discrediting democracy. (pp. 155-156) His base was more proletarian, he raised labour's share of income, broadened the franchise, won clean elections, had no diabolized enemy and no interest in expansion by war; Eva Perón's role was foreign to fascist machismo. (p. 157) Vargas and Perón took power from oligarchies, widened participation, ruled partly formed nations, marginalised the local fascists, and punished individuals and not categories.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 157) Similarities are of instruments borrowed during fascism's apogee; differences are of structure, function and relation to society. These were national-populist developmental dictatorships with fascist trappings, distantly comparable to Mussolini and hardly at all to Hitler.

**H. Japan: fascism without fascists (pp. 158-160)**

- **Premises**: (p. 158) Japan had moved toward democracy in the 1920s, which makes it eligible under the Salvemini criterion. (p. 158) Fascist-like movements from below (Kita Ikki, the young officers) were crushed, Kita executed after February 1936. (pp. 158-159) Military-bureaucratic cabinets, Konoe's mobilisation and the New Order of 1940 adopted selected corporatist and control measures and dropped the solidarist and anticapitalist ones. (p. 159) No single mass party or popular movement existed. (pp. 159-160) Barrington Moore's parallel between German and Japanese late industrialisation under landlord-peasant agriculture has not convinced Japan specialists. (p. 160) Japan faced no imminent revolutionary threat, no external defeat and no internal disintegration.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 160) Imperial Japan of 1932-45 is an expansionist military dictatorship with a high degree of state-sponsored mobilization, not a fascist regime.

**I. The United States (pp. 160-162)**

- **Premises**: (p. 160) Antidemocratic and xenophobic movements have existed in America since the 1840s. (p. 160) Movements with a foreign look won few followers. (p. 161) Native-themed movements (the 1920s Klan, Coughlin, Gerald L. K. Smith, the present politics of resentment) had real reach. (p. 161) These fringe groups would need catastrophic setbacks and polarisation to find powerful allies. (p. 161) An American fascism would use the flag, the cross and the pledge as litmus tests for internal enemies.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 161-162) Around reassuring national symbols and after a severe blow to national prestige, Americans might support forcible national regeneration, unification and purification, aimed at the First Amendment, church-state separation, gun control efforts, minorities and dissident behaviour. This is a conditional, not a forecast.

**J. Religion and the sick-democracy criterion (pp. 162-163)**

- **Premises**: (p. 162) Payne holds that fascism needs the space opened by secularization, since religion would limit the leader. (p. 162) Paxton replies that the anticlericalism of Italian and German fascism was a historical accident of those nationalisms, and that religion-based fascisms existed even in Europe (Falange, Rexism, Lapua, the Legion of the Archangel Michael). (pp. 162-163) Religious identity can be as strong an engine as national identity, or stronger. (p. 163) But al-Qaeda and the Taliban arise in traditional hierarchical societies, their unity is organic in Durkheim's sense, and they never had free institutions to give up. (p. 163) In Israel a democratic tradition exists and has been weakened; by 2002 language close to a functional equivalent could be heard on the right of Likud and in small religious parties.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 163) Religious fascism is conceivable, but only where a democracy has been tried and is failing; on that test Islamic fundamentalist movements are excluded and the Israeli far right is a possible case.

**K. Closing diagnosis (pp. 163-164)**

- **Premises**: (p. 163) Stage One movements are found in all major democracies. (pp. 163-164) Sects with Nazi paraphernalia, while violent, stay a law and order problem so long as they are shut out of alliances with the establishment. (p. 164) Movements that moderate their language and drop the symbols are likelier to gain influence.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 164) The warning signals to watch are deadlock in crisis, conservatives looking for tougher allies and ready to give up due process and the rule of law, and conservatives borrowing fascist techniques and appealing to the mobilizing passions. Historical knowledge lets one tell isolated imitations from a mature fascist-conservative alliance.

**Position versus argument.** What Paxton holds: fascism is not over as a possibility, though it is over in its classical form (p. 139, p. 163). Why he says he holds it: his account in earlier chapters locates fascism's success in available political space and elite alliances, which are replicable conditions, and not in a unique European culture (p. 139, p. 152). He marks the alternative openly: those who tie fascism to the European fin de siècle will deny it can exist elsewhere (p. 152), and his conclusion is conditional on rejecting that view (p. 163).

**Concessions and qualifications Paxton makes**

- Stage Two has been reached since 1945 only "if at all" (p. 139).
- Raw electoral statistics tell little by themselves (p. 146).
- Fortuyn's views were really libertarian (p. 146).
- Serbian nationalism showed none of the outward trappings of fascism except brutality, permitted fairly free multiparty elections, and did not arise by the rooting of a militant party (p. 152).
- Assessing Latin American dictatorships through fascism is perilous and can become an empty labeling exercise (p. 157).
- Mussolini is a closer parallel to Vargas and Perón than Hitler is (p. 157).
- Moore's thesis is reported and then reported as unconvincing to specialists (pp. 159-160).
- Japanese scholars, and not only Marxists, commonly do call imperial Japan fascist (p. 158).
- Paxton's own expectation of an American fascist-type movement after 1968 was wrong (p. 161).
- American patriotic symbols contain no fascism in themselves (p. 161).
- Payne's secularization argument applies best to Europe (p. 162).
- The South African happy ending holds for the moment and could still turn sour (p. 153).

## Evidence offered

- **Electoral statistics, western Europe**: SRP 11 percent in Lower Saxony 1951; DRP about 1 percent in the 1950s; NPD in seven of ten state parliaments 1966-68 and 4.3 percent federally in 1969; Republicans 7.5 percent in Berlin 1989 (p. 140). MSI 1.9 percent in 1948, 4-5 percent average, 8.7 percent in 1972 (p. 140); 2.8 million votes in 1972; Alessandra Mussolini 43 percent in Naples and Fini 47 percent in Rome in 1993 (p. 141). Poujadists about 2.5 million votes, 12 percent, in January 1956 (p. 142). FN 11 percent in 1983-84, 14.4 percent in 1988, 273 regional seats in 1998, 17 percent and then 19 percent in 2002 (p. 145). Ex-MSI 13 percent and five portfolios in 1994 (p. 146). Freedom Party 27 percent on October 3, 1999 and six of twelve portfolios in February 2000 (p. 146). Fortuyn List 17 percent in May 2002 (p. 146). Vlaams Blok over 10 percent nationally and 25.5 percent in Antwerp in 1991, 28 percent in Antwerp in 1994 (p. 149). BNP up to 20 percent in Oldham and three council seats in Burnley, May 2002 (p. 147). LDP nearly 23 percent in December 1993 (p. 150).
- **Opinion and incident figures**: 15-18 percent committed to Nazism in the American zone, falling to about 3 percent (p. 140); 2,639 incidents of far Right violence in Germany in 1992 (p. 144).
- **Narrative fact**: SRP banned 1952 (p. 140); Tambroni's resignation in 1960 and the thirty-year quarantine of the MSI (p. 141); MSI renamed Alleanza Nazionale January 1994, founding congress 1995 (p. 147); Le Pen fined for the "detail of history" remark of September 1987 (p. 148); Schönhuber a former SS officer (p. 148); Milosevic's Kosovo speech of April 24, 1987, the wars of 1991-95, Dayton in November 1995, NATO air strikes in 1999, his fall in September 2000 (pp. 151-152); Vargas's coup of 1930 and Estado Novo of 1937-45 (p. 154); the Argentine coups of 1930 and 1943 and October 17, 1945 (pp. 154-155); labour's share of Argentine national income from 40 percent in 1946 to 49 percent by 1949 (pp. 155-156); Integralist membership peaking at 180,000 in 1934 (p. 154); universal male suffrage in Japan 1926, the Manchurian invasion of September 1931, the Tokyo rising of February 26, 1936, Konoe's cabinets and the New Order of 1940, Nakano's 3 percent in 1942 (pp. 158-159); the Black Hundreds killing three hundred Jews in Odessa in October 1905 (p. 150); Coughlin's radio audience estimated at forty million (p. 161).
- **Scholars relied on**: Nolte for the thesis of fascism confined to its era (p. 137, p. 138); Salvemini for the criterion of giving up free institutions (p. 138, p. 152); Orwell on native dress (p. 139); Prowe and Ignazi for the comparison of programs and circumstances, per the endnotes (pp. 245-247); Payne on secularization (p. 162) and, in an endnote, on the impossibility of re-creating specific historic fascism (p. 245); Kasza and Maruyama on Japan, per the endnotes (p. 249); Barrington Moore (p. 159) and Dore and Ouchi's test of him (p. 250); Gates on Afrocentric rhetoric (p. 162); Durkheim's distinction (p. 163).
- **Quoted verdict by another historian**: the line that Japan's outcome was as if fascism had come to Europe by crushing Mussolini and Hitler (p. 159).
- **Counterfactual and personal testimony**: Paxton's own unfulfilled expectation after 1968 of an American movement with Vietnam veterans as its Freikorps (p. 161).
- **Analogy**: surgeons' scalpels on different bodies (p. 157); 1945 as inoculation (p. 138).
- **Thin spots (my assessment)**: the treatment of Chile, the Congo, Bolivia, South Africa after 1948, Iran and Hindu nationalism is a sentence or two each; Khomeini's Iran and Hindu fundamentalism are raised as questions (p. 162) and never answered case by case. The Israeli passage rests on one newspaper interview cited in the endnote (p. 250). The remark that Jews from North Africa and the Near East are indifferent to the democratic tradition (p. 163) is asserted without evidence. The profile of what an American fascism would target (pp. 161-162) is conjecture, flagged as conditional. The endnote on anti-Semitism in Peronist Argentina (p. 249) qualifies the text's statement that Perón lacked a diabolized enemy.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch02-ch04**: the stage model is used here as a diagnostic instrument and not only as a narrative frame (p. 139, pp. 146-147). The Stage Two test (bearer of interests, available space, elite complicity) is the ch03 analysis; the warning signs at p. 164 restate the ch04 thesis that conservatives' choices bring fascists to power. The Union of Russian People is added retrospectively to the ch02 precursors (p. 150), and La Rocque's 1930s centrism (p. 148) refers back to ch03.
- **Depends on ch05-ch06**: the criteria used to deny the label to Perón, Vargas, Japan and client states (a mass party, a diabolized enemy, expansion by war, popular acclaim, elimination of categories and not individuals) are the regime features set out in ch05 and the radicalization of ch06.
- **Feeds ch08**: the chapter applies a definition it has not yet stated. The functional formula at p. 138 and the list of features used to exclude cases amount to a working definition; ch08 should be checked against them. The endnotes for the Salvemini phrase point forward to ch08 (p. 245, p. 248).
- **Tension (my observation)**: the criteria shift from case to case. Milosevic's Serbia is admitted as a functional equivalent although it lacked a rooting party, an alliance with the establishment as the route to power, and the abandonment of elections (p. 152). Perón is excluded partly for lacking a diabolized enemy and war aims (p. 156), Japan for lacking a mass party (p. 159), client states for lacking popular acclaim and freedom to expand (p. 160). Serbia passes on outcome (mass-backed killing for an ethnically pure, enlarged state) where others fail on process. Which features are necessary is left for ch08.
- **Tension (my observation)**: the Salvemini criterion does a lot of work (pp. 152-153, p. 163) but its application is loose. Perón took power from an oligarchy, not a democracy, and that counts against the label (p. 156, p. 157); Milosevic took over a communist federation, not a democracy, and that is not held against it (p. 152).
- **Tension (my observation)**: normalised parties are said to lack the attack on democracy, the market and peace (pp. 148-149), yet the chapter's closing advice is that the normalised, moderate-sounding movements are the ones to watch (p. 164). If normalization is real the parties are not fascist; if it is tactical the programmatic comparison at pp. 148-149 proves little. Paxton leans on circumstances, not programs, to resolve this (pp. 149-150), which means the verdict on western Europe is a verdict about conditions at the time of writing.
- **Tension (my observation)**: function-over-form risks stretching the concept; Paxton himself warns against empty labeling (p. 157) and against loose use (p. 138). The guard against stretching is the stage test plus the sick-democracy criterion, both of which need the ch08 definition to be more than judgment calls.
- **Dated material**: the chapter is written from 2002-03. The Vlaams Blok, Haider, Fini, Fortuyn, Yeltsin and Putin passages (pp. 145-150) and the post-September 11 remarks (p. 139, p. 161) are snapshots. Later sessions should treat the electoral figures as historical evidence for the argument, not as current description.
- **Comparison point for other books**: the Barrington Moore passage (p. 159) is the one place Paxton engages a long-run social-structural explanation; compare with any structural account in this repo. The Latin American verdict (p. 157) reuses developmental dictatorship, a category ch05 refused for Italy and Germany (ch05, p. 117).

## Open questions

- Is the functional definition at p. 138 the same as the one ch08 finally gives, and does ch08 make any of its elements necessary conditions?
- Which criteria did the work in admitting Serbia (p. 152) and excluding Perón (p. 156)? A table of cases against features, built from this chapter, would show whether the classification is consistent.
- Paxton asks whether the second-generation parties are fascist or neofascist (p. 147) and answers by degrees. What would count, on his own account, as one of them crossing into Stage Three?
- Does the sick-democracy criterion (p. 152) mean a failed democracy must precede fascism, or only that representative government has been attempted? The wording allows both, and the Japanese case (p. 158) depends on the looser reading.
- Khomeini's Iran and Hindu fundamentalism are named (p. 162) and left unclassified. India is a functioning democracy with a religious-nationalist movement, which seems to be exactly the case the argument at pp. 162-163 makes conceivable; Paxton does not pursue it.
- What evidence supports the claim that the appeal of European Union membership is what keeps eastern European radical Right movements marginal (p. 151)? It is stated, with a general reference in the endnote (p. 248).
- How much of the argument that prosperity, peace and working democracy close the space in western Europe (p. 150) survives if those conditions weaken? Paxton's own framework implies the answer, and he says the inoculation is temporary (p. 138), but he does not spell out thresholds.
