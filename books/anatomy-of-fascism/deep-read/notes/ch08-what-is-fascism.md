# ch08: What Is Fascism? (pp. 164–175)

## Question this chapter answers

Having followed fascism through five stages without defining it, what definition can now be given that covers the later stages as well as the early ones, and that does not cut fascism off from its setting and its allies? On the way Paxton asks which of the existing interpretations survive the evidence of the cycle, and where the boundary lies between fascism and tyranny, military dictatorship and authoritarianism. His answer is a one-sentence definition of fascism as a form of political behaviour (p. 174), supported by nine mobilizing passions (pp. 174-175), and a closing claim that fascism in its early stages is still present in democracies (p. 175).

## Leading propositions

**Why a definition, and what it must do**

- Paxton avoided a definition at the start in order to observe fascism in action through a whole cycle (p. 164).
- A definition can no longer be evaded, or generic fascism would disappear into a list of stages and processes (p. 164).
- The regimes had more impact than the movements because they held the power of war and death, so a definition must fit the later stages as well as the early ones (p. 164).
- A definition must not treat fascism in isolation from its environment and accomplices (p. 165).
- Fascism in power is a compound of conservative, national-socialist and radical Right elements (p. 165).

**The interpretations**

- Thugs in power: explains neither place nor time, and hides the part played by liberalism's own narrowness and by frightened liberals (p. 165).
- Agent of capitalism: denies fascism's autonomous roots and popular appeal and ignores human choice (p. 165). Capitalists mostly preferred authoritarians but accommodated once fascists held power (p. 165).
- Business as victim: takes middle-level frictions and postwar self-exculpation too seriously (p. 165).
- Psychological accounts of leaders or publics fail on comparison and on the question of why publics followed (p. 166).
- Uneven development (Parsons, Bloch) has merit but is weakened by the French case (pp. 166-167).
- Mass society is contradicted by the best empirical work: Weimar society was richly structured and Nazism recruited through organisations (p. 167).
- Developmental dictatorship wrongly supposes fascism had a rational economic goal (p. 167).
- Social composition (Lipset) fails because support cut across classes and membership was unstable (pp. 167-168).
- Totalitarianism captures the aspiration to total control but hides the shared rule with traditional elites, the violence from below, and the disorder of fascist government (pp. 168-170).
- Political religion is useful as analogy and splits into three separate issues (p. 170). The first, borrowing from religious culture, says more about rooting and exercising power than about achieving it; the second, the void left by secularisation, fails on comparison; the third, enmity with established religion, is contradicted by cooperation in Germany and Italy (p. 170).
- Ideology: fascism's relation to ideas differs from that of the other isms, since intellectual positions were dropped or added for tactical need (pp. 170-171).
- Cultural studies show how fascism presented itself but seldom ask how influential the imagery was, and seldom compare (p. 171).
- No single interpretation has carried the day (p. 172).

**Boundaries**

- Fascism differs from classical tyranny in being a phenomenon of failed democracies that channels popular passion, where tyranny only silences (p. 172).
- All fascisms are militaristic but most military dictatorships are not fascist (p. 172).
- Authoritarian regimes accept private space and intermediary bodies and want a passive public; fascists want to reduce the private sphere to nothing and to excite the public (p. 173).
- Franco's Spain, Salazar's Portugal and Vichy France fall on the authoritarian side (pp. 173-174).

**The definition (p. 174), verbatim**

"Fascism may be defined as a form of political behavior marked by obsessive preoccupation with community decline, humiliation, or victimhood and by compensatory cults of unity, energy, and purity, in which a mass-based party of committed nationalist militants, working in uneasy but effective collaboration with traditional elites, abandons democratic liberties and pursues with redemptive violence and without ethical or legal restraints goals of internal cleansing and external expansion." (p. 174)

Its parts, in my breakdown of the sentence on p. 174:

1. Genus: a form of political behaviour, not a doctrine or a regime type.
2. Preoccupation: community decline, humiliation or victimhood.
3. Compensation: cults of unity, energy and purity.
4. Agent: a mass-based party of committed nationalist militants.
5. Relation to the establishment: uneasy but effective collaboration with traditional elites.
6. What it gives up: democratic liberties.
7. Means: redemptive violence, without ethical or legal restraints.
8. Goals: internal cleansing and external expansion.

Paxton introduces it as a short handle that covers its subject no better than a snapshot covers a person (p. 174).

**The nine mobilizing passions (pp. 174-175), verbatim**

Paxton says these are the ideas underlying fascist action, that they are best deduced from the actions, and that he first called them mobilizing passions in chapter 2 (p. 174). The first item is on p. 174; the rest are on p. 175.

1. "a sense of overwhelming crisis beyond the reach of any traditional solutions" (p. 174)
2. "primacy of the group, toward which one has duties superior to every right, whether individual or universal, and the subordination of the individual to it" (p. 175)
3. "the belief that one’s group is a victim, a sentiment that justifies any action, without legal or moral limits, against its enemies, both internal and external" (p. 175)
4. "dread of the group’s decline under the corrosive effects of individualistic liberalism, class conflict, and alien influences" (p. 175)
5. "the need for closer integration of a purer community, by consent if possible, or by exclusionary violence if necessary" (p. 175)
6. "the need for authority by natural chiefs (always male), culminating in a national chieftain who alone is capable of incarnating the group’s historical destiny" (p. 175)
7. "the superiority of the leader’s instincts over abstract and universal reason" (p. 175)
8. "the beauty of violence and the efficacy of will, when they are devoted to the group’s success" (p. 175)
9. "the right of the chosen people to dominate others without restraint from any kind of human or divine law, right being decided by the sole criterion of the group’s prowess within a Darwinian struggle" (p. 175)

The second item begins with the word "the" at the foot of p. 174; the quotation above starts on p. 175.

**Ideas and action**

- Paxton sides with Neumann against taking fascist ideology at face value, but holds that fascists knew what they wanted (p. 174).
- Fascism was neither the plain application of a programme nor freewheeling opportunism (p. 174).

**Today**

- Fascism by this definition is still visible. It exists at Stage One in all democratic countries, the United States included (p. 175).
- It does not need a march on a capital to take root; tolerating lawless treatment of national enemies is enough (p. 175).
- Something very close to classical fascism has reached Stage Two in a few deeply troubled societies (p. 175). He names none here.
- Further progress is not inevitable and depends on the severity of crisis and very largely on the choices of those holding economic, social and political power (p. 175).

## Arguments

**A. Why the definition comes last and must cover the regimes (pp. 164-165)**

- **Premises**: (p. 164) Observing a whole cycle is more promising than searching for a static essence. (p. 164) Without a definition, the stage approach becomes another nominalism. (p. 164) Fascism acts differently at each stage, which raises the question of which is the real one. (p. 164) Some authors take the early movements as pure and the regimes as corruptions. (p. 164) The regimes had more impact. (pp. 164-165) The later stages cannot be described without settings and allies.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 164-165) A usable definition must apply to later stages as well as early ones and must include the environment and accomplices. Hence fascism as a compound and as a network of relationships (p. 165).

**B. The test applied to each interpretation (pp. 165-172)**

Paxton's objections recur in a small number of forms. This grouping is mine.

- **Fails on comparison** (cannot say why here and not there): thugs in power (p. 165); sexual repression, no more severe in Germany and Italy than in Britain (p. 166); uneven development, given France's dual economy (p. 167); secularisation as void, since the crisis was not obviously worse in Germany and Italy than in France and Britain (p. 170); cultural preparation, since France was prepared and became fascist only by conquest, if then (p. 171).
- **Ignores allies and elites**: Lipset, eyes on the early stages (p. 168); totalitarian theory, blind to the party's jostling with bureaucracy, proprietors and churches (p. 169).
- **Removes choice or asserts inevitability**: the Third International formula (p. 165).
- **Contradicted by empirical research**: capitalists' actual preferences (p. 165); Weimar's dense associational life against mass-society atomisation (p. 167); cross-class recruitment against Lipset (p. 168); Italian growth rates against developmental dictatorship (p. 167).
- **Mistakes self-presentation for effect**: ideology read from programmes (pp. 170-171); cultural studies that do not ask about influence (p. 171); the totalitarian image, which evokes dictators' dreams and obstructs study of how regimes embedded themselves (p. 170).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 172) No one interpretation has prevailed. Paxton does not say it in these words, but the criteria he applies are the ones his own definition is built to meet.

**C. Against equating Hitler and Stalin (pp. 168-170)**

- **Premises**: (p. 169) The mechanisms of control and the experience of victims were similar. (p. 169) Stalin ruled a society simplified by revolution, with no autonomous inherited power; Hitler came to power with elite assent and governed in strained association with elites. (p. 169) Declared aims differed: a master race against universal equality. (p. 169) The paradigm looks at central authority and misses violence from below. (pp. 169-170) Stalin killed class enemies, a condition that can change, mostly adult males; Hitler killed race enemies, a condition that cannot change and that condemns newborns. (p. 170) The model's image of efficiency misses the disorder of Hitler's and Mussolini's rule.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 169-170) Totalitarian theory obscures the fundamental character of the Nazi governing system; and the book, while acknowledging the repugnance of both terrors, condemns Nazi racial extermination more strongly (p. 170).
- **Note on register**: the last step is a stated moral judgement by the author, not a historical finding. He marks the debate as politicised on both sides and says it should be judged on its merits (p. 169).

**D. Boundaries (pp. 172-174)**

- **Premises**: (p. 172) Fascism presupposes a failed democracy and manipulated mass enthusiasm; tyranny and most military dictatorships have neither. (p. 173) Authoritarians rely on intermediary bodies, not a single party, for social control; they want a demobilised public and a limited state. (p. 173) Franco borrowed fascist trappings, but after 1945 rested on Church, landowners and army and intervened little. (p. 173) Salazar suppressed the Portuguese fascists in 1934. (p. 174) Vichy had neither a single party nor parallel institutions at the outset; later parallel bodies and the Paris fascists who got posts did not amount to a ruling party.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 172-174) Fascism is distinguished by mass mobilisation, a party with parallel institutions, and the drive to eliminate the private sphere, not by brutality. Authoritarian regimes can be murderous (p. 173): the Franquist repression may have killed as many as two hundred thousand between 1939 and 1945 (p. 173).

**E. From behaviour back to ideas (p. 174)**

- **Premises**: (p. 174) Behaviour requires choices and choices imply ideas, as his critics say. (p. 174) Fascist leaders claimed ideas were central; antifascists such as Neumann denied it. (p. 174) Fascist ideology was proclaimed as central yet amended or violated as expedient. (p. 174) Some underlying ideas were unstated in public language and belong to visceral feeling.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 174) The ideas are best deduced from the actions, and they take the form of the nine mobilizing passions (pp. 174-175).

**F. The present (p. 175)**

- **Premises**: (p. 175) The definition and passions describe behaviour still visible. (p. 175) Giving up the freedoms of unpopular groups is recurrently attractive in Western democracies. (p. 175) Rooting requires only tolerance of lawless treatment of enemies.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 175) Stage One is universal in democracies, Stage Two has been reached in a few societies, and the outcome depends on crisis and on elite choices. Understanding past success improves the chance of a wise response.

## Evidence offered

This is a chapter of historiographical argument. Most evidence is by reference to earlier chapters and to other scholars.

- **Scholars and theorists named in the text**: Croce and Meinecke as classical liberals (p. 165); Stalin's Third International (p. 165); Brecht's Arturo Ui (p. 165); Wilhelm Reich, 1933 (p. 166); Talcott Parsons, 1942 (p. 166); Ernst Bloch (pp. 166-167); Hannah Arendt (p. 167); Seymour Martin Lipset, 1963 (p. 167); Giovanni Amendola, 1923 (p. 168); Friedrich and Brzezinski, 1956 (p. 168); Gregory Bateson (p. 171); Luisa Passerini (p. 171); Gaetano Salvemini (p. 172); Franz Neumann (p. 174). The biographer of Hitler praised on p. 166 is identified only in note 12.
- **Empirical counter-evidence, mostly by citation**: German clubs already split into socialist and nonsocialist networks, which eased Nazi takeover (p. 167; note 23 cites Gies and Koshar); I. G. Farben adapting to autarky and prospering (p. 165); Mussolini overvaluing the lira in 1926 and choosing war after 1935 (p. 167); Italian growth faster before 1914 and after 1945 than under Mussolini (p. 167); statistical work on Nazi voters (note 29, at p. 168).
- **Boundary cases as evidence**: Franco's remark on lowering the Falange's sails in 1945 (p. 173); Salazar's 1934 suppression of National Syndicalism and his list of its faults, which Paxton calls a fair description of fascism (p. 173); Vichy's Milice, special sections and Police for Jewish Affairs (p. 174).
- **Anecdote**: Hitler in civilian dress at Venice on June 14, 1934 (p. 172); Vittorio Mussolini giving his brother a picture of Duke Ellington in 1937 (p. 172), used for the point that commercial culture outcompeted fascist culture (p. 171).
- **Numbers**: the Franquist death toll (p. 173) is the only statistic in the chapter. The comparison of Stalin's and Hitler's killing uses no figures.
- **Endnote that qualifies the text**: note 65, attached to the passive-public contrast on p. 173, concedes that the authoritarian-fascist border is blurred in practice because neither side gets its wish, and that even fascists may win only superficial consent (De Grazia, Kershaw). Note 46, at p. 170, cites Bullock for refusing to equate the two kinds of killing.
- **No evidence given**: the claims about the present (p. 175) are asserted without cases. The definition itself is presented as a summary of the preceding seven chapters, not argued clause by clause.

## Concessions and qualifications

- The definition is a snapshot, a short handle (p. 174).
- Capitalism and fascism did have mutual advantage (p. 165).
- Parsons's approach had the merit of treating fascism as a system and product of a history (p. 166); uneven development still arouses interest (p. 167).
- Mussolini's regime fell far short of total control, yet regimented Italians more than any regime before or since; no regime achieved total control (p. 168).
- Nazi and communist mechanisms of control had many similarities (p. 169).
- Political religion is useful at the level of broad analogy (p. 170).
- Cultural studies have exceptions, such as Passerini (p. 171); the macho restoration of patriarchy comes close to a universal fascist value (p. 171).
- Critics are right that behaviour brings one back to ideas; ideas cannot be banished (p. 174).
- Franco borrowed from Mussolini and the Allies treated him as an Axis partner (p. 173).
- The fascist cycle is not likely to repeat itself blindly, so responses are hard to determine (p. 175).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Closes ch01.** The chapter opens by recalling that ch01 declined to define fascism and set aside the fascist minimum (p. 164), and returns to ch01's first takes (p. 165) and to its account of fascism's odd relation to ideology (pp. 171, 174).
- **Restates ch02's list, with changes.** The nine passions were first listed in ch02 (p. 32). Comparing the two lists, eight items are word for word the same. Item 6 differs: ch02 has natural leaders, a national chief, and the group's destiny, where ch08 has natural chiefs, a national chieftain, and the group's historical destiny (p. 175). The change is stylistic as far as I can tell. Paxton presents the ch08 list as the same list (p. 174).
- **Uses ch03.** The definition's clause on collaboration with traditional elites, and the test that a definition include allies (p. 165), summarise ch03's finding that rooting depended on accomplices (ch03, p. 68). The mass-society refutation (p. 167) relies on ch03's account of targeted Nazi appeals (ch03, p. 51). The capitalist-tool refutation (p. 165) relies on ch03's funding argument (ch03, pp. 51-52). The French objection to uneven development and to cultural preparation (pp. 167, 171) relies on ch03's French case.
- **Uses ch05 and ch06.** The compound of party and conservative elites (p. 165) and the disorder of fascist rule (p. 170) summarise the account of exercising power; the fascist maximum (p. 164) and violence from below (p. 169) point to the radicalisation chapter.
- **Follows from ch07.** The last page of ch07 says conservatives borrowing fascist techniques and passions is the warning sign (p. 164); ch08's final paragraph repeats the stage vocabulary for the present (p. 175).
- **Tension, my observation: the definition describes stages three to five, the closing claim is about stages one and two.** The definition requires a mass-based party working in effective collaboration with traditional elites and abandoning democratic liberties (p. 174). A Stage One movement has neither a mass base nor elite collaboration. Yet Paxton says fascism according to this definition exists at Stage One in all democracies (p. 175). Read strictly, the definition picks out fascism in or near power; what exists at Stage One is behaviour in keeping with the passions, which he adds in the same sentence (p. 175). The passions, not the definition, are doing the work for the early stages.
- **Tension, my observation: behaviour or ideas.** The definition's genus is political behaviour (p. 174), but its first two clauses (preoccupation with decline, cults of unity) are states of mind, and the passions are beliefs and feelings. Paxton concedes the point to his critics (p. 174) and answers that ideas are to be inferred from action. The method guards against taking programmes at face value, but it means the passions cannot be checked independently of the behaviour they explain.
- **Tension, my observation: parallel structures.** Ch03 calls parallel structures one of the defining characteristics of fascism (ch03, p. 68), and ch08 uses their absence to exclude Vichy (p. 174). They do not appear in the definition.
- **Tension, my observation: the authoritarian boundary is the one that matters and is the least firm.** Paxton calls it one of the most essential (pp. 172-173). In the text the contrast is clean (p. 173); in note 65 he grants it is blurred in practice. Franco's regime between 1939 and 1945, with a single party, a Caudillo and mass killing (p. 173), is placed on the authoritarian side mainly by what it became afterwards.
- **Tension, my observation: comparison cuts both ways.** Paxton rejects several theories because they do not explain why Germany and Italy and not France or Britain (pp. 166, 167, 170). His own account in ch03 answers that question with a failing liberal state plus elite choices, which he says is not deterministic. An account that allows choice to override setting is harder to refute by the same comparative test.
- **Link to other books.** The section on totalitarianism (pp. 168-170) is the place to compare with Arendt and with Hayek's use of the term in The Road to Serfdom, where collectivism and totalitarianism are treated as one. Paxton's objection, that the model ignores the shared rule with elites and differences of aim, applies directly to any account that equates Nazism and Soviet communism.

## Open questions

- Does the definition admit any case beyond Italy and Germany? Its clauses on external expansion and on a mass party governing with elites were met in power only by those two in this book. Check ch07's treatment of other candidates against each clause.
- Is external expansion a necessary element? A movement fitting every other clause in a state with no expansionist aim would fall outside the definition as written (p. 174).
- Which societies had reached Stage Two when Paxton wrote (p. 175)? Ch07 should name them.
- Paxton dates the Soviet famine to 1931 and says it affected Russians with equal severity (p. 169), relying on note 45, which I did not open. The claim is contested in other literature and should not be taken from this chapter alone.
- The definition contains no economic element. Is that deliberate, given the rejection of developmental and capitalist-tool readings (pp. 165, 167), and does ch05 support treating economic policy as subordinate?
- How are the passions to be observed in a present-day movement if they are deduced from actions (p. 174)? The closing paragraph implies they can be recognised in advance of the actions (p. 175).
