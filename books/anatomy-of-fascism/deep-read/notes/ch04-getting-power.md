# ch04: Getting Power (pp. 68–94)

## Question this chapter answers

How did fascists actually reach office, in the only two cases where they did so on their own account (Italy 1922, Germany 1933), and why there and not elsewhere? Paxton's answer: not by election and not by coup, but by invitation from conservative elites who, in a deadlocked constitutional system and frightened of the Left, chose to share power with a fascist leader. The "seizure of power" proper came afterwards, from inside office. This is stage three of his five stages (p. 88).

## Key terms

For Paxton a myth and a "gigantic bluff" (p. 71): the marchers were stopped or stranded, and Mussolini got office from the king, arriving by sleeping car (p. 71). The myth later misled Hitler in 1923 (p. 72) and the German Left in 1932-33 (p. 74). |

| Allies and accomplices | p. 78 | The conservative power holders (head of state, army, police, courts, party leaders, press, business) whose help or acquiescence was required. Paxton says they deserve as much study as the fascist leaders (p. 78). |
| Complicity | p. 79 | Forms listed: tolerating fascist violence against the Left; the gift of respectability; (limited) finance (p. 79). |

| Normalization | p. 80 | The fascist party's adaptation to its conservative partners; begun in the rooting stage, intensified as office becomes attainable (p. 80), and necessary for sharing power (p. 81). |
| Ras | p. 80 | Mussolini's regional Fascist chieftains, to whom he was more beholden than Hitler was to the SA (p. 80); they forced his hand in December 1924 (p. 87). |
| Prefascist crisis | p. 83 | The shared features of 1919-22 Italy and 1930-33 Germany: economic dislocation and foreign humiliation, constitutional deadlock, a rapidly growing militant Left, and conservatives who would not work with reformist socialists and could not govern against the Left unaided (p. 83). |
| Deadlock | p. 73, p. 84 | Failure of a parliamentary system to produce a governing majority. The indispensable opening: no fascist movement is likely to reach office without it (p. 84); more crucial than electoral success (p. 93). |
| Revolution after power / coup from within | p. 84, p. 88 | The real seizure of power: massive illegal action from inside office, for which allies needed only to acquiesce (p. 84). Germany 1933-34 (pp. 84-86); Italy 1925-27 (pp. 87-88). |

## Leading propositions

- The March on Rome was a bluff, not a conquest; the belief that the Fascists took power by their own exploits is successful Fascist propaganda (pp. 68-69, p. 71).
- The Italian state had the means to stop the march, and Facta's measures nearly did; the king's refusal to sign the martial law decree, not Fascist force, decided the outcome (pp. 70-71).
- Only in Italy did fascism reach power in the first postwar surge; elsewhere outside Russia, elites restabilised and early fascist movements shrank until the Depression revived them (p. 72).
- The failed 1923 putsch taught Hitler to stay at least outwardly within legality (p. 72).
- The German system was deadlocked from March 27, 1930, well before Hitler was a major force; after September 1930 any majority needed either the socialists or the Nazis, and Hindenburg's circle excluded the Left out of hand (pp. 73-74).
- The myth of the Fascist coup also disarmed the German Left: the SPD waited for a putsch that never came, and the KPD treated the SPD as no less an enemy than the Nazis (pp. 74-75).
- Hitler was close to failure at the end of 1932 and was rescued by von Papen (p. 76).
- Neither leader won a majority in a free election, and neither took office by coup; both were invited by a head of state acting within his formal powers (p. 76).
- No insurrectionary coup against an established state has ever brought fascists to power; authoritarian regimes have crushed such attempts (p. 77).
- The fascist route to power has always run through cooperation with conservative elites, so movement strength is only one variable alongside elite willingness, the leader's flexibility, and the urgency of the crisis (p. 78).
- Conservatives decided the fascists' assets outweighed the costs and applied a double standard to fascist and left-wing violence (p. 78).
- Neither arrival in office was inevitable; conservatives rejected available alternatives and chose the fascist option (p. 81, pp. 93-94).
- Fascists offered a mass following for a majority without the Left, youth, commitment, a way to split workers from Marxism, and a promise of order; conservatives assumed they would stay in control (pp. 81-83).
- The deadlock of the liberal state was not the fascists' work alone and is to some degree a separate issue from the rise of fascism (p. 84, p. 93).
- The real seizure of power happened after appointment and was carried out by illegal action that conservatives accepted (pp. 84-88).
- Entrenched conservative regimes are bad terrain for fascism: they repress it or pre-empt it (p. 88).
- Hitler seldom put foreign fascists in power, preferring traditional elites in occupied and satellite states (pp. 88-90).
- Success in reaching power varies less with fascist ideas or leaders than with the depth of the crisis and the desperation of potential allies (p. 91).
- The model requires only polarization, deadlock, mass mobilization against internal and external enemies, and elite complicity, not a replay of 1922 or 1933 (p. 92).

## Arguments

**A. Against the "seizure" reading of October 1922 (pp. 68-72)**

- **Premises**: (p. 69) Through 1922 the squadristi occupied whole cities without serious hindrance. (p. 70) Facta reinforced the Rome garrison, ordered the trains stopped and prepared martial law. (pp. 70-71) Four hundred police stopped trains carrying twenty thousand Blackshirts; about nine thousand reached the gates of Rome, poorly armed, hungry and wet. (p. 71) The king declined to sign the decree and offered Mussolini the prime ministry. (p. 71) Mussolini arrived on October 30 by sleeping car; the Blackshirts paraded on October 31 after he was in office.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 71) Fascist force did not decide the issue; what decided it was the conservatives' unwillingness to risk their force against his. Mussolini's skill lay in forcing a choice between bloodshed and his appointment and in judging correctly that the king and army would not resist.
- **Limit Paxton states**: the king's motive is unknown. The most likely explanation, a private warning from Marshal Diaz that the troops might fraternise, has left no archival trace; a second theory involves the duke of Aosta (p. 71).

**B. The German path: deadlock first, Nazis second (pp. 72-76)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 72-73) Weimar never had general legitimacy and its centre had to govern through incompatible coalitions. (p. 73) The Young Plan and the Depression forced divisive choices on which no available majority could agree; the Great Coalition fell on unemployment benefits versus budget balance. (p. 73) From March 1930 Brüning governed without a majority under Article 48. (p. 74) The Nazis were then still small (2.8 percent in May 1928); the September 1930 election made them second largest. (p. 74) Hindenburg and his advisers excluded the Left. (p. 75) Responsibility narrowed to a half-dozen men.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 73, p. 76) Hitler was useful to conservatives as the first prospect of a parliamentary majority excluding the Left, and he was put into office by a deal among insiders at a moment when his own momentum was failing.

**C. What did not happen (pp. 76-78)**

- **Premises**: (p. 76) Nazi vote 37.2 percent (July 1932), 33.1 percent (November 1932), 43.9 percent (March 1933, with state resources and intimidation); Italian Fascists 35 of 535 seats in May 1921. (p. 76) Both leaders were appointed by a head of state on the advice of civilian and military counsellors. (p. 77) Coup attempts by fascists failed: the Romanian Legion three times, the Austrian Nazis in July 1934. (p. 77) Conservatives tolerate violence against the Left but not against the state; a fascist insurrection would need the street, would risk benefiting the Left, and would alienate the army and police that fascists need later for expansion.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 78) Since the route to power has always passed through conservative cooperation "at least in the cases so far known", one must study the accomplices and the situations as closely as the movements.

**D. Why conservatives took the fascist option (pp. 78-83)**

- **Premises**: (p. 80) The alternatives as conservatives saw them were the Left in power or a military dictatorship excluding both parliamentary conservatives and fascists. (p. 81) Fascists could supply a majority without the Left; Hitler's contribution here was decisive, Mussolini's thirty-five deputies were not. (pp. 81-82) They supplied youth and a deeper kind of belonging. (p. 82) They had a formula for workers: recruit a few, coerce the rest. (p. 82) They posed as restorers of an order they had themselves disrupted. (p. 82) Their terms were not high: Mussolini had adopted productivism, Hitler reassured industrialists at Düsseldorf in January 1932. (pp. 82-83) Conservatives were sure such social outsiders could not govern without them.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 83) Fascists offered a recipe for governing with popular support, without sharing power with the Left and without threat to conservative privileges; the conservatives held the keys.
- **Unstated premise (my reading)**: that the conservatives' assessment of the Left as unacceptable was a choice and not a constraint. Paxton asserts this (p. 81, p. 94) more than he demonstrates it; see Open questions.

**E. The crisis is partly independent of fascism (pp. 83-84, p. 93)**

- **Premises**: (p. 83) Both crises combined dislocation, humiliation, deadlock, a growing Left, and conservatives unwilling to work with reformists. (pp. 83-84) Fear of communist revolution was real even where the revolution was not; Chabod: middle-class fear peaked after the maximalist wave had subsided. (p. 84) Fascists made democracy work worse but did not alone cause the deadlock; Vivarelli is cited for the independent collapse of the Liberal state.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 84, p. 93) Deadlock or collapse of the liberal state is the more crucial precondition, more than electoral success; fascism exploits an opening it did not solely create.
- **Interpretive choice Paxton flags**: contemporaries read the crisis as systemic, the end of liberalism; since the post-1945 revival of constitutional democracy it looks circumstantial (World War I, sudden enlargement of democracy, the Bolshevik Revolution) (p. 84).

**F. The seizure of power happens from inside (pp. 84-88)**

- **Premises (Germany)**: (p. 84) Hitler began with constitutional powers only and a minority of cabinet posts. (p. 85) The Reichstag fire gave a pretext; Hindenburg signed the decree suspending legal protections. (p. 85) The March election still gave no Nazi majority. (p. 85) The Enabling Act got its two-thirds with communist deputies arrested and with Zentrum and Nationalist votes. (pp. 85-86) Other parties were dissolved; conservatives overlooked the "revolution from below" and Dachau. (p. 86) The June 30, 1934 purge killed SA leaders and also recalcitrant conservatives.
- **Premises (Italy)**: (p. 86) Nearly two years of ordinary coalition government with orthodox finance. (p. 87) The Acerbo law, passed under Blackshirt pressure, then the April 1924 election. (p. 87) The Matteotti murder gave the king and conservatives their best chance to remove Mussolini; they declined for fear of chaos or the Left. (p. 87) The Militia consuls' ultimatum of December 31, 1924 pushed Mussolini to the January 3, 1925 speech. (p. 88) By early 1927 Italy was a one-party dictatorship.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 84, p. 88) The dictatorships were built by illegal action after office was granted, and conservatives accepted the coup from within because the alternatives seemed deadlock or the Left.

**G. Comparison: where fascists did not get power (pp. 88-93)**

- **Premises**: (p. 88) Junior partners were muzzled or destroyed (Romania, Spain, Portugal, Brazil). (pp. 88-90) Conquest rarely brought fascists to power: Norway, Holland, Denmark, France, Romania, Slovakia and Hungary were run through German officials or traditional elites; Croatia was the exception because it had no ruling elite in place. (p. 91) In France conservatives did not feel threatened enough; in Britain the Conservative majority left no space; in Scandinavia social democrats took in farmers and the lower middle class. (pp. 91-92) Where conservatives could crush the Left by force they did (Dollfuss, Franco), at the price of ruling by overt force.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 92) German and Italian conservatives chose fascists because they wanted mass support and believed it was too late to demobilise the public; the alliance with elites was no quirk, and other routes are implausible.

**H. Antideterminism (p. 81, pp. 93-94)**

- **Premises**: (p. 81) Structural factors (shallow liberal traditions, late industrialisation, surviving predemocratic elites, revolutionary surges, national humiliation) enlarged the crisis and narrowed the choices. (pp. 93-94) Other governments were arithmetically possible: Popolari plus reformist socialists in Italy; social democrats plus centre in Germany; a cabinet of technicians; a military government. (p. 94) None was tried or wanted.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 94) The arrival in power is best seen as a process of alliances, choices and closed alternatives, with room for luck; crises opened a space, and choices by a few Establishment leaders put the fascists into it.

## Evidence offered

- **Narrative reconstruction of October 1922** with numbers: about nine thousand Blackshirts at the gates (p. 71); endnote 1 adds that General Pugliese had ninety-five hundred infantry, three hundred cavalry and about eleven thousand police in Rome, citing Répaci (p. 227). The judgement on the march's failure is quoted from Martin Broszat (p. 71, source in endnote 2, p. 227).
- **Election figures**: Nazi 2.8 percent in 1928 and 12 to 107 seats in 1930 (p. 74); 37.2, 33.1 and 43.9 percent (p. 76); Fascists 35 of 535 seats in 1921 (p. 76); the National list 64.9 percent and 374 seats in April 1924 (p. 87). Endnote 35 gives the KPD rise from 9 to 17 percent and the SPD fall from about 30 to about 21 percent (p. 229).
- **Violence figures**: 103 killed in the weeks after the SA ban was lifted (p. 75, from Kershaw per endnote 14, p. 228); 19 dead and 104 wounded on Italian election day 1921 (p. 75); estimates of five to six hundred Fascists and two thousand others killed in 1920-22, a further thousand in 1923-26, with Paxton's own caveat that the statistics are unreliable (p. 75). Endnote 16 reports a higher estimate by Petersen (p. 228).
- **Cases of failed coups**: Romania 1938-41 (p. 77); Austria July 1934 (p. 77); Hitler's own 1923 putsch (p. 72).
- **Party-internal conflict**: Mussolini's resignation over the pacification pact, August 1921 (p. 80); the Stennes revolts of 1930-31 and the purge of five hundred SA radicals (p. 80); Strasser (pp. 75-76).
- **Business finance**: German big business preferred von Papen; the NSDAP was nearly broke after November 1932; contributions became major only after power (p. 79). Rests on Henry Turner (endnote 33, p. 229). Italian financing is admitted to be less studied (p. 79).
- **Documents**: the Boxheim papers of 1931, showing Nazi planners expected a communist revolution (p. 84).
- **Occupied and satellite Europe**: Quisling set aside after six days (p. 89); Mussert, Clausen (p. 89); Pétain and Vichy (p. 89), where Paxton cites his own Vichy France (endnote 52, p. 230); Tiso, Horthy, Szálasi (p. 90); Ustaša killings with estimates by group, from Lampe (p. 90, endnote 56, p. 231).
- **Authorities leaned on**: Lyttelton for Italy, Bracher for the German Machtergreifung, Turner for January 1933 and for business, Kershaw, Payne on authoritarian regimes as a barrier to fascism (endnote 22, p. 228), Linz and Farneti on breakdown and narrowing alternatives (endnotes 28 and 61, pp. 229, 231), Schieder, Vivarelli, Chabod, Arendt (p. 82).
- **Counterfactuals**: the alternatives list (pp. 93-94) and the Kornilov scenario (p. 92) are reasoning about what might have happened, not evidence. Paxton labels the first an exercise of the historical imagination (p. 93).
- **Admitted uncertainty**: the king's motive (p. 71); who fired first in Munich (p. 72); whether Mussolini ordered Matteotti's murder (p. 87); the Reichstag fire, where he follows what "most historians" now believe, namely that van der Lubbe acted alone (p. 85).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch03**: the close of ch03 names the final precondition as decision-makers ready to share power (p. 68), and ch04 cashes it out. The ch03 material on Po Valley squadrismo, Giolitti's 1921 list and limited business funding is assumed, with back-references (p. 70, p. 79).
- **Feeds ch05**: the three contenders (leader, party zealots, conservative establishment, p. 86) and the "parallel" structures (p. 68, p. 77) become ch05's account of rule as a permanent tension. The Herrschaftskompromiss (p. 80) is the founding bargain of that regime.
- **Feeds ch06**: the Matteotti crisis and the January 1925 coup (p. 87) are retold in ch06 as a cycle of normalization and radicalization (ch06, p. 120). Franco and Salazar are promised for ch06 (p. 88).
- **Feeds ch07 and ch08**: the minimal model (p. 92) and the Milosevic example (p. 92) anticipate the question of fascism outside the interwar setting; the dismissal of agency theories and of intellectual history at this stage (p. 91) anticipates the review of interpretations.
- **Tension, my observation**: Paxton insists the outcome was contingent and chosen (p. 81, p. 94), yet also says it is hard to believe fascists could come to power any other way (p. 92) and that at each fork elites choose the antisocialist solution (p. 94). The contingency is in whether elites call fascists in; the route, if they arrive at all, is treated as close to fixed. The two claims are compatible but the chapter's rhetoric of openness is stronger than its model.
- **Tension, my observation**: the generalisation that fascists reach power only through conservative allies rests on two cases, and the hedge "at least in the cases so far known" (p. 78) concedes this. The comparative cases mostly show that fascists without such allies failed, which supports necessity but cannot show sufficiency.
- **Tension, my observation**: occupation fascisms are set aside as probably not authentic (pp. 90-91), and Milosevic is admitted as something that "looks very much like fascism" produced by leaders already in power (p. 92). Both adjust the category at its edges in ways that protect the allies thesis; Croatia, where native fascists did rule, is explained by the absence of an existing elite (p. 90).
- **Accuracy flags, my observations, to check before reuse**: (1) Von Papen is said to have lifted the SA ban on June 16, 1932 (p. 75, p. 79) but also to have been chancellor from July (p. 75) and to have succeeded Brüning in July 1932 (p. 78); the text is inconsistent with itself. (2) The March 1933 election is dated March 6 (p. 76) and March 5 (p. 85). (3) The Reichstag fire is dated February 28, 1933 (p. 85); it is usually dated to the night of February 27, with the decree on the 28th. (4) Endnote 37 names Funk and Goering as the two other Nazis in Hitler's first cabinet (p. 229); as far as I know the Interior Minister was Wilhelm Frick and Goering held no Reich portfolio at first, so this note looks wrong. (5) Slovak independence is placed in May 1939 and the Ustaša takeover in May 1941 (p. 90); both are usually dated a month or two earlier. None of these affects the argument.

## Open questions

- How does Paxton know the alternatives were live? The coalition of Popolari and reformist socialists is called an arithmetic possibility that was neither tried nor wanted (pp. 93-94), and the German equivalent a real possibility only with strong presidential leadership (p. 94). If the actors would not consider them, in what sense were they available? Synthesis should compare this with the structural accounts he sets aside.
- Is "deadlock" doing too much work? Italy's parliament was "never as completely deadlocked as the German one" (p. 84), and Mussolini's thirty-five seats added little to any majority (p. 81). So the majority-without-the-Left motive explains Germany far better than Italy, where the operative factors look more like squadrist violence and the king's fear of using the army (p. 71).
- The claim that fascists split workers from Marxism (p. 82) sits beside the admission that worker recruitment was always below workers' share of the population (p. 82). Paxton's bridge is that a few workers plus violence sufficed. Is there evidence that conservatives valued this, or is it inferred?
- Does the model in p. 92 (polarization, deadlock, mass mobilization, elite complicity) distinguish fascist arrival from other right-wing takeovers? The Kornilov discussion suggests the discriminating factor is a mobilised mass public under an established democracy (p. 92); check whether ch08 makes this explicit.
- How much of the German account depends on Turner's Thirty Days (endnote 17, p. 228), which is itself a strongly contingency-minded reading?
- Paxton says a military authoritarian government would have been preferable to Hitler but the army did not want it (p. 94). What is the evidence for the army's preference in January 1933, given the Schleicher episode (pp. 75-76)?
