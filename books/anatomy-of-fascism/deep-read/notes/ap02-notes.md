# ap02: Notes (pp. 203–257)

Scope note. This is a selective record of the substantive endnotes, not a full digest. I skimmed all the endnotes for chapters 1 to 8 and the four footnotes to the bibliographical essay: 732 notes in all (90, 111, 88, 62, 132, 80, 91 and 74 by chapter, plus 4). By my estimate roughly 70 percent are bare citations, "ibid." entries or internal cross-references; the remaining 30 percent or so carry some argument, qualification, number or judgment. About 120 of those are recorded below, grouped by the chapter they annotate. Note numbers are given as "n. 35" and are Paxton's own; page cites are to the page markers of this text. Internal cross-references in the notes (for example "see chapter 8, pp. 216–18") use the printed pagination, which differs from the page markers.

The chapter file begins with the last page of the bibliographical essay (p. 203), covered in the ap01 note, and ends with a copyright and cataloguing tail (p. 257) of no analytical content. There is no about-the-author text in this file beyond a photograph credit (p. 257).

## Question this chapter answers

What do the endnotes add to the main text? Three things: (1) Paxton's direct judgments on other historians and interpretive schools, often blunter than in the chapters; (2) numbers and factual qualifications that the narrative states loosely; (3) concessions, where a note admits that a boundary is blurred, a dataset is weak, or a debate is unresolved.

## Leading propositions

1. Paxton holds anti-Semitism to be instrumental to fascism, not its essence (ch. 1 n. 35, p. 206).
2. A general concept covering both Fascism and Nazism is defensible because every fascism mobilises against an enemy whose identity the national culture supplies (ch. 1 n. 85, p. 212).
3. Fascist and communist anti-bourgeois postures are different in kind, and treating them as one is careless reading (ch. 1 n. 41, p. 207).
4. The March on Rome was militarily a bluff: about nine thousand Blackshirts faced a larger, better-armed garrison (ch. 4 n. 1, p. 227).
5. Mass society theory is refuted; fascism conquered dense associational networks, not atomised individuals (ch. 8 nn. 21, 23, 24, p. 252).
6. The line between authoritarian and fascist regimes is blurred in practice, and even fascists achieved only shallow consent (ch. 8 n. 65, p. 256).
7. The Stalin-Hitler equation is rejected on the ground that Nazi killing became an end in itself (ch. 8 nn. 43-46, p. 254).
8. Mussolini's colonial brutality should be treated as central to his regime, not peripheral (ch. 1 n. 81, p. 211; ch. 5 n. 82, p. 237; ch. 6 n. 68, p. 244).

## Arguments (premises -> conclusion)

**A. Why generic fascism survives the race objection (p. 212).** Sternhell and Bracher deny that a general theory can combine Fascism and Nazism, mainly because biological racism was central to one and weak in the other (n. 85). Paxton replies that "all fascisms mobilize against some enemy" and that national culture fixes who the enemy is (n. 85, p. 212). Supporting notes: Italian racism was cultural-historical but "no less aggressively competitive" (ch. 2 n. 32, p. 215); Sarfatti stresses Italian roots of the 1938 laws over Nazi influence (ch. 6 n. 66, pp. 243-244); one adult Italian Jew in three was a party member in 1938 (ch. 1 n. 31, p. 206).

**B. Against totalitarian equivalence (pp. 253-254).** Bracher preferred "totalitarian" because "fascist" blurred dictatorship and democracy for Marxists (ch. 8 n. 39); the concept informs Burleigh, and Malia dismisses fascism as a category (n. 40). Against this: the Black Book's death-count comparison (n. 43); the Ukrainian genocide charge is doubted even there (n. 45); Bullock finds no Soviet counterpart to killing as an end (n. 46); Mommsen criticises the theory (n. 47). Paxton also notes that the camps comparison means Dachau, not Auschwitz (n. 42). Conclusion: the notes side with comparative fascism over totalitarianism while conceding the comparison is serious.

**C. Against the capitalist-agent and the anti-capitalist readings alike (pp. 207, 223, 229, 251).** Weber said hostility to capitalism united all fascists, though opportunism limited it (ch. 1 n. 39). Turner is "authoritative" because he measured Nazi funding against that of other parties (ch. 3 n. 38), and business worry about Nazi radicalism rose in 1932 (ch. 4 n. 33). IG Farben held back, Daimler backed the party, both profited (ch. 3 n. 41). Yet the "fundamental incompatibility" of capitalism and fascism asserted by Milward and Payne "fits poorly" how the regimes worked in normal times (ch. 8 n. 9, p. 251). Conclusion: neither instrument nor enemy; accommodation.

**D. Modernity (pp. 208-210).** Older work saw a revolt against modernity (Mosse, Stern, Parsons, Winkler), with the aside that Italy has no equivalent literature (ch. 1 n. 48). Gregor and Zitelmann see modernisers; Zitelmann "admits" his Hitler is one who might have been had he won (n. 49). Herf reconciles the two; Turner sees modern means to an anti-modern agrarian utopia (n. 55); Mommsen sees simulation (n. 56); Peukert and Bauman tie the Holocaust to science and bureaucratic rationality (n. 58). Conclusion: Paxton presents the debate as open and leans toward Mommsen and Peukert.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

The following is my analysis, not Paxton's.

- **The sharpest commitments are in the notes, not the text.** "I see it as instrumental" (ch. 1 n. 35, p. 206) is one of the book's most contestable positions and it is made in a footnote. Anyone assessing Paxton's treatment of Nazism within generic fascism should start from nn. 35 and 85 of chapter 1.
- **The blurred border undercuts a load-bearing distinction.** Chapters 1, 4, 6 and 8 rely on the authoritarian-fascist line (ch. 1 n. 34, p. 206). Chapter 8 n. 65 concedes it is "blurred" in practice (p. 256), and nn. 50 and 71 entertain Kasza's third category (pp. 230, 256-257). The notes on Franco (ch. 6 n. 4, p. 240; ch. 8 n. 67, p. 256) praise the case for calling his regime fascist that the main text does not adopt.
- **Goldhagen is condemned and used.** Ch. 1 n. 30 calls the book a "primitive demonization" (p. 206); ch. 6 n. 57 cites it for evidence (p. 243). The two are consistent (bad thesis, useful documents) but the reader has to assemble that.
- **Marxism is handled briefly.** One note (ch. 8 n. 6, p. 251) credits Gramsci, Togliatti, Bloch and Poulantzas; another gives Bonapartism (ch. 2 n. 64, p. 217). There is no sustained engagement with Marxist economic history of the regimes; Mason is the Marxist-influenced scholar actually used.
- **Data quality is conceded for Italy.** Italian electoral and membership data are "much shakier" than German (ch. 8 n. 29, p. 253), squadrismo figures derive from Fascist Party sources via Tasca (ch. 3 n. 23, p. 222), and no study exists of the limits of Mussolini's power (ch. 5 n. 41, p. 234). Italian halves of Paxton's comparisons rest on thinner evidence than German halves.
- **A probable factual slip.** Ch. 4 n. 37 lists the other Nazis in Hitler's first cabinet as "Economics Minister Walter Funk and Interior Minister Hermann Goering" (p. 229). From general knowledge, not from this book: the Reich Interior Minister in January 1933 was Wilhelm Frick, Goering entered as minister without portfolio and Prussian interior minister, and Funk became economics minister only in 1938. The note's point (few Nazis in the cabinet, control of police) survives, but the names should not be quoted from it.
- **Small inconsistencies in the apparatus.** Griffin's Nature of Fascism is dated 1994 in the essay (p. 176) and 1991 in ch. 1 n. 65 (p. 210); "Griffith" appears for Griffin in ch. 1 n. 87 (p. 212); Kocka appears as "Kocha" and Eley as "Ely" (ch. 3 n. 73, p. 225; ch. 8 n. 19, p. 252). These look like copy or EPUB errors and do not affect the argument.

## Open questions

- If anti-Semitism is instrumental (p. 206), what in Paxton's account explains why the Nazi regime pursued extermination at the cost of its war effort? The chapter 6 notes on decision timing (p. 242) describe the process but do not connect it to the instrumentality claim.
- Does the nine-thousand figure for the March on Rome (p. 227) rest on Répaci alone, and has later work revised it?
- Paxton says mass society theory is "convincingly refuted" (p. 252). Does the associational-capture evidence from Marburg and Northeim generalise to Italy, where he admits the data are weaker (p. 253)?
- How far does Kasza's intermediate category (pp. 230, 256-257) dissolve Paxton's stage model for cases like Japan, Portugal and Brazil, which never pass through stages one and two in his sense?
- The 40 percent permanence figure for pre-1930 Nazi members (p. 253) implies heavy churn. Do the class-composition studies Paxton relies on control for it?
