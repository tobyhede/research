# ap01: Bibliographical Essay (pp. 175–203)

Scope note. The chapter file opens with the last lines of chapter 8 (the tail of the "mobilizing passions" list and the closing paragraph, p. 175); those belong to the ch08 note. The essay proper runs from the heading on p. 175 to the Austria entry on p. 203. Its four footnotes are printed at the very end of the endnotes (p. 257). Cross-references inside the book (for example "bibliographical essay, p. 225") use the printed pagination, not the page markers used here.

## Question this chapter answers

What scholarship does Paxton build on, and how does he judge it? The essay is "a personal choice of works that were particularly helpful to me" (p. 175), organised to mirror the book's own stages: general works and interpretations (I-II), biographies (III), then creation and rooting (IV), getting power (V), exercising power (VI), radicalization (VII), fascism elsewhere (VIII) and after 1945 (IX). Because Paxton attaches a verdict to most titles, the essay doubles as a statement of his historiographical position: which approaches he thinks explain fascism (local, social and political studies of how it took root, allied and ruled) and which he thinks only describe or decode it (ideological genealogy, one-sentence definitions, psychology, cultural decoding).

## Leading propositions

1. The literature is unmasterable, so the essay is avowedly selective and personal. De Felice's bibliography alone lists 12,208 items, mostly on Italy (p. 175); footnote 1 adds that about two thousand of them concern generic fascism and the war (p. 257).
2. The best narrative survey (Payne) describes rather than explains, and the most influential definition (Griffin) risks closing down the questions Paxton cares about: how fascism worked, and with whom (p. 176).
3. Tracing ideological lineage has been "the dominant approach" to fascism's origins, but it cannot explain fascism's later course; for that one needs political and social settings, interests, allies and regional variation (p. 178).
4. Psychological explanations all face the same objection: the emotional experiences alleged cannot be shown to differ between countries that went fascist and those that did not (p. 180).
5. Fascist arrival in power presupposes the failure of democracy, a subject neglected because leaders are assumed to have done everything themselves (p. 181).
6. Voting and membership research has dissolved the lower-middle-class thesis: Nazism drew from all classes, the SA heavily from workers, and resistance to it tracked communal anchoring (Catholic, Marxist) more than class (p. 182).
7. German big business did not buy Hitler: Turner's archival work shows industrialists funded all non-Marxist parties, distrusted Hitler, and preferred Papen; the Nazis financed themselves largely from rallies and small gifts (p. 183).
8. Fascist rule is no longer seen as the emanation of a dictator's will; the live question is whether the project was imposed by force, spread by propaganda, or "negotiated around converging interests with powerful elements in society" (p. 183).
9. Terror was selective and rested on willing cooperation, above all denunciation (p. 185).
10. Cultural studies of fascist ritual are valuable only when they tie culture to institutions and society; some treat decoding as an end in itself (p. 191).
11. Radicalization is mostly a German story, but Italian Fascism radicalized through empire, and war was "integral" to fascism: German success opened the way to party rule in the east and the Final Solution, Italian failure destroyed the regime's legitimacy (pp. 191-192).
12. Outside Germany and Italy, the question in each country is whether "fascist" fits at all; Paxton reports the arguments on both sides and usually leans toward caution (France pp. 197-198, Japan p. 201, Argentina p. 200, China p. 201).

## Arguments (premises -> conclusion)

**A. Against definition-first and lineage-first approaches (pp. 176, 178).**
- Griffin's work is "the most influential recent attempt to define fascism", but his "zeal to reduce fascism to one pithy sentence" is "more likely to inhibit than to stimulate analysis of how and with whom it worked" (p. 176).
- Payne's survey is "prodigiously learned" yet "it describes better than it explains" (p. 176).
- Ideological genealogy (Gentile, Sternhell for Italy; Mosse, Stern for Germany) is acknowledged as dominant and "important" (p. 178).
- But "one must also look at the political and social settings" to understand fascism's later course and following (p. 178).
- Conclusion: the explanatory weight should fall on studies of how fascism came "to represent certain specific interests and engage important allies" (p. 178), hence the long list of local and regional monographs that follows (pp. 179).

**B. Why structural preconditions are informative but incomplete (pp. 179-180).**
- Kocka: persistent pre-industrial elites; Eley: crisis of the capitalist state; Luebbert: coalition building; Moore: agrarian routes to modernity (pp. 179-180).
- De Meur and Berg-Schlosser's multivariate system exposes the many-variables, few-cases problem, and "their approach necessarily leaves out leaders' individual choices" (p. 180).
- Conclusion (implicit, and consistent with the book's last paragraph on p. 175): preconditions set the stage; choices by leaders and elites decide the outcome.

**C. Against psychological explanation (p. 180).**
- Theweleit documents masculine-fraternity fantasies, "though similar fantasies may have existed in countries that did not go fascist" (p. 180).
- Loewenberg roots the Nazi youth cohort in the hunger winter of 1917 and absent fathers, "though the children of all belligerent countries suffered the latter" (p. 180).
- Brustein's opposite claim, rational self-interest among early Nazi members, rests on "problematical" statistics and reaches a "controversial" conclusion (p. 180).
- Conclusion: "The problem with all psychological explanations is that it is very difficult to prove that the emotional experiences of Italians and Germans differed decisively from those of, say, the French." (p. 180)

**D. Power is opened from outside as much as seized (pp. 181-183).**
- The failure of democracy is "too often overlooked because so many assume that the fascist leader did everything himself" (p. 181); Linz and Stepan is the "rare and valuable" study, with Farneti on Italy singled out (p. 181).
- Turner's Thirty Days is the best account of "the contingencies, uncertainties, and choices" in Hitler's last steps (p. 181).
- Voters came from all classes; "Class seems to have mattered less than culture." (p. 182)
- Funding did not come mainly from big business (p. 183).
- Conclusion: neither a class movement nor a capitalist instrument; an alliance outcome that depended on choices.

**E. From monolith to negotiated rule (pp. 183-185).**
- Older studies stress control from above (Bracher) (p. 183).
- Fraenkel and Neumann founded, and Broszat and Mommsen refined, the picture of coexistence between constitutional and conservative structures and "capricious party rule", with Hitler arbitrating among competing agencies (pp. 183-184).
- For Italy, De Felice stressed personal rule and consensus; Gentile argues the regime made real totalitarian progress in the 1930s, but is "less interested in the problem of how the Fascist project was altered and subverted in the process of its integration into Italian society" (p. 184).
- Legnani, de Bernardi, Lupo and the Del Boca volume carry the polycratic approach into Italy (p. 184).
- Conclusion: Paxton sides with the polycratic school for both regimes and treats Gentile's totalitarian reading as one-sided.

**F. The French case as a test of definitions (pp. 196-198).**
- Rémond and Berstein: fascism foreign to France (p. 197). Soucy, Sternhell, Irvine: highly developed or even original there (p. 197).
- Sternhell reaches "impregnation" by "assigning a broad range of nationalist and conservative authors to the fascist camp" (p. 197); footnote 4 observes that Girardet had used the same word in 1955 "with impunity" (p. 257).
- Müller "rejects the usual lists" of symptoms and follows the French Right through time "to show that conservatives did not need fascism" (p. 196).
- Passmore "sensibly judges the league fascist, more on behavioral than ideological grounds, but not the party" (p. 197).
- Paxton's own rule: the PSF's turn to elections "in no way by itself makes it nonfascist", since elections were essential to Nazis and Fascists while taking root and coming to power (p. 198).
- Conclusion: classification should rest on behaviour over time and on whether conservatives needed fascist help, not on ideological checklists.

## Evidence offered

Section by section, with the works Paxton treats as essential and his verdicts.

**I. General works (pp. 175-177).** Payne, A History of Fascism: most authoritative narrative, weak on explanation (p. 176). Milza, Les fascismes: well informed (p. 176). Griffin: most influential definition, criticised (p. 176). Short introductions: Passmore "very brief but lively"; Neocleous takes a cultural-studies line in which fascism is "the dark side of modernity and capitalism"; Morgan a careful narrative to 1945; Eatwell gives half his space to the postwar period (p. 176). De Grand is "the classic short introduction" to Fascist Italy (p. 176). Collective works: Larsen, Hagtvet and Myklebust, Who Were the Fascists; Laqueur; Rogger and Weber; Woolf (p. 176). Documents: Noakes and Pridham "outstanding" (p. 176); Weber's Varieties of Fascism chosen to illustrate his thesis that fascism was revolutionary in nature (p. 177).

**II. Interpretations (p. 177).** De Felice, Interpretations of Fascism, faulting general approaches and ending in the view that "each regime was unique and that no general interpretation works" (p. 177). Ayçoberry, Wippermann, Nolte's reader as guides to the theories (p. 177). Totalitarianism: Friedrich and Brzezinski "the most substantial analysis"; Gleason "expertly reviews the long debate" (p. 177). Authoritarianism: Linz (p. 177). This section is strikingly short (four paragraphs) and names no Marxist theorist.

**III. Biographies (pp. 177-178).** Kershaw's Hitler is "preeminent" because it relates the dictator "to the society that imagined him" (p. 177). Bullock and Fest still useful; Hamann on Vienna; Gordon on the putsch (p. 177). Psychoanalytic lives (Langer, Waite, Binion) are reported as an irresistible temptation; Redlich "is more cautious" (p. 177). Jäckel insists Hitler had a programme; Turner shows his social Darwinism covered economy and society (p. 178). Mussolini: Bosworth the fullest in English, presenting "a clever but hollow opportunist"; Milza "well informed, balanced, and thoughtful"; Mack Smith "condescending, and thin on the broader setting" (p. 178). De Felice's seven volumes are "the biography of reference", called "uneven and idiosyncratic but exhaustively documented" (p. 178); assessed by Painter, by Gentile, and "more critically" by Knox (p. 178). Passerini on Mussolini's image, with the caveat that "his images were more the result of his power than an explanation for it" (p. 178).

**IV. Creation and taking root (pp. 178-181).** Vivarelli on origins (p. 178). Ideological lineage: Gentile, Sternhell, Mosse, Stern (p. 178). Local studies, which Paxton plainly prizes: Corner, Fascism in Ferrara, "the most sophisticated and probing account" of one Italian locality (pp. 178-179); Snowden, Cardoza, Kelikian, Steinberg on the south (p. 179). For Germany: Allen's single-town study "the reader should not miss"; Koshar on Nazi takeover of associational life; Noakes, Pridham, Grill, Heberle on the states (p. 179). SA: Fischer, Longerich (p. 179). Preconditions: Kocka versus Eley, Luebbert, de Meur and Berg-Schlosser, Moore (pp. 179-180). Rational choice: Brustein (p. 180). Irrational appeal: Theweleit, Spackman, Adorno, Fromm, Loewenberg (p. 180). Veterans: Prost "the richest study of any European country's veterans" (p. 181), with the aside that veterans were key to early recruitment "though many were younger" (pp. 180-181).

**V. Getting power (pp. 181-183).** Lyttelton, The Seizure of Power: "the most penetrating analysis in any language" of Mussolini's arrival (p. 181). Tasca still worth reading (p. 181). Turner, Hitler's Thirty Days; Bracher, Schulz and Sauer for the longer view (p. 181). Democratic breakdown: Linz and Stepan; Bracher on Weimar's dissolution; Mommsen, Peukert, Kolb; Larry Jones on the collapse of the liberal centre (pp. 181-182). Farmers: Gies and Zofka (p. 182). Voters: Hamilton "first proved" upper-class support; Childers, Falter (p. 182). Members: Mühlberger, Fischer; Petersen for Italy (p. 182). Methodological caution: social-composition studies "need to distinguish among different stages", because movement-stage membership fluctuated and parties in power gained bandwagoners (p. 182). Parties: Gentile "the first serious history" of the PNF; Kater and Orlow on the NSDAP (pp. 182-183). Money: Turner, German Big Business; Renzi "definitively" settles who paid for Mussolini's paper in 1914-15; Italian Fascist finance "less studied" (p. 183).

**VI. Exercising power (pp. 183-191).** Interpretive guides: Kershaw, The Nazi Dictatorship; Bosworth, The Italian Dictatorship, "enlightening despite a testy polemical edge" and "highly critical of De Felice, his student Emilio Gentile, and cultural studies" (p. 183). Structure of rule: Bracher (top-down); Fraenkel, Neumann, Broszat, Mommsen, Frei (pp. 183-184); Legnani, de Bernardi, Burrin, Del Boca, Lupo, Dogliani for Italy (p. 184). Terror and consent: Gellately, Johnson ("a superior synthesis"), Franzinelli, Canosa; Corner's "timely reminder of the harsh side" of Mussolini's regime (p. 185). Youth and education: Koon, Rempel, Peukert on the failure of youth mobilisation (p. 185). Church: Jemolo, Binchy, Pollard, Kent; communism was "considered the greater threat" (pp. 185-186). Bureaucracy, army, judiciary, medicine: Mommsen, Caplan, Melis, Craig, Rochat, Gruchmann, Proctor, Kater, Lifton; legal professions "equally crucial, have been less studied" (p. 186). Business: Hayes on IG Farben, which adapted to autarky and "profited mightily", moved more by "a narrow business success ethic" than ideology; Bellon finds Daimler-Benz "more enthusiastic"; Feldman on insurers keeping "some independence" (pp. 186-187). Italian industry: Sarti "argues that the industrialists got most of what they wanted"; Adler concurs; Melograni "has been criticized for overemphasizing conflicts" (p. 187). Workers: Tim Mason is "the most thoughtful scholar of labor under the Nazis"; Lüdtke, Herbert, Overy (pp. 187-188). Women: De Grazia "indispensable" for Italy; the German victims-or-collaborators debate reviewed by Grossmann and von Saldern; fascism "provocatively called" a boy's ideology, though some women supported it and were helped "selectively and in demeaningly paternalistic ways" (pp. 188-189). Peasants "did not always benefit" (p. 189). Comparison: usually paired articles rather than "sustained comparison"; De Grand and Levy are the exceptions (pp. 189-190). Public opinion: Kershaw's Hitler Myth and Bavaria study; Colarizi (p. 190). Intellectuals and culture: Bobbio, Turi, Isnenghi; Gentile's Sacralization, Falasca-Zamponi, Ben-Ghiat, Stone as examples that "successfully relate culture to institutions and society" (pp. 190-191); Roberts reviews the genre "with some asperity", Griffin "with enthusiasm" (p. 191). Foreign policy: Weinberg "magisterial" (p. 191).

**VII. Radicalization (pp. 191-194).** Mommsen's cumulative radicalization (p. 191). Italy: Milza's chapter on radicalisation; Del Boca on gas in Ethiopia and racial law in the empire; Segrè; Mack Smith criticised for making empire look like a whim; Goglia and Grassi as corrective (pp. 191-192). War: Deist et al.; Rich; Knox, "the main authority in English on Italy's war", who attributes it to Mussolini's expansionism (p. 192). Salò: Klinkhammer, Deakin (pp. 192-193). Cleansing: Burleigh and Wippermann, The Racial State; Burleigh, Friedlander, Aly, Bock (pp. 193-194). Holocaust: Saul Friedländer "the masterful new synthesis"; Browning "the most convincing current work in English on how the Holocaust was carried out"; Herbert's collection for new German research on the wider ethnic-redrawing project (p. 194).

**VIII. Fascism elsewhere (pp. 194-202).** Country lists, of uneven depth. France gets by far the longest treatment (pp. 196-198); Britain: Thurlow "essential", Skidelsky "empathetic enough to raise hackles" (p. 195); Romania: Weber reads the Legion as "truly revolutionary", a reading Paxton qualifies (p. 199); Spain: Preston and Richards make the case that Franco's regime was fascist "at least for the period up to 1945" (p. 200). Outside Europe: Payne is "skeptical"; Larsen "adopts a broad-church approach" (p. 200). Argentina: Rock finds nationalists "more reactionary than fascist"; Germani (p. 200). China: Wakeman says the Blueshirts "were not fascist" (p. 201). Japan: Kasza "authoritative"; Duus and Okimoto, Wilson, Furuya doubt the label; Brooker argues Japan mobilised best of the three (p. 201). United States: Lipset and Raab's "bestiary", Brinkley, MacLean on the Klan, Ribuffo; Sheehan on El Salvador as US support for "something very like fascism overseas" (p. 202).

**IX. Since 1945 (pp. 202-203).** Prowe's article is the recommended starting point (p. 202); Hainsworth, Betz, Kitschelt, Ignazi ("particularly thoughtful and well informed") (pp. 202-203); Ferraresi and Ignazi for Italy; Perrineau and Mayer on Front National voters (p. 203).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

The following is my analysis, not Paxton's.

- **The essay is the book's method stated as taste.** Its preferences (local studies over genealogy, alliances over ideas, polycracy over monolith, stages over snapshots) are the same commitments that organise chapters 2 to 6. The remark that social-composition studies must "distinguish among different stages" (p. 182) is the five-stage scheme applied to other people's data.
- **Griffin is criticised but not answered here.** The complaint about a "pithy" definition (p. 176) sits oddly beside chapter 8, where Paxton offers his own one-paragraph definition and the list of mobilizing passions whose tail opens this file (p. 175). The difference he would claim is that his definition comes last and is behavioural; the essay does not argue the point.
- **Rival interpretations are thinly represented in the essay itself.** Section II covers only De Felice's scepticism, totalitarianism and authoritarianism (p. 177). There is no entry for Marxist theory beyond a passing reference to "open-minded Marxists" in the Quazza volume (p. 184) and Trotsky on 1934 (p. 196); nothing on political religion as a concept (Gentile's Sacralization is filed under culture, p. 191); and the Sonderweg appears only through Kocka versus Eley (p. 179), without the word. Paxton's actual judgments on those schools are in chapter 8 and in the endnotes (see the ap02 note), not here.
- **Gentile is treated coolly throughout.** He is cited as essential on ideology, the party and sacralized politics (pp. 178, 182, 191), yet the only explicit criticism of a living Italian scholar is of his totalitarian reading (p. 184), and Bosworth's hostility to him is reported without demurral (p. 183). This matches chapter 5's preference for the dual-state model.
- **Asymmetry of coverage.** Germany and Italy dominate; France receives more space than any other third country (pp. 196-198), reflecting Paxton's own field. Eastern Europe is thin (single titles for the Baltic states, Croatia, Czechoslovakia, Poland, pp. 195, 199), and Paxton says of the one regional survey that it "is more descriptive than analytical" (p. 196). Claims in chapters 3, 4 and 7 about Hungary, Romania and beyond rest on a narrow shelf.
- **Self-citation is restrained.** Paxton lists his own Vichy France only for the fascist-or-authoritarian question (p. 198).
- **Date.** The newest items are from 2001-2002, so the essay predates, for example, Tooze, Evans's trilogy, and Mann's Fascists; later sessions comparing books should not expect them here.

## Open questions

- If psychological explanations fail because the experiences were not nationally distinctive (p. 180), does the same objection apply to Paxton's own "mobilizing passions" (p. 175), which were also widespread in countries where fascism failed? He would presumably answer that passions are necessary and alliances decisive, but the essay does not say so.
- Paxton accepts Turner on business funding as settled (p. 183) while also endorsing Hayes, Sarti and Adler on how well business did under the regimes (pp. 186-187). How much of the "capitalist instrument" thesis survives once the question shifts from who paid before power to who benefited after?
- He reports Brustein's rational-choice finding as methodologically doubtful (p. 180). What exactly are the objections, and do they affect the membership figures Paxton himself uses?
- Is "Class seems to have mattered less than culture" (p. 182) compatible with the weight chapters 3 and 4 give to agrarian and industrial elites' class fears? The claim concerns voters; the alliance argument concerns elites. The essay does not join them.
- For Spain, Paxton reports the Preston and Richards case that Franco's regime was fascist to 1945 (p. 200) without saying here whether he accepts it; chapter 6 treats Franco as authoritarian. Which criterion decides?
