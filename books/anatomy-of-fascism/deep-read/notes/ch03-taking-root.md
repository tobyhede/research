# ch03: Taking Root (pp. 42–68)

## Question this chapter answers

Why did a few fascist movements, out of the many founded after 1918, become serious political players (Paxton's second stage), while most stayed marginal? Paxton's answer: success depended less on the movements' ideas or on national cultural preparation than on the setting. A movement rooted where a liberal state was failing, where a crisis discredited existing parties and opened a political space, where a leader was willing to trim the movement to fit that space, and where established elites and officials were willing to help or look away. The price of rooting was a transformation of the movement itself, usually to the Right.

## Key terms

| Taking root | p. 43 | The second stage of the fascist cycle: a movement becomes a carrier of substantial grievances and interests, able to reward political ambitions, and so becomes a party that competes on equal terms with established ones (p. 43). | Political space | p. 43 | The room available in a political system for a new contender: a definite position in which a movement can obtain practical results, as opposed to indiscriminate protest (p. 43). | Antiparty / movement | p. 44 | The self-description of early fascists: a brotherhood that gathers the whole nation, against both parliamentary faction and socialist class struggle (p. 44). |
| Antipolitics | p. 45 | A posture that appeals to people whose main political motive is scorn for politics (p. 45). |

| Nationalization of the masses | p. 61 | Mosse's phrase (note 70) for the arrival of mass politics, which put notable rule under pressure (p. 61) and which fascism offered the anti-Left techniques to manage (p. 62). |
| Crisis of transition | p. 62 | A second reading of the liberal crisis: the strains of late, fast industrialisation with labour already organised (p. 62). |
| Least-barricaded gate | p. 64 | Trotsky's metaphor for Bolshevism's breakthrough in a weak country, applied by Paxton to fascism: it enters weak or failed liberal states, not triumphant ones (p. 64). |

| Polarization | p. 66 | A climate in which fascists and communists each profit from showing the centre to be inadequate (p. 66); in postrevolutionary western Europe it favoured fascism (p. 67). |

## Leading propositions

- Nearly every nation with mass politics produced a fascist-like movement between the wars; the fact of foundation therefore explains little, and only the few that grew are of urgent interest (pp. 42-43, 59).
- Taking root means becoming a carrier of real grievances and interests and a vehicle for political ambition, so that influential people think the movement can serve them better than a conventional party (p. 43).
- Rooting forces choices. A movement must leave indiscriminate protest, find a definite political space, and offer measurable benefits to followers and partners (p. 43).
- At this stage rhetoric can be tested against action. The test shows that fascist anticapitalism was highly selective and that the space fascism actually found was as a remedy against socialist revolution (p. 43).
- Only leaders who put power above purity rooted their movements. Mussolini and Hitler did; José Antonio, Maurras, Szálasi and D'Annunzio did not (pp. 44, 46).
- Rooting meant allying with parts of the establishment the early movement had denounced, and losing early followers while gaining new ones (pp. 44-45, 48).
- Fascist parties offered a new political style: an antipolitics, a promise to unite and not divide, and the first catch-all parties of engagement (p. 45).
- Italian Fascism was saved from the failure of 1919 by squadrismo in the Po Valley, where it served landowners against socialist labour organisation and substituted for a state that would not act (pp. 45-48).
- The movement was reshaped by this: its social composition and its programme moved Right, and so no fixed essence can be read from the early programmes (pp. 48-50).
- Nazism rooted in the Depression by turning from an urban to a rural strategy and by assembling many particular, sometimes incompatible, grievances into a catch-all vote (pp. 50-52).
- German business did not create or mainly finance Hitler before 1933; it hedged and preferred traditional conservatives (pp. 51-52).
- Violence and electioneering were complementary. Selective violence against people seen as outside the nation helped win votes (p. 52).
- In France fascism failed to root because the crisis was less severe, the republic never deadlocked, conservatives did not feel threatened enough to seek fascist help, and no leader united the rival movements (p. 56).
- The French rural case shows the mechanism: where the state protected the harvest and conservative farm organisations delivered services, the Greenshirts found little space (pp. 56-57).
- A single large protest vote does not root a party; it must come to represent important interests and gratify career politicians (p. 58).
- Intellectual and cultural preparation does not predict where fascism succeeded; antiliberal thought was common to all European states, and what differed were political, social and economic preconditions (pp. 60-61).
- The most important precondition was a faltering liberal order, which Paxton reads in four ways (mass politics, late industrialisation, heterogeneous social structure, cultural strain); Italy and Germany fit all four (pp. 61-63).
- Fascist success tracks, closely but not exactly, the map of defeat in the First World War and the map of attempted or feared Bolshevik revolution (pp. 63-64).
- Fascism is a phenomenon of weak or failed liberal states, not of triumphant ones (p. 64).
- Conservatism, liberalism and the Left had each lost the capacity to voice postwar feelings; the Left in particular lost its monopoly on anger because it had no room for the nation (pp. 65-66).
- Fascist violence carried calculated messages, and its acceptance by law-and-order conservatives and the middle class was an essential step (pp. 66-67).
- Parallel structures are a defining characteristic of fascism (pp. 67-68).
- Success depended as much on allies and accomplices as on the movements; the final essential precondition was decision-makers ready to share power (p. 68).
- None of this is deterministic: a country with every precondition need not become fascist (pp. 63, 64, 68).

## Arguments

**A. From protest to political space (pp. 43-45)**

- **Premises**: (p. 43) Effective participation in electoral or pressure-group politics requires usefulness to partners and concrete advantages for followers. (p. 43) That requirement forces a movement to reveal its priorities. (p. 43) In practice the priorities were antisocialism and the protection of national producers; the Left-nationalist programme failed at the polls in Milan in 1919. (p. 44) Leaders differed in whether they would pay the price of purity. (p. 45) Rooting also needed a new style that attracted voters disgusted with politics and parties tied to class or confession.
- **Unstated premise**: that what a movement does when forced to choose shows what it "really" is, more than what it said when it was free of constraint. This is the method of the whole book applied to stage two.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 43, 45) Rooted fascism is an antisocialist catch-all party allied with parts of the establishment, keeping some revolutionary aura. How it kept that aura is left as a mystery (p. 45).

**B. The Po Valley case: substitution for a failing state (pp. 45-50)**

- **Premises**: (p. 47) After November 1919 socialists controlled the agricultural labour market through the Labor Exchanges and squeezed landowners financially and in status. (p. 47) The liberal state under Giolitti declined to break strikes, and local officials were socialist or passive, so landowners felt abandoned. (p. 47) Veterans already hated socialists for their wartime stance. (p. 47) The squads supplied force, and (pp. 47-48) also jobs and small parcels of land, which the socialists' collectivist goal could not match. (p. 48) Police, army officers and some prefects lent arms, trucks and tolerance. (p. 48) The squads came to supplant the state locally.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 48-50) Fascism became a de facto power that national politicians could not ignore, and Giolitti took it into his 1921 coalition (p. 50). The movement that did this was no longer the movement of 1919: purists left, sons of landowners and policemen joined (p. 48), and the programme dropped pacifism, nationalisation, confiscatory taxation, anticlericalism and republicanism between 1919 and 1922 (p. 49).
- **Counterfactual Paxton asserts**: without the Po Valley transformation Mussolini would have remained an obscure failed agitator (p. 50).

**C. Why Mussolini and not D'Annunzio (p. 46)**

- **Premises**: (p. 46) D'Annunzio was the greater celebrity in 1919-20 and invented the fascist theatrical repertoire at Fiume. (p. 46) He staked everything on Fiume, cared for the purity of gesture more than power, lost his conservative backers, and was dispersed by the navy. (p. 46) Mussolini made deals with centrist politicians and (pp. 46-47) served economic and social interests as well as nationalist feeling.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 46) Fascism cannot be interpreted primarily through its cultural expressions.

**D. Schleswig-Holstein: discredit of the old parties plus targeted appeals (pp. 50-53)**

- **Premises**: (p. 50) The Nazis failed in 1918-23 and with an urban strategy in 1924 and 1928. (pp. 50-51) Farm prices collapsed; cattle farmers lost faith in the DNVP and the national government, then in the ineffective Landbund. (p. 51) The first process at work is the humiliation of existing leaders and organisations, which opens space. (p. 51) Hitler used new electoral techniques and tailor-made appeals to particular occupations, indifferent to contradiction, while other parties stayed tied to one class or interest. (p. 52) Liberal parties were blamed for the twin crises of Versailles and the Depression.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 52-53) By July 1932 the Nazis were the largest party, short of a majority but indispensable to any nonsocialist coalition wanting a popular majority (p. 53). The support was volatile: the farmers would probably have moved on, and the party was losing momentum and money by November 1932, until conservatives rescued it (pp. 51, 53).

**E. Against the "agents of capitalism" account of Nazi funding (pp. 51-52)**

- **Premises**: (p. 52) Thyssen was exceptional; Kirdorf left the party in 1928. (p. 52) Business archives show firms contributed to all nonsocialist formations and more to traditional conservatives, with Papen the favourite. (p. 52) Business was alarmed by Nazi radicals, and Nazi radicalism rose in late 1932. (p. 52) I. G. Farben gave almost nothing before 1933. (p. 52) Much Nazi money came from rally entry fees, sales and small contributions.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 51-52) Hitler was not created by businessmen as a private anticommunist army.

**F. France: the same test, a negative result (pp. 53-57)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 53-54) France had an antirepublican Right, a triple crisis, and a noisy crop of leagues that could topple a government in February 1934. (p. 54) The closer a French movement imitated Mussolini or Hitler, the less it succeeded; the one near-mass movement, La Rocque's PSF, tried to look moderate and republican. (p. 55) Far Right movements other than the PSF lost ground in 1938-39 under Daladier, and Vichy was run by the traditional Right. (p. 56) The Depression was milder, the republic never deadlocked, conservatives did not feel threatened enough, no chief united the rivals. (pp. 56-57) The Greenshirts had a Po Valley-like opening in the 1936-37 harvest strikes but the state sent gendarmes and conservative farm organisations held their members.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 57) Fascist interlopers cannot easily break into a political system that functions tolerably well.
- **Hinge Paxton names himself**: the assessment of French fascism turns on whether La Rocque's movements are counted as fascist (pp. 54-55). He does not give a flat verdict here, but he describes the PSF as a move toward the centre (p. 55) and treats French fascism as a failure.

**G. Preconditions, not predispositions (pp. 59-65)**

- **Premises**: (p. 60) Countries with rich antiliberal intellectual traditions, France above all, did not go fascist. (p. 60) Around 1900 anti-Semitic violence was more visible in France, Algeria, Britain, the United States, Poland and Russia than in Germany. (p. 60) What distinguished Germany was that its army and bureaucracy were less subject to judicial or political oversight in a crisis. (p. 61) The European states resembled each other in antiliberal criticism and differed in political, social and economic preconditions. (pp. 61-63) The liberal order failed as a technique of government (notable rule facing mass electorates), under late industrialisation, in heterogeneous social structures, and under cultural strain. (pp. 63-64) Defeat or thwarted victory and the fear of Bolshevism mark the map of success.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 64) Fascism stems from crises in weak or failed liberalisms. (p. 61) The role of intellectuals is limited to three functions: discrediting liberal regimes, creating poles of protest outside the Left, and making violence respectable (pp. 60-61).

**H. What the successful movement itself supplied (pp. 66-68)**

- **Premises**: (p. 66) One chief had to emerge from many rivals and be willing to reject purity. (p. 66) Violence was a coded message that communist violence was rising, the state was inept, and only fascists were tough enough. (pp. 66-67) Conservatives and the middle class tolerated it because it was reserved for national enemies. (p. 67) Polarisation swept those disillusioned with deference parties into the catch-all party. (pp. 67-68) Parallel structures claimed to do the state's work better.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 68) Even so, success depended as much on allies and accomplices; wherever authorities winked at direct action against the Left, a door opened.

**Position versus argument.** Paxton's position is that the outcome is contingent on choices by leaders and by elites (pp. 63, 68). His argument, however, is mostly a catalogue of structural preconditions. He sees the risk and twice interrupts himself to warn against inevitability (pp. 63, 68).

## Evidence offered

- **Electoral figures.** Schleswig-Holstein voted 51 percent Nazi on July 31, 1932 (p. 50); 64 percent of its rural vote went to the Nazis (p. 51); the Nazis rose from ninth party in 1928 to first in 1932 (p. 51) and reached 37 percent nationally (p. 53). Thirty-five Fascists were elected on Giolitti's list in May 1921 (p. 50). Arrow Cross: about 750,000 votes of 2 million in May 1939 (p. 57). Legion of the Archangel Michael: 15.38 percent and 66 of 390 seats in 1937 (p. 58). Rex: 11.5 percent and 21 of 202 seats in May 1936 (p. 58). Dutch NSB: 7.94 percent in 1935 (p. 58). Quisling: 2.2 percent in 1933 and 1.8 percent in 1936 (p. 58).
- **Squad violence statistics.** In the first six months of 1921 the squads destroyed 17 newspapers and printing works, 59 Peoples' Houses, 119 Chambers of Labor, 107 cooperatives, 83 Peasants' Leagues, 151 socialist clubs and 151 cultural organisations; between January 1 and April 7, 1921, 102 people were killed, of whom 25 were fascists, 41 socialists, 20 police and 16 others (p. 47). Sources are in notes 23 and 24, which I did not open.
- **Narrative cases.** The burning of the Balkan Hotel in Trieste, July 1920 (p. 45); the Bologna city hall attack of November 21, 1920, with six killed (p. 47); Fiume under D'Annunzio (p. 46); February 6, 1934 in Paris, sixteen killed (p. 54); the Olympia meeting of June 1934 and the loss of 90 percent of the BUF's fifty thousand members after the Night of the Long Knives (p. 59); the Potempa murder of August 1932 and Rosenberg's defence of the killers (p. 67).
- **Programme comparison.** Fascist positions on the League of Nations, the army, nationalisation, taxation, Church property and the monarchy traced from the San Sepolcro programme through 1920-22 (p. 49). This is the chapter's most direct documentary evidence for the rightward drift.
- **Testimony of disillusioned insiders.** Gatelli's complaint that Fascism had become "the bodyguard of the profiteers" and D'Annunzio's phrase "agrarian slavery" (p. 48).
- **Business archives.** Cited in general terms for the hedging of German firms; named cases are Thyssen, Kirdorf and I. G. Farben (p. 52). The text names no historian, but the notes show the argument rests on Henry A. Turner's German Big Business and the Rise of Hitler (note 38, which calls it authoritative because it compares Nazi receipts with other parties'), Peter Hayes on I. G. Farben (note 41), and Matzerath and Turner on Nazi self-financing (note 42). Note 41 adds a counter-case the text omits: Daimler was a major backer. Note 68 likewise qualifies the oversight claim on p. 60: Schoenbaum's study of the Zabern affair found Germany not really exceptional.
- **Paxton's own research.** The Greenshirts section rests on his Peasant Fascism in France (note 53, cited at p. 56). He remarks that earlier studies of French fascism looked only at urban movements (p. 56).
- **Historians and theorists leaned on.** Mosse for the nationalization of the masses (p. 61, note 70); Linz and Stepan on the breakdown of democratic regimes (note 69, at p. 61), with Paxton's comment in that note that scholars have paid little attention to how liberal regimes failed; Sternhell as the foil on France (p. 54); Trotsky's least-barricaded gate (p. 64); Marxist crisis theory, accepted without its inevitability (p. 62).
- **Comparative reasoning by matched cases.** Po Valley against the French northern plains (pp. 56-57); Schleswig-Holstein farm organisations against the French ones (pp. 56-57); Brüning's failure to ban paramilitary leagues against Blum's ban in June 1936 (p. 54); Russia in 1917, where a large landless peasantry and an unenfranchised middle class sent protest Left (p. 64).
- **Assertions without evidence in the text.** That the PSF was probably the largest party in France on the eve of war, which Paxton qualifies at once by saying size is very hard to measure without electoral results (p. 55). That the cattle farmers would likely have switched again (p. 51). That Mussolini would have remained obscure without the Po Valley (p. 50). That the Nazi movement might have ended as a footnote had conservatives not rescued it (p. 53).

## Concessions and qualifications

- The radical rhetoric never disappeared (p. 43), and the Po Valley Fascists still advocated public works that recalled the first programme (p. 48).
- Some German businessmen, usually from small business, were attracted to Hitler (p. 51), and some firms gave some money (p. 52).
- Comparison shows that neither map fits exactly: Italy was a victor, Spain a neutral whose humiliation led to Franco and not to the Falange, Belgium was divided by ethnicity and not class (pp. 63-64).
- France and even England also had large pre-industrial sectors, which weakens the social-structure reading (p. 63).
- The importance of the republican tradition in France cannot be overestimated, though the failure was not due to an allergy (p. 56).
- Democracy and human rights were less solidly implanted in some national traditions than others, even though national-character explanations are a false trail (pp. 64-65).
- Intellectual preparation does matter at three specified points (pp. 60-61).
- Bringing new parties into the system is usually wise, but not when it rewards violence (p. 68).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch02.** The chapter opens by rejecting the study of early movements and their intellectuals as a guide to the whole (p. 42, the closing page of ch02) and re-uses ch02's account of the first Fascists as Left-leaning veterans, syndicalists and Futurists (p. 48). The mobilizing passions are listed in ch02 and are not restated here.
- **Feeds ch04.** The chapter stops both stories short of power: Italy with the 1921 coalition (p. 50), Germany in July and November 1932 (p. 53). Its last sentence names the theme of ch04, decision-makers who share power (p. 68).
- **Feeds ch05.** Parallel structures and the claim that fascist regimes kept both party and state in permanent tension are announced here and deferred to ch05 (p. 68).
- **Feeds ch08.** The crisis readings on pp. 62-63 are the same interpretations judged in ch08 (uneven development, mass society), and note 73 points forward to the noncontemporaneity theories discussed there. The final definition in ch08 (p. 174) writes in the collaboration with traditional elites that this chapter establishes as a condition of rooting.
- **Tension, my observation: structure against choice.** The chapter lists preconditions at length and then denies that they determine anything (pp. 63, 68). The denial is supported by the Spanish and Russian counter-cases (p. 64) but the chapter gives no way of telling, before the event, when choice will override setting. The explanation is therefore strong after the fact and weak as prediction, which Paxton appears to accept.
- **Tension, my observation: what counts as fascist in the failures.** The French argument needs La Rocque's PSF to be either non-fascist or a failure. Paxton reports that it was probably the largest French party in 1939 and expected to do well in 1940 (p. 55). If the PSF is counted as fascist, France is a case of rooting interrupted by war, not of failure. Paxton flags the dependence (pp. 54-55) but the list of reasons for French failure (p. 56) assumes the answer.
- **Tension, my observation: "space" risks circularity.** Space is shown to exist where fascism succeeded and to be absent where it failed (pp. 57, 59). The independent indicators Paxton offers are a discredited state, discredited parties, and elites feeling abandoned (pp. 47, 51, 56). These are observable in principle, but in this chapter they are mostly reported through the outcome.
- **Tension, my observation: the mystery left open.** Paxton says the retention of revolutionary aura alongside establishment alliances is one of the mysteries of fascist success (p. 45). The chapter describes it (selective violence, antipolitics, youth) but does not resolve it.
- **Small inconsistencies in the text, my observation.** The thirty-five deputies of May 1921 are called PNF candidates (p. 50), though the chapter itself dates the change from movement to party to late 1921 (p. 44). The Hungarian leader is spelled Szálasi (p. 44) and Szálasy (p. 57).
- **Link to other books.** The treatment of German business (pp. 51-52) is the place to compare with accounts that stress industrial backing; the claim about the liberal state as a technique of notable rule (pp. 61-62) is comparable with accounts of mass politics elsewhere.

## Open questions

- Is the PSF fascist on Paxton's own final definition (ch08, p. 174)? It had a mass base and a strong leader, but abandoned paramilitarism and sought election. The answer changes the reading of France.
- How much of the Po Valley outcome was due to Giolitti's refusal to use national force (p. 47), and is that refusal the same thing as a "failed" liberal state or a policy choice by a functioning one?
- The claim that Germany stood out by weak oversight of army and bureaucracy and not by stronger anti-Semitism (p. 60) is carried by one sentence and note 68. Does ch06 on radicalisation rely on it?
- Four readings of the liberal crisis are offered and Italy and Germany fit all four (p. 63). Do any of the failed cases also fit all four? Paxton concedes France and England had pre-industrial sectors (p. 63) but does not run the other three tests on them.
- The funding argument covers the period before 1933 (p. 52). Ch05 and ch08 (p. 165) need to be checked for how he treats business after power.
- What is the evidence that violence won votes (p. 52) as opposed to coinciding with a rising vote? Note 85 cites Johnson and Gellately on Germans not fearing the Gestapo, which concerns the regime period.
