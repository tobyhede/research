## Preface (pp. 1–2)

- **Motive.** Paxton taught a university course on fascism for years and grew more perplexed the more he read. Monographs on particular aspects of Mussolini's Italy and Hitler's Germany were excellent; books on fascism as a generic phenomenon seemed by comparison "abstract, stereotyped, and bloodless" (p. 1).
- **Aim.** To draw the monographic literature into a discussion of fascism in general, in a way that allows for variation and complexity (p. 1). "It seeks to find out how fascism worked." (p. 1)
- **Method announced.** The book "focuses more closely on the actions of fascists than on their words" (p. 1), which Paxton says is contrary to usual practice, and gives more space than usual to fascism's allies and accomplices and to the interaction of regimes with the societies they tried to transform (p. 1).
- **Scope disclaimer.** "This is an essay, not an encyclopedia." (p. 1) Coverage is admittedly cursory in places; the endnotes and the critical bibliographical essay are meant to carry the reader further (pp. 1-2).
- **Provenance.** Chapters drafted at the Villa Serbelloni (Rockefeller Foundation); ideas tried out at the EHESS in Paris, the European University Institute in Florence, and on Columbia students. Readers of the draft: Philippe Burrin, Paul Corner, Patrizia Dogliani, Henry Ashby Turner Jr.; portions read by Carol Gluck, Herbert Klein, Ken Ruoff. Juan Linz and Stanley Hoffmann are among those thanked (p. 2). Dated New York, February 2003 (p. 2).

## Question this chapter answers

How should fascism be studied, given that no definition has won general assent and that the national cases differ so much? Paxton's answer: not by starting from a definition or an ideology, but by watching a core set of accepted cases (Italy and Germany above all) in action, through a cycle of five stages, comparing them to explain different outcomes, and leaving the definition to the end of the book (pp. 11-12, 16-18).

## Leading propositions

- Fascism was the major political innovation of the twentieth century. The other main currents matured between the late eighteenth and mid-nineteenth centuries; fascism was still unimagined in the 1890s (p. 2).
- Its novelty was the combination of dictatorship against the Left with popular enthusiasm, which even Engels, who expected a conservative breach of legality, could not foresee in 1895 (p. 2).
- Fascism got its name and first steps in Italy, but similar movements arose independently across postwar Europe with the same mixture of nationalism, anti-capitalism, voluntarism and active violence against both bourgeois and socialist enemies (p. 5).
- No interpretation or definition of fascism has gained universal assent in more than eighty years, and some propose scrapping the term (p. 6). The book's purpose is to rescue the concept for meaningful use (pp. 6-7).
- The familiar images of fascism mislead. The all-powerful dictator personalises it and hides the people and institutions that helped; the chanting crowd suggests a national predisposition and lets onlooker nations off (p. 7).
- Exacerbated anti-Semitism is not the essence of fascism: Fascist Italy showed little of it for sixteen years, while Vichy, better classed as authoritarian, was aggressively anti-Semitic (p. 7).
- Anticapitalism is not its essence either. In power, fascists carried out none of their anticapitalist threats and all of their antisocialist ones (pp. 7-8).
- What fascists did tells at least as much as what they said (p. 8). Their anticapitalism was selective: aimed at international finance and bourgeois flabbiness, not at property or exploitation (p. 8).
- Fascism altered no social hierarchy, yet it did make changes deep enough to be called revolutionary in a different sense, and these brought it into conflict with conservatives. It is therefore not simply a more muscular conservatism (pp. 8-9).
- Fascism is hard to place on the Right-Left map and always kept that ambiguity, but it was never in the centre, and it readily allied with conservatives against the Left (p. 9).
- Fascism's relation to modernity cannot be settled by a yes or no. It changed by stage: early movements exploited modernization's losers; regimes pursued industry and arms. What they sought was an alternative modernity (pp. 9-10).
- The Holocaust can plausibly be seen as that alternate modernity run amok and not as a relapse into barbarism (p. 10).
- Fascist movements could not grow, take power or commit their excesses without the help or acquiescence of ordinary people and of traditional elites (p. 10).
- Definitions freeze into a static picture something better understood as a process; the book will arrive at a concept at the end and begins with a strategy (pp. 11-12).
- Fascism is not an ism like the others. It rests on feelings, not on a philosophical system; its truth is whatever makes the chosen people prevail (pp. 12-13).
- In fascism power came first and doctrine afterwards; programs were fluid and were changed without any attempt at justification (pp. 13-14).
- Ideas still matter at the two ends of the cycle: intellectuals opened space and made fascism imaginable at the start, and ideology returned as the guide of wartime radicalization at the end. They explain the middle stages poorly (pp. 14-15).
- Fascisms are more disparate than the other isms because they reject every universal value; each expresses its own national particularism, and no fascist international has worked (pp. 15-16).
- Diversity is an asset for method: comparison is used to explain differing outcomes, not to sort cases into a taxonomy (p. 16).
- The generic term is indispensable and should be rescued, not dropped; neither encyclopedic description nor an ideal-type essence is adequate (pp. 16-17).
- Fascisms that reached power did so with the help of frightened ex-liberals, opportunist technocrats and ex-conservatives, and governed in awkward tandem with them (p. 18).
- Fascism should be studied as a cycle of five stages. Each is a prerequisite for the next, but none is inevitable and movement can go backwards (p. 18).

## Arguments

**A. Fascism as unforeseen novelty (pp. 2-3)**

- **Premises**: (p. 2) Conservatism, liberalism and socialism were mature by the mid-nineteenth century. (p. 2) Engels in 1895 assumed wider suffrage would keep delivering votes to the Left and that conservatives could only break legality. (pp. 2-3) Only Tocqueville and, in 1908, Sorel had glimmers of a new kind of oppression or of a revolution with conservative ideals. (p. 3) Even the fasces symbol belonged mostly to the Left before 1914.
- **Unstated premise**: that the failure of contemporaries to imagine fascism is good evidence that it was new in kind, and not merely unnamed. Ch02's material on precursors complicates this.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 2) Fascism was an innovation, defined at first pass by an unexpected pairing: anti-Left dictatorship with mass approval.

**B. Words versus deeds (pp. 7-9)**

- **Premises**: (p. 7) Early fascists attacked finance capitalism and bourgeois values and promised expropriations. (pp. 7-8) In power they banned strikes, dissolved unions, cut wage earners' purchasing power and funded armaments, and never carried out the anticapitalist threats. (p. 8) Scholars who take the words literally call fascism radical anticapitalism; others call it capitalism's rescuer. (p. 8) The rhetoric was selective even at its most radical: it spared national producers and attacked the bourgeoisie for weakness, not exploitation. (p. 8) Regimes confiscated only from opponents, foreigners and Jews, and changed no social hierarchy.
- **Unstated premises**: that the acts of regimes in power reveal what the movement was more reliably than its early programs do; that Italy and Germany suffice to ground the claim about all fascist parties that acquired power.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 8) Deeds count at least as much as words. (pp. 8-9) Fascism was neither radical anticapitalism nor plain conservatism: it kept property and hierarchy but transformed the relation of citizen, community and state.

**C. Against a single essence: modernity as the test case (pp. 9-10)**

- **Premises**: (p. 9) Fascists cursed cities and secularism and praised an agrarian utopia. (p. 9) They also loved cars and planes, used the newest propaganda techniques, and forced industrial growth to rearm. (p. 9) So neither antimodernist reaction nor modernising dictatorship captures the whole. (p. 9) The relation differed by stage: early movements drew on modernization's losers; regimes chose industrial concentration, and rural nostalgia survived only as tokens. (p. 10) The regimes wanted technical advance with its social strains suppressed.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 10) The ambiguity is resolved only by following the whole itinerary. The general lesson is that binary questions about fascism's essence are badly posed; the answer depends on the stage.

**D. Against starting from a definition (pp. 10-12)**

- **Premises**: (p. 10) Conventional images fix on moments of high drama and omit everyday complicity. (pp. 10-11) Explaining something like the absence of any institutional brake after Kristallnacht requires looking at wide circles of acquiescence. (p. 11) Definitions give a static picture and tend to take programmatic statements as constitutive. (p. 11) The search for a perfect phrase closes off questions about origins and development.
- **Unstated premise**: that one can pick a core set of cases "generally accepted as fascist" (p. 12) without already holding a working concept of fascism. Paxton relies on scholarly consensus to supply the sample.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 11-12) Set definition aside, examine accepted cases in action as processes over time, and reach a concept at the end.

**E. Fascism is not an ism like the others (pp. 12-14)**

- **Premises**: (p. 12) Most people, and the fascist leaders themselves, describe fascism as an ideology; books usually start with thinkers and programs. (p. 12) That assumes fascism resembles conservatism, liberalism and socialism, which were made in an age of learned parliamentary debate and rest on systematic philosophies. (p. 12) Fascism was made for mass politics and appeals to emotion through ritual and rhetoric. (pp. 12-13) No system builder or major critical mind underpins it. (p. 13) Its rightness does not depend on the truth of its propositions; early fascists said so. (pp. 13-14) Leaders boasted of having no program; Mussolini set out a doctrine only in 1932; Hitler declared his 25 Points immutable and ignored many of them.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 14) Zeal mattered more than reasoned assent, programs were fluid, and no justificatory literature was needed when they changed. A study that starts from doctrine misdescribes the phenomenon.

**F. What ideas can and cannot explain (pp. 14-15)**

- **Premises**: (p. 14) Early intellectuals weakened elite attachment to Enlightenment values, made fascism conceivable, and helped shift emotions so that the Left was no longer the only home for the angry. (pp. 14-15) Ideology returned in wartime, when the hard core gained independence from conservative allies in occupied territory. (p. 15) To gain and hold power, leaders built alliances and dropped parts of the program.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 15) Ideology helps with beginnings and endings and much less with the middle of the cycle.

**G. Why comparison, and why a generic term (pp. 15-17)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 15-16) Fascisms reject universal values, so each expresses national particularism; they are not for export. (p. 16) Movements calling themselves fascist existed in every Western country after 1918 with very different outcomes. (p. 16) Communism and liberalism are kept as generic terms despite wide internal variety. (p. 17) The encyclopedic survey and the ideal type both give a static view and isolate fascism from its setting.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 16) Keep the generic term; use comparison to find the reasons for differing outcomes. (p. 17) Watch fascism in action within its web of interaction with society.

**H. The five-stage cycle (pp. 17-18)**

- **Premises**: (p. 18) Fascisms came to power and governed in coalition with former liberals and conservatives. (p. 18) Following those coalitions through time and across countries needs more than the usual movement/regime dichotomy. (p. 18) Most societies produced movements; few had regimes; only one approached full radicalization.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 18) Divide the subject into five stages. This allows comparison at equivalent degrees of development and shows fascism as a succession of processes and choices; tools that illuminate one stage may not work for another.

**Position versus argument.** What Paxton holds: fascism is a real generic phenomenon best understood by what it did, stage by stage. Why he says he holds it: every single-essence account (anti-Semitism, anticapitalism, antimodernism, ideology) breaks on a counter-case or on a gap between rhetoric and practice (pp. 7-10, 12-14).

## Evidence offered

- **Contemporary texts as markers of novelty**: Engels's 1895 preface (p. 2); Tocqueville on a new oppression for which the old words do not fit (p. 3); Sorel 1908 (p. 3).
- **Narrative fact, Italian founding**: the 23 March 1919 meeting at the Piazza San Sepolcro, somewhat more than a hundred present (p. 3); the program issued two months later, with women's suffrage, the eight-hour day, a capital levy and confiscation of 85 percent of war profits alongside expansionist claims (p. 4); the three founding groups, veterans (including Arditi), pro-war syndicalists and Futurists (pp. 4-5); the 15 April 1919 sacking of the Avanti offices, four killed and thirty-nine injured (p. 5).
- **Chronology**: Mussolini in power a little over three years after the founding; the Nazis eleven years after that; general war six years later (p. 5).
- **Early interpretations surveyed**: Thomas Mann's diary, 27 March 1933 (p. 6); Croce's onagrocracy and his parenthesis thesis (p. 6); Meinecke on moral degeneration (p. 6); the early Marxist definition and the Stalin-era Comintern formula (p. 6). Paxton reports these as positions, not as findings.
- **Counter-cases against anti-Semitism as essence**: Jewish financial backers, Aldo Finzi, Margherita Sarfatti, about two hundred Jews in the March on Rome (p. 7); Vichy as anti-Semitic but authoritarian (p. 7). Endnote 35 states the interpretive stance directly: some see anti-Semitism as the heart of the matter, and Paxton sees it as instrumental (p. 206).
- **Regime practice against anticapitalist rhetoric**: bans on strikes, dissolution of independent unions, lower purchasing power, arms spending (pp. 7-8); confiscation limited to opponents, foreigners and Jews (p. 8). Stated as general fact without figures in the text.
- **Modernity**: leaders' cars and planes, propaganda technique, rearmament (p. 9); Speer's career from Tessenow's pupil to armaments minister (p. 10); the claim that pogroms would have needed two hundred years for what the Holocaust did in three, reported as a suggestion by others (p. 10); Tim Mason's study of the 1934 labour law is named in endnote 61 as the model of step-by-step analysis (p. 210).
- **Kristallnacht, 9 November 1938**: hundreds of synagogues burned, more than seven thousand shops smashed, about twenty thousand deported to camps, ninety-one killed, a billion-mark fine (p. 11). Used to pose the question of why distaste produced no lawsuits or enquiries (p. 11).
- **Statements by fascists on doctrine**: an early Fascist text on the truth of an ideology lying in its capacity to set action in motion (p. 13); Mussolini's 1920 Postulates and his reply to Il Mondo (p. 13); a militant's line about the fist (p. 13); the 1932 encyclopedia article part-written by Gentile (pp. 13-14); Hitler's 25 Points and his first speech as chancellor (p. 14). This is the densest evidence in the chapter, and it is, ironically for the method, evidence from words.
- **Authorities invoked**: Walter Benjamin on politics turned into aesthetics, with war as the ultimate aesthetic experience (p. 13); Hannah Arendt on Mussolini as the first leader to replace a program with leadership and action (p. 14); Roger Chartier on cultural origins as conditions of conceivability and not causes (p. 14); Marc Bloch on comparison (p. 16); Roger Griffin's definition as the leading ideal type (p. 17); Lampedusa's Tancredi for the conservative calculation (p. 18).
- **Contrast case**: Stalin always wrote to prove conformity with Marx and Lenin; Hitler and Mussolini never bothered (p. 14).
- **Analogy and image**: waxworks and stuffed birds for definitions (p. 11); the elephant (p. 12); the medieval bestiary for encyclopedic surveys (p. 17); communism in Russia, Italy and Cambodia and liberalism in Britain, France and Germany as generic terms that survive variety (pp. 16-17).
- **Historiographical debts and opponents (endnotes)**: Gilbert Allardyce as the most thorough sceptic about the generic term (p. 205); Sternhell and Bracher as denying that Fascism and Nazism can share one theory, because of biological racism, with Paxton's reply that every fascism mobilises against an enemy that national culture specifies (p. 212); the Broszat-Friedländer exchange on the risk that historicising Nazism banalises it (p. 210); a rebuke of Furet for lumping fascist and communist antibourgeois feeling together (p. 207).
- **Confident generalisations**: "None altered the social hierarchy" (p. 8); it has proved impossible to make any fascist international work (p. 16); fascism "always" retained its Left-Right ambiguity (p. 9). These rest in this chapter on the two main cases and on later chapters.

## Concessions and qualifications

- What fascists said cannot be ignored, because it helps explain their appeal (p. 8).
- Fascism in power did make changes that can be called revolutionary if the word is given a different meaning (p. 8).
- A concept of fascism is needed in the end; definition is postponed, not abandoned (p. 11).
- The ideological roots of the early movements are important; the task is to establish what intellectual history can contribute and what it cannot (p. 14).
- Italy and Germany predominate in the sample (p. 12).
- Liberals, and not only conservatives, were sometimes ready to cooperate with fascists (p. 15).
- Some conservatives kept an antifascist stance to the end (p. 18).
- The stages are not a necessary sequence: most fascisms stopped short, some slipped back, and features of several stages could operate at once (p. 18).
- The Preface concedes cursory coverage of many subjects (p. 1).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Sets up the whole book**: the five stages (p. 18) map onto ch02 (creation), ch03 (rooting), ch04 (seizure), ch05 (exercise) and ch06 (radicalization or entropy). The deferred definition and the review of interpretations are promised for ch08 (pp. 6, 11). The fascist revolution is deferred to ch05 (p. 8, with endnote 43, p. 207), the liberal crisis to ch03 (p. 15), the authoritarian/fascist boundary to ch08 (p. 7).
- **Link to ch02**: the claim that ideas explain the opening of space and not the thing itself (p. 14) is argued at length in ch02's section on intellectual roots; the claim of novelty (p. 2) is qualified there by precursors from the 1880s onward.
- **Link to ch06**: the return of ideology in wartime radicalization (pp. 14-15) and the reading of the Holocaust as alternate modernity (p. 10) anticipate that chapter.
- **Tension (my observation)**: the method needs a sample before it has a definition. Paxton takes cases "generally accepted as fascist" (p. 12), which imports the judgments of the definitional literature he sets aside. A working characterisation is in fact present from the start: anti-Left dictatorship with popular enthusiasm (p. 2), and a popular movement against the Left and liberal individualism (p. 16).
- **Tension (my observation)**: the case for deeds over words is supported here largely by quoting fascists' words about having no doctrine (pp. 13-14). That is evidence about their self-presentation, which the chapter elsewhere says should not be taken at face value (p. 7).
- **Tension (my observation)**: Paxton says he is not interested in deciding whether a regime falls within a fascist essence (p. 16), yet he classifies Vichy as authoritarian and not fascist (p. 7) and later devotes a section of ch08 to boundaries. The taxonomy is postponed more than renounced.
- **Tension (my observation)**: the stages are both a sequence in which each is a prerequisite for the next and a set with no required direction (p. 18). With only two cases reaching stage four and one approaching the extreme of stage five, the later stages are close to descriptions of Italy and Germany, which limits how much comparison they can support.
- **Tension (my observation)**: fascism is said to rest on feelings about master races and their predominance over inferior peoples (p. 12), while exacerbated anti-Semitism is denied as its essence and Italian Fascism is shown as initially not anti-Semitic (p. 7). The two fit only if the racial or national enemy is variable by culture, which Paxton states in endnote 85 (p. 212) and argues in ch02.
- **Cross-book note (my observation)**: the account of liberals as fascists' accomplices in fascist eyes, and of liberal institutions failing after 1914 (p. 15), is a useful contrast with accounts that derive fascism from socialism or planning. Paxton's fascists keep property and hierarchy (p. 8).

## Open questions

- Does the book's eventual definition in ch08 follow from the stage-by-stage evidence, or does it restate the working characterisation already present on pp. 2 and 16?
- How is the core sample defended against the nominalist objection (Allardyce, endnote on p. 205) other than by analogy with communism and liberalism (pp. 16-17)?
- "None altered the social hierarchy" (p. 8): does ch05 support this for Nazi Germany, given expropriation of Jews and the rise of party and SS elites? The exception clause about a few adventurers may carry a lot of weight.
- Is the claim that fascisms are more disparate than the other isms (p. 15) an empirical finding or a deduction from their rejection of universal values (pp. 15-16)? It is presented as the latter here.
- What decides whether a regime radicalises or decays (p. 18)? The chapter names the alternatives without a mechanism; see ch06.
- The complicity argument (pp. 10-11) asks why no institution braked Hitler in November 1938 but does not answer; check ch05 and ch06.
- How far does the stage model apply outside interwar Europe? See ch07.
