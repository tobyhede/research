# ch02: Creating Fascist Movements (pp. 19–42)

## Question this chapter answers

Where did the first fascist movements come from: what immediate circumstances, ideas, feelings, long-term structural changes and forerunners made them possible, and who joined them? And how much does knowing the origins tell us about fascism as a whole? Paxton's answer: World War I opened a political space that prewar cultural and structural changes had prepared; ideas and precursors explain that space and the weakness of fascism's rivals more than fascism itself; and stage one, however heavily studied, gives only a partial picture (pp. 22, 31, 41-42).

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |

| Family resemblance | p. 19 | The looser relation Paxton uses to group early movements (Hungary, Austria, Germany, Italy) that arose independently and did not yet call themselves fascist. |
| Political space | p. 22 | Room in the political system for mass-based nationalist activism mobilised against both socialism and liberalism; dimly visible in 1914, a yawning gap after the war. Preconditions make fascism possible; they do not cause it. |
| Fourth principle of world order | p. 25 | Fascism's formula alongside liberalism, conservatism and communism: the strong prevail, strength is measured by popular fervour and unity as well as arms, class conflict is overcome by integrating workers into the nation and expelling the alien, and war is expected and welcomed. |
| Myth (Sorel) | p. 26 | A galvanising ideal capable of rousing people to perform beyond their everyday capacities; first the general strike, later the nation. |
| Residues (Pareto) | p. 26 | The irrational popular feelings that, with the permanent power of elites, subvert the formal rules of electoral democracy. |
| Social Darwinism | p. 27 | The popular reworking of race, nation, will and action into harder, more aggressive forms; struggle among races for supremacy. |

| Volksgemeinschaft | p. 28 | People's community; the Nazi goal, with the word borrowed from Tönnies's Gemeinschaft. |
| Decadence | p. 28 | The dread that great historic nations were doomed by comfort and complacency to falling birth rates and diminished vitality. |
| völkisch | p. 29 | The German Right's traditional devotion to defending a biological people against foreign impurity, socialist division and bourgeois softness. Contrasted with Italian nationalism, which was more political and cultural. |

| Proletarian nation | p. 29 | The Italian nationalist claim that Italy as a nation was entitled to a share of the world's colonies. |
| Function (versus themes) | p. 31 | What defines fascism is not the particular themes of any national case but what the themes are used for: mobilising a mass movement of regeneration, unification and purity against liberal individualism and constitutionalism and against Leftist class struggle. |
| Mobilizing passions | p. 32 | The mostly unargued feelings that shape fascist action, listed as nine items (pp. 32-33). Paxton's substitute for a fascist philosophy. |
| Mass politics | p. 33 | The involvement of the whole citizenry in politics through broad suffrage. A necessary precondition: a mass movement against the Left cannot exist without it. |
| Latecomer | p. 33 | Fascism's position among political movements; it could find space only late in the development of mass politics, after socialism had shared in government and been compromised (p. 34). |
| Politics in a new key | p. 35 | The first popular movements, from the 1880s, that asserted the priority of the nation against all internationalism or cosmopolitanism. |

| Professional politicians | p. 36 | The new elected representatives of modest origin who lived on their salaries and had to invent new support networks and appeals, including anti-Semitism and nationalism. |

## Leading propositions

- Fascism began as a name in Milan in March 1919, but similar movements arose independently in Hungary, Austria and Germany; something broader than Mussolini was afoot (p. 19).
- These movements outlasted the postwar turmoil, so they expressed something more durable than a nationalist spasm at the end of the war (p. 22).
- World War I did not so much create fascism as open cultural, social and political opportunities for it; the war experience was the most decisive immediate precondition (p. 22).
- The war discredited liberal optimism, produced restless veterans, and generated strains beyond the capacity of existing institutions (p. 22). Countries with a large but threatened middle class, including family farmers, polarised against the Left (p. 23).
- After the war liberalism, conservatism and communism each failed to impose its principle of world order, and that mutual failure left space for a fourth, fascist one (pp. 24-25).
- Fascism was conceivable before 1914 but not realisable until the war had changed Europe; its epoch opened in 1918 (p. 25).
- The deeper cultural precondition was the late-nineteenth-century revolt against liberal faith in liberty, reason, harmony and progress (p. 25).
- Nietzsche, Sorel, Le Bon, Pareto, and at a remove Bergson and Freud, supplied material that undermined confidence in rational democratic politics; none of them amounts to a creator of fascism (pp. 25-27).
- Looking for fears is more fruitful than looking for founding thinkers: fear of the collapse of community, of decadence, and of internal and external enemies (pp. 27-28).
- Fascists need a demonised enemy, but each national culture specifies which one; the enemy need not be Jewish (p. 29).
- Biological purification was a northern European and American agenda; Italian Fascism was little touched by it (p. 29).
- A linear intellectual pedigree for fascism is pure invention. Thinkers quoted by fascists were also used by antifascists, and several refused or opposed the regimes (pp. 30-31).
- Intellectual critics account for the space available to fascism and the weakness of liberalism and reformist socialism better than they account for fascism; concrete choices and actions were needed to fill the space (p. 31).
- Fascists select myths and symbols from the national repertory; it is the function of the themes, not their content, that defines the phenomenon (p. 31).
- Fascism appealed to intellectuals most widely in its early stage, and regimes later played down or discarded currents that had opened the way (pp. 31-32).
- Fascism is better linked to a set of mobilizing passions than to a philosophy; it was an affair of the gut more than of the brain (pp. 32-33).
- Mass politics was a necessary precondition. Conservatives such as Louis Napoleon and Bismarck who chose to manipulate a mass electorate pioneered ground that fascists later mastered (pp. 33-34).
- Fascism is inconceivable without a mature socialist Left that has shared in government and disillusioned part of its following (p. 34).
- The fright caused by the Bolshevik Revolution, and the failure of parliament, market and school to cope with postwar emergencies, drove the search for a new response in 1918-22 (pp. 34-35).
- The 1880s, with depression, wider suffrage and the first globalization crisis, were the threshold at which precursor movements appeared (pp. 35-36).
- Precursors (Boulangism, the anti-Dreyfusards, Schönerer and Lueger, German anti-Semitic parties, the Cercle Proudhon, Morès, Italian national syndicalists) show that the ingredients existed before conditions were ripe to combine them (pp. 36-38).
- The earliest movement functionally related to fascism may be the first Ku Klux Klan (pp. 38-39).
- Early fascists were above all young; the parties offered an intense fraternity and did bring together all classes, though not in equal proportions (p. 39).
- Fascism was not simply lower-middle-class: it drew upper-class support and more workers than once thought, with workers held back mainly by prior attachment to the socialist subculture (pp. 39-40).
- Fascists were not mentally disturbed; treating them so offers an alibi and hides the normality of fascism (p. 40).
- Early cadres were united by values, not by a social profile (p. 41).
- Studying origins gives only a partial understanding, and concentration on them misleads in specified ways (pp. 41-42).
- The map of fascist intellectual creativity does not coincide with the map of fascist success (p. 42).

## Arguments

**A. Independent emergence shows a general phenomenon (pp. 19-22)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 19-20) In Hungary, after territorial dismemberment and Béla Kun's Soviet republic, Gömbös's officers sought a mass-based movement of nationalist renovation distinct from both liberal parliamentarism and old-style dictatorship. (p. 20) In Habsburg Austria a working-class German nationalism existed before 1914, and a German National Socialist Workers' Party used the swastika from 1918. (pp. 20-22) In Germany defeat, veterans and Freikorps violence fed nationalist sects, one of which Hitler took over in 1919-20. (p. 22) These groups persisted after normality returned.
- **Unstated premise**: that the cases resemble each other closely enough to count as one phenomenon before any definition has been given. The grouping rests on family resemblance (p. 19).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 22) Fascism was not an Italian export or a passing postwar spasm but a durable, generally European response.

**B. The war opened a space; it did not create fascism (pp. 22-25)**

- **Premises**: (p. 22) A space for mass nationalist activism against socialism and liberalism was only dimly visible in 1914. (pp. 22-23) The war was longer and more brutal than anyone had thought possible and pushed governments to the limits of regimentation and opinion management. (p. 23) No belligerent achieved its aims; weakly integrated states failed outright. (pp. 23-24) Inflation mocked bourgeois virtues, and veterans claimed a right to rule. (pp. 24-25) The liberal, conservative and communist projects for postwar order each fell short.
- **Unstated premise**: that the failure of three rival principles leaves room for exactly this fourth one. Why the vacancy was filled by fascism and not by something else is left to the later sections and to ch03.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 25) Fascism offered a fourth principle and became realisable in practice only after 1918.

**C. Ideas explain the space, not the occupant (pp. 25-31)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 25-27) Prewar thinkers and popularisers eroded liberal convictions about reason, progress and democracy. (p. 30) A repertory of themes usable by fascists was available in European culture by 1914, which supports Sternhell's side of the dispute with Arendt. (p. 30) Reading backwards from fascism and rounding up every anticipating text produces a false teleology. (p. 30) Nietzsche and Sorel are distorted when only the proto-fascist parts are picked out. (pp. 30-31) Spengler, Stefan George, Niekisch, Spann, Mosca and Prezzolini refused, left or opposed fascism. (p. 31) National cases differ widely in their themes. (pp. 31-32) Many who shared the currents never became fascists, and regimes discarded early principles.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 31) The cultural critics explain the opening and the weakness of rivals. Fascism itself required choices and actions. What is common across cases is the function of the themes.

**D. Passions, not philosophy (pp. 32-33)**

- **Premises**: (p. 32) No thinker built a philosophical system for fascism. (p. 32) Even historians of fascist culture such as Mosse say a mood matters more than individual precursors. (pp. 32-33) Nine mobilizing passions can be listed, mostly taken for granted and not argued. (p. 33) Prewar nationalism and the war sharpened them.
- **Unstated premise**: that the nine items are found across the cases and are distinctive enough to pick out fascism. Paxton himself says many are as old as Cain (p. 33).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 33) A study of roots confined to thinkers and writers misses the most powerful impulses.

**E. Structural preconditions: mass politics and a compromised Left (pp. 33-35)**

- **Premises**: (p. 33) A mass movement against the Left needs a mass electorate. (pp. 33-34) After 1848 some conservatives learned to manage broad suffrage; after 1918 narrow suffrage was impossible. (p. 34) The Right had to accept mass politics, helped by the drift of the middle class toward conservatism. (p. 34) The democratic and socialist Lefts had to split, and the Left had to lose its monopoly as the recourse of the discontented. (p. 34) That happened once socialists entered government, from 1899, and disappointed purists. (pp. 34-35) Bolshevism turned existing liberal difficulties into emergencies that parliament, market and school handled poorly.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 34) Fascism can be dated late in the development of mass politics, after socialist participation in government. (p. 33) It was inconceivable earlier.

**F. Precursors: ingredients without conditions (pp. 35-39)**

- **Premises**: (p. 35) The 1880s combined depression, wider suffrage and globalization shocks. (pp. 35-36) Weakened deference brought in professional politicians who needed new appeals. (p. 36) The first depression in the era of mass politics rewarded demagoguery. (pp. 36-38) Boulanger, the anti-Dreyfusards, Schönerer, Lueger, Stöcker, the Cercle Proudhon, Morès and the Italian national syndicalists each assembled some of the later ingredients. (p. 37) The German anti-Semitic parties had shrunk to insignificance by the early twentieth century.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 37) Many elements of later fascism existed, but conditions were not ripe to combine them and win a following. (p. 39) They are visible as harbingers only in retrospect.

**G. Who joined (pp. 39-41)**

- **Premises**: (p. 39) Veterans alone would have made only a pressure group. (p. 39) Recruits were young and drawn by a fraternity unlike the parties of notables. (p. 39) All parties are largely middle class, and fascism also attracted the upper class. (pp. 39-40) Workers outside the socialist community, the unemployed in some cases, and workers in special local conditions did join. (p. 40) Hitler was capable of rational choices; no evidence shows fascists were disproportionately disturbed or deviant. (pp. 40-41) Leaders were often marginal figures of a new type, but cadres ranged from street-brawlers to a philosophy professor.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 41) Values, not class or pathology, united early fascists. (p. 41) The clientele changed over time, so recruitment at the start does not describe the later movement.

**H. The limits of origins (pp. 41-42)**

- **Premises**: (p. 41) The effort to become more than a marginal voice transformed the first movements. (p. 41) Origins attract attention because of a misleading assumption that origins reveal an inner blueprint and because early words and artifacts are plentiful. (pp. 41-42) Most fascist movements never got past stage one. (p. 42) Mussolini's first electoral test on the San Sepolcro program failed badly.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 42) Focus on origins over-rates intellectuals, over-rates antibourgeois rhetoric, and gives comparison little to work with. As much effort must go to the later forms.

**Position versus argument.** What Paxton holds: origins are necessary background and insufficient explanation. Why: the same cultural materials existed where fascism failed, and were shared by people who opposed it (pp. 30-32, 42).

## Evidence offered

- **Hungary (narrative fact)**: Trianon removed 70 percent of prewar territory and almost two thirds of the population (p. 19); the counterrevolution killed some five to six thousand, ten times the victims of the Soviet regime (p. 20); thirty-two of Kun's forty-five commissars were Jewish (p. 20); Gömbös later prime minister under Horthy, 1932-35 (p. 20).
- **Austria**: Lueger mayor of Vienna from 1897 on a mix of anti-Semitism, anticorruption, defence of artisans and municipal services (p. 20); German Workers' Party with three seats in the Diet by 1911 (p. 20).
- **Germany**: Spannaus's recollection of returning in 1921 (p. 21); Freikorps murders of Luxemburg and Liebknecht, January 1919 (p. 21); Hitler sent by Army Intelligence to the DAP in September 1919, party card No. 555, the Hofbräuhaus meeting of 24 February 1920 before nearly two thousand people (p. 21).
- **War experience**: Mussolini's claim to the political succession on the ground of having pushed Italy into war (p. 22); Balbo's statement of veteran disgust and his career from failed law student to paid secretary of the Ferrara fascio in January 1921 (pp. 23-24). Paraguay's loss of half its population in 1864-70 is the contrast case for what Europe had been spared (p. 22).
- **Comparative generalisation with a source**: the Left/anti-Left polarisation by social structure (p. 23) is attributed in endnote 17 to Luebbert's analysis of whether family farmers allied with the middle class or with socialists (p. 214). Endnote 15 adds Linz's observation that neutral countries had low levels of fascism (p. 213).
- **Intellectual history**: summaries of Nietzsche (pp. 25-26), Sorel (p. 26), Le Bon and Pareto (p. 26), Bergson and Freud (pp. 26-27), Galton and eugenics (p. 27), Carlyle (p. 27), Durkheim and Tönnies (pp. 27-28), Spengler (p. 28). Mussolini's attendance at Pareto's Lausanne lectures in 1904 is the one direct biographical link asserted (p. 26).
- **Enemies and purification**: Goebbels at the 10 May 1933 book burning (p. 28); forcible sterilisation in the United States and Sweden, and Nazi medical euthanasia as going beyond them (p. 29); the list of varying national enemies, including Slovenes for Italy and blacks and Catholics for American fascists (p. 29).
- **Historiographical positions**: Sternhell (fascism as coherent ideology and integral part of European culture) against Arendt (Nazism owed nothing to the Western tradition) (pp. 29-30); Isaiah Berlin on de Maistre, judged a stretch (p. 29); Mosse on mood (p. 32); Nolte's title for the epoch (p. 25). Paxton sides partly with Sternhell on the availability of themes and against him on their causal weight (pp. 30-31).
- **Counter-examples to the pedigree**: Spengler's refusal and his 1932 remark, George's exile, Stauffenberg, Niekisch, Spann's arrest in 1938 (p. 30); Mosca's opposition and signature on Croce's manifesto, Prezzolini's departure (pp. 30-31); Mayakovsky as a Futurist turned Bolshevik (p. 31); D. H. Lawrence's blood rhetoric followed by conscientious objection (p. 32).
- **The nine mobilizing passions**: crisis beyond traditional solutions; primacy of the group; victimhood justifying any action; dread of decline; integration of a purer community by consent or exclusionary violence; authority of natural male leaders culminating in a national chief; superiority of the leader's instincts over reason; beauty of violence and efficacy of will; right of the chosen people to dominate (pp. 32-33). The list is Paxton's own synthesis; no source is cited for it except endnote 60 on the victim trope (p. 217).
- **Mass politics**: Louis Napoleon's election by manhood suffrage in December 1848, his defence of it against the 1850 legislature, and plebiscitary practice after the 1851 coup (p. 33); Bismarck's broad suffrage (p. 33); the first socialist to enter a bourgeois cabinet in September 1899 (p. 34); the British Reform Bill of 1884 (p. 35); Gambetta's new social layers (pp. 35-36).
- **Precursor cases**: Boulanger's rise from January 1886 to flight in April 1889 (pp. 36-37); the Dreyfus Affair and Maurras's patriotic forgery (p. 37); Schönerer and Lueger's municipal socialism (p. 37); Stöcker's Christian Social Party (p. 37); the Cercle Proudhon of 1911 (pp. 37-38); Morès and his slaughterhouse squads in cowboy dress (p. 38); Italian Sorelians after the failed general strike of June 1914 (p. 38); the Klan's uniform, intimidation and parallel civic authority (pp. 38-39).
- **Recruitment data, mostly by reference**: the middle-class thesis and its revision (p. 39), with Lipset, Hamilton and Childers named in endnotes (p. 219); a third of British Union of Fascists members in East London unskilled or semiskilled workers (p. 40); Arrow Cross winning a third of the vote in industrial central Budapest (p. 40); fewer Catholics than Protestants among Nazi voters (p. 40). Endnote 89 qualifies the veteran theme: many veterans turned Left, and veterans were only a quarter of SA membership (p. 219). Endnote 101 notes that Brustein's rational-choice account of Nazi recruits has been questioned on method and data (p. 220).
- **Electoral test**: Mussolini in Milan, 16 November 1919, with 4,796 votes out of 315,165 (p. 42).
- **Hedged or bare claims**: the Klan passage is framed with "It may be" and "arguably" (pp. 38-39); "Hardly anyone puts Germany first" (p. 38); the claim that the unemployed more often went communist is qualified by exceptions (p. 40); the contrast between Protestant north and Catholic south on medical purification is asserted without figures (p. 29).

## Concessions and qualifications

- The Great War was also at the root of much else, Bolshevism included; for some authors it suffices to explain both (p. 22). Paxton does not adopt that view but records it.
- Sternhell is right that the themes were available, and has established that socialist heresies are among fascism's roots, though not alone (pp. 29-30).
- Bergson and Freud had nothing to do with fascism and suffered from it (p. 26).
- Nietzsche's texts contain raw material for anti-Semites and prophets of decline even though he scorned both patriotism and actual anti-Semites (pp. 25-26).
- It would be absurd to call Napoleon III or Bismarck fascists (p. 33).
- The mobilizing passions are hard to treat historically because many are as old as Cain (p. 33).
- Workers were always proportionally fewer among fascists than in the population (p. 39).
- Hitler was driven by hatreds and abnormal obsessions, though capable of pragmatic decisions, especially before 1942 (p. 40).
- Many fascist leaders were marginal figures (p. 41), though the cadres as a whole do not fit that label (p. 41).
- Precursors are recognisable only in retrospect (p. 39).
- Comparison has little bite at the first stage, because every country with mass politics had a fledgling movement (p. 42).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Carries out ch01's program**: ch01 said ideas help with beginnings and not the middle stages (ch01, pp. 14-15); this chapter makes the case in detail and then turns the same scepticism on the study of beginnings as such (pp. 41-42).
- **Feeds ch03**: the uneven national impact of the liberal crisis is explicitly deferred to the next chapter (p. 35), as is the reason fascism stayed marginal in France despite its intellectual creativity there (p. 42). Mussolini's 1919 electoral failure (p. 42) sets up the question of what adjustments made rooting possible.
- **Feeds ch06**: the remark that certain ideas reasserted themselves in the radicalization stage (p. 42) and the victimhood passion that licenses action without limits (p. 32) both point there.
- **Feeds ch08**: the mobilizing passions (pp. 32-33) are the affective core that the final definition is likely to draw on; psychohistory is deferred to ch08 (p. 40). The functional test for the Klan (p. 38) is the same test that boundary cases will need.
- **Link to ch07**: the Klan as an American forerunner (pp. 38-39) and the point that precocious democracies produced precocious backlashes (p. 39) anticipate the discussion of fascism outside Europe.
- **Tension with ch01 (my observation)**: ch01 presents fascism as unimagined in the 1890s (ch01, p. 2); here the ingredients are assembled by Boulanger in the 1880s and the Klan in the 1860s (pp. 37-39). Paxton reconciles the two by saying precursors were seen as aberrations and become visible only in retrospect (p. 39), which is itself a retrospective selection of the kind he criticises in intellectual history (p. 30).
- **Tension (my observation)**: the method prefers deeds to words, yet the section most distinctive to Paxton, the nine mobilizing passions, is a list of beliefs and feelings inferred from what fascists said and did, with almost no documentation (pp. 32-33). It functions as a soft definition placed in the chapter on origins, before the book has officially reached one.
- **Tension (my observation)**: function over themes (p. 31) lets Paxton hold together cases with very different content, but it also makes the category depend on a prior decision about which movements were performing the fascist function. The Hungarian, Austrian and German cases are grouped at the start by family resemblance (p. 19) before that function has been specified.
- **Tension (my observation)**: Paxton says the war by itself does not explain fascism (p. 22), but most of the causal weight in the chapter falls on it: it turns a dimly visible space into a gap (p. 22), supplies the veterans (p. 23), sharpens the passions (p. 33), and makes narrow suffrage impossible (p. 34). The structural preconditions date fascism; the war triggers it. The chapter does not say how fascism in countries with a light war experience should be explained, apart from Linz's point in endnote 15 (p. 213).
- **Tension (my observation)**: the claim that fascism needs a Left that has already shared in government (p. 34) fits France after 1899 and Germany after 1918, but Italian socialists had not held national office before 1919-22. The Italian disillusion Paxton describes is with parliamentary compromise and failed general strikes (p. 38), a weaker condition than the one stated.
- **Cross-book note (my observation)**: the political-space argument puts fascism's opening in the failure of liberal institutions and the fright of Bolshevism (pp. 34-35), and treats the socialist-heresy lineage (Sternhell) as one root among several (p. 29). That is a different causal story from one that derives fascism from socialist planning.

## Open questions

- Are the nine mobilizing passions (pp. 32-33) carried into the ch08 definition unchanged, and are they ever tested against a non-fascist movement to show that they discriminate?
- What exactly is the threshold at which a political space opens? The concept is credited to Linz (p. 213) and used as an explanation, but the chapter offers no way to tell in advance whether a space exists.
- Does ch03 explain why the structural preconditions, present in every country with mass politics (p. 42), produced rooted movements in only a few?
- How much of the recruitment section rests on German electoral studies alone? The text cites British and Hungarian cases for workers (p. 40), and the endnotes lean on Hamilton and Childers (p. 219).
- The Klan claim (pp. 38-39) is heavily hedged. Is it followed up in ch07 with any attention to how far the functional parallel goes, given the Klan faced no mature socialist Left, which Paxton makes a precondition (p. 34)?
- Is the division between a biologically minded Protestant north and a culturally minded Catholic south (p. 29) supported anywhere beyond assertion? It bears on why Italian and German radicalization differed (ch06).
- If the first movements were transformed by the effort to gain power (p. 41), in what sense is the later regime the same thing as the early movement? The continuity of the object across stages is assumed here.
