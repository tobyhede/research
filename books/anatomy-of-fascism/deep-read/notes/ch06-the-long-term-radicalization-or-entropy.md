# ch06: The Long Term: Radicalization or Entropy? (pp. 117–137)

## Question this chapter answers

What happens to a fascist regime over time? Paxton's answer: it cannot rest. Either it damps itself down and decays into ordinary authoritarianism (the "entropy" of the title; Franco, Salazar, and Mussolini for much of 1926-35), or it keeps escalating, through war above all, until it destroys its enemies, its allies and finally itself (Nazi Germany, and Italy in Ethiopia and at Salò). This is the fifth and last of his stages, and the one he calls most distinctive of fascism (p. 135). The chapter also asks what drives radicalization, and tests the answer on the hardest case, the Holocaust.

## Leading propositions

- Fascist regimes cannot settle into the enjoyment of power, because the leader has promised unification, purification and energy, and must show momentum to keep faith (pp. 117-118).
- The choice is between decay into something like authoritarianism and an escalation that ends in self-destruction (p. 118).
- Regimes that normalised themselves became more authoritarian than fascist. Franco subordinated and diluted the Falange; Salazar abolished Portugal's fascist movement and borrowed only its trappings (pp. 118-119).
- Nazi Germany alone radicalised fully, and war of conquest in the east was what released the prerogative state from the normative state (p. 119).
- Italy followed an irregular course: Mussolini as prime minister preferred stability and repeatedly strengthened state and traditional institutions against his party (pp. 120-121).
- Running the police through a career civil servant on bureaucratic lines was Italian Fascism's most important divergence from Nazi practice (p. 121).
- Neither pure intentionalism nor pure structuralism explains radicalization; fascist regimes have radicalizing impulses from below, in the party and its militants, that authoritarian dictatorships lack (pp. 121-122).
- The leader cannot dispense with the party, since it is his main weapon against the old elites (p. 122).
- Hitler's administrative chaos and his habit of proclaiming goals and letting subordinates compete produced radicalization; Mussolini's refusal to delegate produced inertia (p. 123).
- War is the clearest radicalizing impulse and plays a circular role: it formed the movements, it holds the regimes together, and it ends them (p. 123).
- Both leaders chose war deliberately; Mussolini's campaigns of 1940 served no vital economic or strategic interest (pp. 123-124).
- Authoritarian regimes use military pomp to preserve the status quo; fascist regimes need actual conquest (p. 125).
- Fascist war government differs from democratic wartime restriction: a fanatical minority is freed to act beyond any calculation of interest (p. 125).
- The Holocaust developed step by step, through an upward spiral of local party initiative and state channelling, and required thousands of participants (p. 126).
- Hitler's central responsibility is not in doubt, but the shift to total extermination is best explained by cumulative radicalization (p. 128).
- The killing centres were built outside the reach of the normative state (p. 130).
- The regime did not kill Jews to please public opinion; it hid the killing, though knowledge was fairly widespread (p. 130).
- Italian anti-Jewish law of 1938 had native stems, colonial and Catholic, and was not only a copy made to please Hitler (pp. 132-133).
- Fascist mobilization proved more fragile than democratic mobilization (p. 133).
- Radicalization is not a rational method of war-making, and at the end it denies even the nation (p. 136).
- Fascism is destabilising and so was no long-run solution for the conservatives and liberals who turned to it (p. 136).

## Arguments

**A. Why fascist regimes cannot stand still (pp. 117-118)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 117-118) The leader promised to unify, purify and energise the community and to rescue it from decadence. (p. 118) He offered violence, immersion in the community, purification and expansionist war as the means. (p. 118) Fulfilling this requires an impression of permanent momentum.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 118) Without an ever-mounting spiral of challenges the regime risks decaying toward authoritarianism; with it, it drives toward self-destruction. Later restated: abandoning dynamism would undermine the leader's main source of power independent of the old elites (p. 122).
- **Unstated premise (my reading)**: that the regime's legitimacy with its own militants and public depends on continued delivery of excitement, and cannot be converted into routine consent. The Italian decade 1926-35 partly tests this; see Tensions.

**B. Normalised regimes are not fascist (pp. 118-119)**

- **Premises**: (p. 118) Franco looked fascist: Axis aid, mass repression, autarky, the Anti-Comintern Pact, Tangiers. (p. 118) But he priced belligerency out of reach, wanted quiet, and gave the Falange no share in policy or administration. (p. 119) He merged it into an umbrella organisation, arrested Hedilla, and by 1970 abolished its name. (p. 119) Salazar dissolved the National Syndicalists in 1934, ruled through the Church, stayed neutral and even resisted industrialisation.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 119) Franquist Spain became an authoritarian regime with almost no fascist coloration; Salazar's was nonfascist and by choice nontotalitarian.
- **Counter-view he records**: endnote 4 calls Michael Richards's reading of Franco's Spain as fascist until 1945 brilliant, and endnote 5 notes Preston's portrait of a Franco committed to the Axis until at least 1942 (p. 240). The main text concedes Franco's preference for the traditional form is clear after 1942 and only probable before (p. 119).

**C. What drives radicalization (pp. 121-125)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 121-122) The leader's intentions need willing executors; pure structuralism is absurd. (p. 122) The party is a standing force for radicalization: militants want rewards, old comrades are potential rivals, and the leader needs the party against the elites. (p. 122) Mussolini gave in to, channelled or diverted his lieutenants; Hitler mastered his party and used it. (p. 123) Overlapping jurisdictions and Darwinian competition push subordinates to extremes. (p. 123) War generates both the need for extreme measures and acceptance of them.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 122-123, p. 125) Radicalization comes from the interaction of leader, party and war. It is strongest where the leader controls and exploits the party, where administration is fragmented, and where conquest opens territory beyond the normative state.

**D. Fascist war is not ordinary war government (pp. 124-125)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 124-125) Authoritarian regimes also glorify the military (Franco's parades, Vichy's Armistice Army, Salazar's empire). (p. 125) All regimes radicalise in war; democracies accepted rationing, the draft and censorship. (p. 125) But fascist regimes chose aggressive war in order to raise the tension of their own people, and in war a fanatical minority was freed from calculation of interest.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 125) The difference is one of kind: emotional commitment to war and the replacement of civil servants by party radicals in conquered territory. Here Arendt's atomization thesis, weak as an account of origins, fits as an account of the fragmentation of government.

**E. Accounting for the Holocaust (pp. 126-131)**

- **Premises**: (p. 126) Convincing accounts include both Hitler's hatred and the thousands of subordinates, and recognise stepwise development. (p. 126) Phase one, segregation: street actions of 1933 channelled into the April 1 boycott; the Nuremberg laws; Kristallnacht channelled into Aryanization; the yellow star. (pp. 126-127) Phase two, expulsion: Eichmann's Vienna system, Poland, Frank's resistance, Himmler's resettlement of ethnic Germans, Madagascar; expulsion stayed official policy until late 1941. (p. 127) Local administrators had wide leeway and some moved to killing whole Jewish populations by August-September 1941. (pp. 128-129) Dress rehearsals lowered inhibitions and trained killers. (p. 129) Blockages and emergencies (failure before Moscow, food shortage, security fears, incoming ethnic Germans) made Jews seem an unbearable burden; intermediary solutions failed. (p. 130) The killing was moved to territory outside German law.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 128, p. 130) Extermination emerged through cumulative radicalization in a fragmented system where radicals were unaccountable and the Führer rewarded initiative. Wannsee looks more like state coordination of local initiatives than a new policy from above (p. 127).
- **How he handles the Hitler-order question**: the absence of a written order troubles intentionalists "probably unnecessarily" in his view; a verbal order in fall 1941 is likely, and he lists three datings: the euphoria of the first advance, rage at the failure before Moscow (which he thinks more likely), and Gerlach's December 12, 1941 speech after American entry (p. 128).
- **On Goldhagen**: widespread murderous Jew-hatred was necessary, a point on which he says Goldhagen is right, but it does not explain why the line was crossed in some places and times and not others (p. 128).

**F. Italian radicalization was partial but real (pp. 131-135)**

- **Premises**: (p. 131) From 1930 a more aggressive tone, the service ministries retaken, Ethiopia prepared and invaded. (p. 132) The war revived the party, including squadrismo in Addis Ababa in 1937. (p. 132) Starace's reform of custom and the 1938 racial laws. (pp. 132-133) The laws had Italian roots in colonial racial practice and Catholic anti-Judaism. (p. 133) After 1938 the regime subsided again; the war of 1940 brought defeat. (p. 134) Salò, freed from Church, king and business, reverted to populist national socialism and rounded up Jews.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 131, p. 135) Italian Fascism shows the same forces in weaker form; it radicalised most fully in East Africa and in the final Italian campaign.

**G. Final thoughts: radicalization is distinctive and self-defeating (pp. 135-137)**

- **Premises**: (p. 135) Any regime can radicalise, but the fascist drive to destructive violence, to the point of self-destruction, is distinct. (p. 135) Stalin's case shares the rejection of law, but lacks the idealisation of violence as a master-race virtue and the built-in competition between party organisations and established elites. (p. 135) In authoritarian war regimes the army extends its control; in the east after 1941 the German army lost it to party organisations. (p. 136) Yet the war could be pursued only with continued complicity of state services and the socially powerful. (p. 136) Resources were diverted from fighting to murder.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 136-137) Radicalization cannot be explained as rational war mobilisation; fascism is destabilising, has no orderly succession, and the regimes we know drove themselves off a cliff.

## Evidence offered

- **Spain**: repression that may have killed as many as two hundred thousand (p. 118); Falange membership near a million in 1941-42 (p. 118); Hitler's remark to Mussolini after Hendaye (p. 118); Hedilla's arrest, April 1937 (p. 119). Sources: Preston, Payne (endnotes 5-8, p. 240).
- **Portugal**: abolition of the National Syndicalists in July 1934; Salazar's complaint about fascists; the Estado Novo's borrowed devices (p. 119). Source: Costa Pinto (endnotes 9-11, p. 240).
- **Italy, normalising**: De Stefani's finance, 1922-25 (p. 120); Federzoni at Interior, Farinacci appointed then sacked in April 1926 (p. 120); Bocchini at the police (p. 121); Rossoni's removal in 1928 (p. 121); the Lateran Pact of 1929 (p. 121). Sarti is the source for the pro-business judgement (endnotes 12 and 15, pp. 240-241). Montanelli's 1933 complaint is used as testimony of generational impatience (p. 121).
- **Germany, chaos**: Schulenburg's 1937 lament about split state power, from the Noakes and Pridham documents (p. 123, endnote 25, p. 241).
- **Leaders' own words on war**: Hitler to Goebbels (p. 123), which endnote 27 says concerned the Jewish issue and dates to March 1943 (p. 241); Mussolini to Ciano in 1937 and to parliament in 1934 (p. 124).
- **War decisions**: Corfu 1923 (p. 124); Ethiopia prepared 1933-34 (p. 124); entry into war June 10, 1940 (p. 124). On 1940 endnote 32 records a disagreement: Knox attributes it to Mussolini's bellicosity, Bosworth sees more caution and public support (p. 241).
- **A. J. P. Taylor** is cited as a useful revisionist and then set aside: Hitler probably wanted a short local war in Poland (pp. 123-124).
- **Holocaust chronology and numbers**: boycott April 1, 1933; Nuremberg laws September 15, 1935 (p. 126); Goering's complaint after Kristallnacht (p. 126); five hundred thousand ethnic Germans to resettle (p. 127); T-4 killing more than seventy thousand (p. 128), with endnote 49 raising the wider total to about two hundred thousand by 1945 (pp. 242-243); Zyklon-B first tested on six hundred Soviet prisoners, September 3, 1941 (p. 129); thirty gas vans in fall 1941 (p. 130); six camps, accounting for 60 percent of Jews murdered (p. 130).
- **Holocaust historiography leaned on**: Schleunes and Adam for stepwise development, Friedländer and Longerich for synthesis (endnote 35, pp. 241-242); Aly and the Herbert volume for resettlement, local initiative and intermediary solutions (endnotes 39-43, p. 242); Browning, Burrin and Gerlach for the three datings of Hitler's decision (endnotes 46-48, p. 242); Fleming on Hitler's knowledge (endnote 45, p. 242); Burleigh on euthanasia (endnote 49, p. 242); Kershaw and Kulka on public knowledge (endnote 58, p. 243); Bartov on the army (endnote 79, p. 244).
- **Secrecy and knowledge**: euphemisms and the trace-removal operations (p. 130), which endnote 56 identifies as Aktion 1005 (p. 243); soldiers' photographs (p. 130).
- **Italy, radicalising**: the May 9, 1936 balcony speech, quoted at length (pp. 131-132); the gold wedding rings (p. 132); the Addis Ababa reprisals of 1937, hundreds killed (p. 132); the 1938 Manifesto and laws, one in twelve professors removed, Fermi's departure (p. 132); colonial internment and racial law in Libya and Ethiopia (p. 133); Catholic liturgy and press (p. 133). Sarfatti is the source for Italian roots, against Michaelis and De Felice (endnote 66, pp. 243-244); Kertzer for the Church (endnote 69, p. 244).
- **Counter-evidence he reports**: the laws were generally unpopular, Italian authorities protected Jews in occupied Croatia and southeastern France, and few Italians joined the 1943 deportations (p. 133).
- **Salò**: the November 1943 programme (p. 134); Borghese's unit (p. 134); the arrest of Primo Levi (pp. 134-135); the Verona executions (p. 135).
- **Bare assertion or generalisation**: that fascist economies never matched pre-1914 or postwar growth belongs to ch05 (p. 117); in this chapter, the "general rule" about war and fascist muscle tone (p. 123) and the claim that orderly succession is harder for fascism than for communism (p. 136) rest on two cases and on reasoning. Paxton marks the succession problem as hypothetical (p. 136).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch05**: the normative/prerogative vocabulary, the leader-party-elites triangle and "working toward the Führer" are all taken as already established (p. 119, p. 122, p. 123). The chapter cannot be read alone.
- **Depends on ch04**: the Matteotti crisis and January 1925 are re-read here as one cycle of normalization and radicalization (p. 120); ch04 told the same events as the seizure of power from within (ch04, pp. 87-88).
- **Feeds ch08**: the fascist/authoritarian boundary used throughout (pp. 118-119, p. 125) is formally defined only in ch08 (endnote 3, p. 240). The rehabilitation of Arendt for this stage only (p. 125, p. 136) anticipates the stage-specific use of rival interpretations.
- **Feeds ch07**: the closing claim that the fascisms we know seem doomed to self-destruction (p. 137) frames the question of whether fascism can recur.
- **Tension, my observation**: the opening says fascist regimes could not survive without the rush forward (p. 118), yet Mussolini's regime spent roughly 1926-35 normalising (pp. 120-121) and again subsided after 1938 (p. 133), and Paxton still counts it as fascist. Either the claim means only that they could not survive as fascist regimes, which makes it close to definitional, or Italy is a counter-example softened by the Ethiopian turn. The Montanelli quotation and the "crisis of identity" (p. 121, p. 124) are the evidence that the pressure was real, but it is thin.
- **Tension, my observation**: radicalization is called the stage where fascism is most distinctive (p. 135), and also a stage only one regime really reached (p. 135). A defining feature observed fully in one case is hard to separate from the particular features of Nazism, above all its racial antisemitism. Paxton's answer is that the same forces are visible in Italy in weaker form (p. 131), which the Ethiopian and Salò material supports only in part.
- **Tension, my observation**: war is both cause and expression of radicalization (p. 123). Paxton calls the role circular, which is candid, but it makes the causal claim hard to test: fascist regimes choose war because they are radical, and are radical because they are at war.
- **Tension, my observation**: the Holocaust section stresses local initiative and structural pressure (pp. 127-129) while insisting on Hitler's central responsibility and a probable verbal order (p. 128). This is the mainstream synthesis of its date, not a contradiction, but the chapter does not say how much weight each side carries.
- **Franco**: here firmly authoritarian (p. 119), while the endnotes admit serious scholars who place him closer to fascism before 1945 (p. 240). Compare ch04 on Franco leaving little room for the Falange (ch04, p. 92).
- **Accuracy flags, my observations, to check before reuse**: (1) Wal-wal is placed near the frontier between Ethiopia and "Italian Somaliland (now Eritrea)" (p. 131); Italian Somaliland became part of Somalia, not Eritrea. (2) Mussolini's attacks on "Albania and Greece in the fall of 1940" (p. 124); Italy occupied Albania in April 1939 and attacked Greece from Albania in October 1940. (3) The Commissar Order is described as given to the Einsatzgruppen (p. 129); as far as I know it was an army order, and I have not checked this. None affects the argument.

## Open questions

- Is "entropy" a real alternative path for a fascist regime, or just Paxton's label for regimes that were never fascist? Franco and Salazar are cases of the latter by his own account (pp. 118-119). The only fascist regime shown drifting is Italy, and it was pulled back by war. Synthesis should ask whether the book has any case of a fascist regime that decayed into authoritarianism and stayed there.
- What would falsify the claim that fascism cannot stabilise? Paxton concedes that prolonged radicalization has never been seen and that succession is hypothetical (p. 136). The sample is two regimes, both ended by military defeat.
- How far does the stage model explain the Holocaust as opposed to accommodating it? The explanatory work is done by antisemitism plus conquest plus administrative fragmentation (pp. 128-130); Italy had the last two in Ethiopia and produced colonial atrocity but not genocide of this kind. Does ch08 address the weight of ideology?
- The claim that Italy's 1938 laws had native roots (pp. 132-133) follows Sarfatti against the older view. How settled was this in 2004, and has later work changed it?
- "Fascist mobilization turned out to be more fragile than democratic mobilization" (p. 133) is supported by Italy but not by Germany, which Paxton says fought longer and harder in 1939-45 than in 1914-18 (p. 133). Is the generalisation about fascism or about Italy?
- Paxton says the comparison with Stalinist radicalization fails on two counts (p. 135). Is the lack of party-versus-elite competition in the Soviet system argued anywhere in the book, or only stated here?
- The final page says Mussolini had to enter the war in June 1940 (p. 137), while the text earlier allows other motives and endnote 32 records Bosworth's dissent (p. 124, p. 241). How strong is the necessity claim?
