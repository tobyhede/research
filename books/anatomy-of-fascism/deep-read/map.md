## Classification

- **Kind**: theoretical. It explains what fascism was and how it worked; the last pages of ch07 and ch08 draw a practical corollary about recognising it today.
- **Genre**: comparative history written as an interpretive essay ("This is an essay, not an encyclopedia", p. 1), with a critical bibliographical essay and discursive endnotes as its scholarly apparatus. Read with the history strategy: separate narrative fact from Paxton's interpretation, ask what he is trying to prove, and record which monographs and historians he leans on.

## The whole-book question

What was fascism, judged by what fascists did and by who helped them, and under what conditions did it take root, reach power, and radicalise?

## Unity statement

Paxton sets aside the search for a fixed ideological essence and follows fascism through five stages: the creation of movements, their rooting in a political system, the arrival in power, the exercise of power, and the long term of radicalisation or entropy (p. 18). At each stage the outcome depends less on fascist doctrine than on the setting, on a crisis of liberal democracy, and on the choices of conservative elites who ally with fascists; only in Italy and Germany did the full cycle run, and only Nazi Germany reached the extreme of radicalisation. He ends with a definition of fascism as a form of political behaviour rather than a body of ideas (p. 174) and argues that its early stages remain possible in any democracy (p. 175).
