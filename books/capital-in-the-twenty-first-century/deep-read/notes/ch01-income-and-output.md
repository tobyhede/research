# ch01: Income and Output (pp. 31–55)

## Question this chapter answers

What are the basic quantities in which the book's argument is stated (national income, capital, the capital/income ratio β, the capital share α, the return r) and how do they fit together? And, in broad strokes, how has the global distribution of output and income between countries changed since 1700, and what forces drive convergence between rich and poor countries? Piketty's answers: national income is net domestic product plus net foreign income; capital is all non-human wealth that can be owned and traded, valued at market prices; α = r × β holds by definition, leaving two degrees of freedom; and international convergence comes mainly from the diffusion of knowledge, not from rich countries owning poor ones.

## Key terms

Endnote 3 equates them with "functional" and "personal" distribution (p. 435). |

Valued at market prices (p. 38). |

National wealth = private wealth + public wealth (p. 39). |
| Domestic capital / net foreign capital | pp. 39-40 | National capital = domestic capital (located within the borders) + net foreign assets (assets owned abroad minus domestic assets owned by foreigners) (pp. 39-40). Globally net foreign positions sum to zero (p. 40). |

## Leading propositions

- The split of output between wages and profits has always been at the heart of distributional conflict (the Marikana massacre of 2012, Haymarket 1886, Fourmies 1891) (p. 31).
- Conflict over the capital-labour split stems "first and foremost" from the extreme concentration of capital ownership; inequality of capital income always exceeds inequality of labour income (p. 32).
- Some return to capital may be justified: investment must be financed, savers may deserve reward, and part of "capital income" is really entrepreneurial labour (p. 32).
- The textbook claim that labour's share is stable at about two-thirds is wrong: the split moved a great deal in the twentieth century (pp. 32-33).
- The shocks of 1914–1945 cut the capital share to historic lows in the 1950s; capital recovered, accelerated after Thatcher (1979) and Reagan (1980), and by 2010 "was prospering as it had not done since 1913" (p. 33).
- Labour's share may rise slightly over the very long run, but capital's share (excluding human capital) today is "only slightly smaller" than in the early nineteenth century (pp. 33-34).
- Capital's importance in rich countries today is mainly due to slower demographic and productivity growth, plus political regimes that favour private capital (p. 34).
- Most countries today have national income within 1–2% of domestic product; wealth inequality is "a far greater domestic issue than it is an international one" (pp. 35-36).
- Capital is not an immutable concept; the boundary of what can be privately owned shifts with society (slaves, the atmosphere, knowledge) (p. 37).
- Capital in all its forms has a dual role, store of value and factor of production, so Piketty refuses to separate wealth from capital or to exclude housing (p. 38).
- Each of the two types of capital, housing and professional capital, is about half the capital stock in developed countries (p. 38).
- Market prices of capital are "largely arbitrary and uncertain", for all capital not just immaterial capital; yet total national wealth "obeys certain laws and conforms to certain regular patterns" (p. 39).
- In rich countries in 2010: national income about 30,000 euros per head, private wealth about 180,000 (half housing, half financial and business assets), so β ≈ 6, α ≈ 30%, r ≈ 5% (pp. 40-42).
- β says nothing about inequality within a country but measures the overall importance of capital, a "necessary first step" in studying inequality (p. 41).
- α = r × β is a tautology but should be called the first fundamental law because it links the three central concepts of capitalism (p. 42).
- Returns vary: stocks 7–8% over the long run, real estate and bonds 3–4%, rural land 4–5%, listed firms at 12–15 years of profits ≈ 6–8% before tax (pp. 42-44).
- α = r × β does not explain how any of the three is determined; it only says they are not independent: "two degrees of freedom, not three" (pp. 44-45).
- National accounts are "a social construct in perpetual evolution" reflecting each era's concerns: wealth estimates before 1914, income and output after the Depression, wealth again in the 1990s–2000s (pp. 45-47).
- Official national accounts deal only with aggregates, so distributional sources must supplement them (pp. 47-48).
- Europe and America produced 70–80% of world output from 1900 to 1980; this fell to about 50% by 2010 and may fall to 20–30%, in line with their share of population (p. 48).
- The divergence in per capita output is over and convergence has begun, though it is far from complete and could be reversed (p. 48).
- Global output per head in 2012 is about 10,000 euros (760 euros a month of income); regional incomes range from 150–250 euros a month (sub-Saharan Africa, India) to 2,500–3,000 euros (Western Europe, North America, Japan) (pp. 49-50).
- Inequality between countries is harder to measure than inequality within them; PPPs carry margins of error of about 10% or more, and no single index captures multidimensional differences in living standards (pp. 50-51).
- The rich-country share of world income was 46% at PPP but 57% at market exchange rates in 2012; the truth is probably closer to the first (p. 52).
- The global income distribution is more unequal than the output distribution, because rich countries also own capital in poor ones: "the rich countries are doubly wealthy" (p. 52).
- Africa is the only continent out of balance: income is about 5% below output, which implies nearly 20% of African capital is foreign-owned (p. 53).
- In 1913 European powers owned an estimated one-third to one-half of the domestic capital of Asia and Africa and more than three-quarters of their industrial capital (p. 53).
- The classical theory that capital mobility equalises marginal products and brings convergence fails logically (convergence of output does not imply convergence of income) and historically (the Asian catch-up countries financed themselves) (pp. 53-54).
- Foreign ownership breeds alternation between expropriating governments and property-protecting governments; international inequality of capital ownership is "almost impossible to sustain without a colonial type of political domination" (pp. 54-55).
- Gains from trade come mainly from knowledge diffusion and productivity, not from static specialisation gains (p. 55).
- The principal mechanism of convergence is the diffusion of knowledge, which depends on investment in education and a stable legal framework (p. 55).

## Arguments

**A. Definitions as choices: what "capital" includes (pp. 36-39)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 36-37) Human capital cannot be owned by another or permanently traded in non-slave societies. (p. 38) It is hard to separate the value of land from improvements, or of natural resources from investment in them. (p. 38) Gold and housing are both stores of value and productive. (p. 39) Immaterial capital is priced through stock-market capitalisation.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 36-39) Capital is all non-human, marketable wealth at market value; "capital" and "wealth" are synonyms.
- **Assessment**: These are defensible choices, but each has consequences later. (1) Using market value means β moves with asset prices as well as with saving and investment, so β can rise from capital gains (especially housing) without any change in the physical stock. (2) Including housing makes β and α sensitive to rents and house prices, a point later critics press on the capital-share story. (3) Excluding human capital from capital makes "labour income" absorb the return to skills, which affects how Piketty reads the rising human capital hypothesis (fm01, p. 19). Piketty is explicit about the choices and admits the line between "accumulation and appropriation" still needs examining (p. 38).

**B. The first fundamental law and how it is used (pp. 41-45)**

- **Derivation**: Capital income = r × capital (by definition of r as the average return). Divide both sides by national income: capital income / national income = r × capital / national income, i.e. α = r × β. Nothing is assumed beyond the definitions.
- **Worked numbers**: (p. 42) β = 600%, r = 5% gives α = 30%. Per head (p. 42): 30,000 euros of income = 21,000 labour + 9,000 capital; 9,000 / 180,000 = 5%. Firm examples (p. 44): capital 5m, value added 1m, profits 0.4m gives β = 5, α = 40%, r = 8%; capital 3m, profits 0.3m gives β = 3, α = 30%, r = 10%.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 44-45) The law says nothing about how β, α or r are determined, only that one of the three follows from the other two.
- **Critical point**: In practice r is not observed directly but calculated: "With a capital/income ratio on the order of 600 percent, this meant that the rate of return on capital was around 5 percent" (p. 42). And when α is hard to measure (mixed self-employment income), Piketty proposes to "apply a plausible average rate of return to the capital/income ratio" (p. 43). So the identity is run in both directions: r inferred from α and β, or α inferred from r and β. That is legitimate for an identity, but it means r > g, the book's central inequality, rests on an r that is often a residual of national accounts, not a separately measured return. Anyone checking r > g later (ch06, ch10) should ask which way the identity was used.

**C. National accounts are constructs (pp. 45-48)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 45-46) Early estimates (Petty, King, Boisguillebert, Vauban, Lavoisier) served tax-reform aims. (p. 46) Before 1914 wealth estimates dominated; (pp. 46-47) after the Depression, income and output took over and wealth estimates were sidelined. (p. 47) Wealth accounts returned in the 1990s–2000s, still imperfect (natural capital, environment).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 47) National accounts "always reflect the preoccupations of the era"; they should not be fetishised, but used critically they remain "an indispensable tool" (p. 47).
- **Assessment**: This is the book's main statement of methodological humility about the denominator of β and α. It also explains why Piketty can build long β series: he splices pre-1914 private estimates (Giffen, de Foville, Colson) onto post-1990 official balance sheets (p. 47). That splice is a key source risk for Part Two.

**D. Capital mobility does not explain convergence (pp. 53-55)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 53-54) Classical theory: capital flows from rich (low marginal product) to poor (high marginal product) until returns equalise, bringing convergence. (p. 54) Even then, at best output per head converges, and only if skills are also equal; income per head need not converge because foreign owners keep receiving returns. (p. 54) Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and China financed their own investment. (p. 54) Foreign-owned countries (colonies, Africa today) did worse, specialising badly and suffering political instability.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 55) Convergence comes from knowledge diffusion, helped by trade openness but dependent on education and legitimate government.
- **Assessment**: The logical point (output convergence ≠ income convergence) is sound and sets up the r-vs-g comparison for international ownership (p. 54). The historical premise for China is debatable: foreign direct investment into China was large in the 1990s–2000s, though Piketty can reply that domestic saving financed most investment. The claim about colonies is causal ("most notably because", p. 54) but no evidence is presented here.

## Evidence offered

- **Labour/capital split.** The textbook two-thirds/one-third figure (p. 32); endnote 5 gives 65–70% vs 30–35% (p. 436). The twentieth-century movement is stated, not yet shown; data come in ch06.
- **Rich-country orders of magnitude, 2010 (pp. 40-42).** Income 30,000–35,000 euros per head, private wealth 150,000–200,000 euros; β > 6 in Japan and Italy, < 5 in the US and Germany (p. 40). Source: official national wealth accounts compiled with central banks (p. 47); country detail in the online appendix (endnote 10, p. 436). Endnote 12: cash is only 1–2% of wealth; public assets roughly equal public debts (p. 436).
- **Returns by asset (pp. 42-44).** Stocks 7–8%, real estate and bonds 3–4%, rural land 4–5% (also in Austen and Balzac, where capital is twenty years of rent), a Paris apartment worth 1m euros renting at 2,500 euros a month (3%), a 100,000-euro apartment at 400 euros a month (nearly 5%), listed firms at 12–15 years of profits (6–8%). These are illustrative, not sourced series.
- **History of national accounting (pp. 45-47).** Petty (1664), King (1696), Boisguillebert (1695), Vauban (1707), Lavoisier (1791), Giffen (1870–1900), de Foville and Colson; annual series by Kuznets and Kendrick, Bowley and Clark, Dugé de Bernonville; post-war official series; 1990s–2000s wealth accounts. Endnote 19 points to Vanoli and Stone on SNA 1993, the first system with consistent capital accounts (p. 437).
- **Global output and population, 1700–2012 (Figures 1.1–1.3, pp. 48-49).** Europe 47% of world GDP in 1913, 25% in 2012; Europe 26% of world population in 1913, 10% in 2012; Asia-Africa per capita GDP from 37% of the world average in 1950 to 61% in 2012. Source: Angus Maddison (p. 48; endnote 20, p. 438), whose series cover only output and population, not national income, the capital-labour split or the capital stock (endnote 20).
- **Table 1.1, 2012 regional distribution (pp. 49-50).** World 7 billion people, 70 trillion euros of output; EU 540m at 27,000 euros; Russia-Ukraine 200m at 15,000; US-Canada 350m at 40,000; Latin America 600m at 10,000; sub-Saharan Africa 900m at 2,000; China 8,000; Japan about 30,000. Endnote 22 notes the EU bloc includes non-members such as Switzerland and Norway (p. 438). Values at PPP.
- **PPP vs exchange rates (Figures 1.4–1.5, pp. 50-52).** Euro/dollar: $1.30 market vs $1.20 PPP in 2012; euro/yuan: 8 market vs 5 PPP. PPP correction 1.6 for China and 2.5 for India (p. 51). Source: ICP (World Bank, Eurostat); endnote 27 cites the 2005 ICP report (p. 438). Maddison argued official statistics underestimate Chinese GDP (p. 51; endnote 30, p. 439).
- **Net foreign income (pp. 52-53).** +1–2% of GDP for the US, France and Britain; +2–3% for Japan and Germany; continents balance within 0.5%; Africa −5% (as low as −10% in some countries) from UN, World Bank and IMF balance-of-payments data since 1970 (p. 53). Endnote 33: the outflow of capital income from Africa is about three times the inflow of international aid (p. 439).
- **Derivation of Africa's foreign-owned share (p. 53).** If income is 5% below output and capital's share is 30%, the foreign-owned share of capital is about 5/30 ≈ 17%, which Piketty rounds to "nearly 20 percent". This assumes foreign-owned capital earns the same return as domestic capital. Since housing and farmland are rarely foreign-owned, the foreign share of manufacturing capital may be 40–50% (p. 53).
- **1913 imbalances (p. 53).** British national income about 10% above domestic product; France more than 5%; Germany a close third. Source: online appendix (endnote 34, p. 439).
- **Static trade gains (p. 55).** Endnote 36: opening India and China yielded static gains of just 0.4% of world GDP (3.5% for China, 1.6% for India) (p. 440); the study is not named in the note.
- **Endnote numbering anomaly.** Endnote 6 (attached to the identity National income = domestic output + net income from abroad, p. 36) repeats endnote 5's labour/capital split almost word for word, while endnote 7's definition of national income as net national product fits the earlier position (p. 436). The notes for this chapter appear to be shifted by one from note 6 onward, so readers should check note content against the text it is attached to.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Market value β (to ch03–ch06).** Because capital is valued at market prices (p. 38) and prices are "largely arbitrary and uncertain" (p. 39), β mixes accumulation with relative asset prices. Part Two must show how much of the post-1950 rise in β comes from saving (β = s / g) and how much from capital gains, especially housing. The claim that national wealth "obeys certain laws" (p. 39) despite arbitrary prices for each asset is a bet on aggregation.
- **Inferred r (to ch06, ch10, ch12).** r is often a residual of α and β (pp. 42-43). Later r > g figures should be checked for whether r is measured directly (asset returns, as for university endowments in ch12) or derived from national accounts.
- **Capital share history asserted early.** The claim that capital "was prospering as it had not done since 1913" by 2010 (p. 33) and that capital's share today is only slightly below the early nineteenth century (p. 34) are stated before evidence; ch06 supplies the series.
- **Politics vs mechanics again.** Capital's current importance is put down to slower growth *plus* "political regimes that objectively favor private capital" (p. 34). This doubles the explanation in fm01 (pp. 19, 22): β = s / g on one side, political choice on the other. The book has to weigh them.
- **"Entrepreneurial labour" in capital income.** Piketty concedes part of capital income may be labour (p. 32) and that mixed income is hard to split (p. 43). This weakens the sharpness of α and r for countries with many self-employed, and matters for historical series with large farm sectors.
- **International ownership and r vs g (to ch12).** The logic that rich countries can keep owning poor ones indefinitely depends on comparing the return poor countries pay with growth rates (p. 54). Piketty defers this; ch12 takes up whether oil states or China could come to own much of the world, echoing fm01's "emir of Qatar" (p. 7).
- **Housing is half of capital.** The claim that housing and professional capital are each about half the stock (pp. 38, 41) will matter when the book explains the rise in β: if housing drives it, the mechanism may be scarcity of land (Ricardo, fm01 pp. 6-7) rather than accumulation (Marx).
- **Convergence via knowledge, not capital (to fm01, ch02).** Restates fm01's main convergence force (p. 19). ch02 then argues that catch-up growth is transitional and that frontier growth is at most about 1.5%, which sets up a low g.

## Open questions

- How is r actually measured in the long-run series: directly from asset returns, or as α / β? And is it before or after tax and depreciation?
- How much would β and α change if housing were valued at replacement cost rather than market price, or if imputed rents were treated differently?
- Do the illustrative returns (stocks 7–8%, housing 3–5%) hold after taxes, transaction costs and losses on failed firms? The average r should include losses.
- Is the endnote shift from note 6 onward a translation or typesetting error, and are any substantive notes lost as a result?
- What would a parallel account of human capital, if valued, do to the capital share story? Piketty excludes it by definition, so the claim that capital's share has barely fallen is partly a matter of definition.
- How sensitive is the Africa foreign-ownership estimate to assuming foreign capital earns the same return as domestic capital?
