# ch09: Inequality of Labor Income (pp. 220–244)

## Question this chapter answers

What determines inequality of labor income, and in particular what caused the explosion of top wages and the rise of the supermanager in the United States after 1980? Piketty's answer: the standard theory (wages equal marginal productivity, which depends on the supply of and demand for skills, so inequality is a "race between education and technology") explains long-run wage levels and the broad wage structure, but fails at the very top. There, individual productivity cannot be measured, so pay is set by executives and compensation committees under country-specific institutions and social norms. Low top marginal tax rates since 1980 in the English-speaking countries amplified the process. The chapter also extends the top-income series to other rich countries and to six emerging economies, and attacks household surveys as a source.

## Leading propositions

**The education–technology model and its limits**

- The race between education and technology is the most accepted theory, but "does not explain everything", in particular not the supermanager or post-1980 US wage inequality (p. 220).
- The theory is "limited and naïve": productivity is not an objective quantity, and relative power often decides pay; still, skill supply (education) and skill demand (technology) matter and at least shape bargaining power (p. 221).
- France's wage hierarchy stayed stable despite mass schooling because all levels moved up together; without that democratisation inequality would have risen (pp. 221-222).
- Goldin and Katz: the US college wage premium fell until the 1970s and widened from the 1980s, when growth in college graduates slowed; they blame under-investment in higher education (p. 222).
- In the long run education and technology determine wage levels; fivefold wage growth in a century tracks fivefold growth in output per head (p. 222).
- Broader access to high-quality training is the best long-run lever for raising low and middle wages and cutting the top decile's share; Scandinavia's moderate wage inequality reflects its inclusive education (pp. 222-223).
- Health and education have intrinsic value; the marginal-productivity model is "far too instrumental and utilitarian" about training (p. 223).
- The main defect of the theory is that it cannot explain the diversity of wage distributions across countries and periods (p. 223).

**Institutions and the minimum wage**

- The labor market is a social construct; wartime wage boards and minimum-wage policy drove episodes of compression and expansion (p. 224).
- France: minimum wage rarely raised 1950–1968 (hierarchy widened), raised fast 1968–1983 (compressed), slowly 1983–2012 (widened) (p. 224).
- US: federal minimum wage from 1933; peak real value in 1969 ($1.60, or $10.10 in 2013 dollars); frozen at $3.35 in 1980–1990; $7.25 in 2013, a third below France's, reversing the early-1980s position (p. 224; Figure 9.1).
- The US bottom of the wage distribution tracked the minimum wage; the top decile's wage share rose steadily regardless. The minimum wage acts at the bottom, "much less" at the top (p. 225).
- Justifications for wage rules: productivity is hard to measure; arbitrary daily wage-setting would be unjust and inefficient (pp. 225-226); wage insurance (p. 226); specific investments (pp. 226-227); monopsony (p. 227).
- Card and Krueger showed the 1980–2000 US minimum wage was low enough to raise without job losses; Obama's proposed rise to $9 would likely have little employment effect; doubling or tripling would not be harmless; in France, where the minimum wage is high, training and tax reform are better tools, but the minimum should not be frozen (pp. 227-228).
- Summary: education and skills raise wages in the long run; labor-market rules matter crucially over the long periods during which the education–technology balance is set (p. 228).

**Explaining the US explosion**

- The theory's "most striking failure" is the explosion of top US labor incomes since 1980 (p. 228).
- First problem: the rise is concentrated in the top 1 and 0.1 percent; pay at $100,000–200,000 rose only slightly faster than average, pay above $500,000 "literally" exploded, yet there is no skill discontinuity between "the 9 percent" and "the 1 percent" in education, school selectivity or experience (pp. 228-229).
- Two phenomena coexist: a rising college premium (Goldin–Katz) and a take-off of the top centile within college graduates; the second is quantitatively larger, nearly three-quarters of the top decile's rise since 1970 (p. 229).
- Second problem: the explosion happened in some rich countries and not others, which points to institutions rather than universal technology (p. 229).
- Top centile share in the 1970s: 6–8 percent in the four English-speaking countries (Canada 9, Australia 5); early 2010s: nearly 20 percent US, 14–15 percent Britain and Canada, 9–10 percent Australia (p. 230). In these countries the top-pay explosion explains at least two-thirds of the rise in the top centile's share (p. 229).
- Continental Europe and Japan: top centile up only two to three points (Japan and France 7 to 9 percent, Sweden just over 4 to 7, Germany about 9 to nearly 11, Denmark just over 5 to nearly 7, Italy and Spain 7 to 9) (pp. 230-231).
- The top 0.1 percent: US share 2 to nearly 10 percent; France and Japan about 1.5 to 2.5 percent; Sweden under 1 to over 2 percent (p. 231). In all rich countries the top thousandth saw spectacular gains in 1990–2010 while average purchasing power stagnated (p. 232).
- The macroeconomic transfer to the top 1 percent is two to three points in continental Europe and Japan against 10–15 points in the US (p. 232).
- The US in 2000–2010 regained its 1910–1920 inequality level, with more labor and less capital income at the top; continental Europe and Japan remain far below their early-century levels (p. 232).
- Technology (including IT) and productivity growth were similar across rich countries, so the divergence needs an explanation the marginal-productivity theory cannot give (pp. 232-233).

**History and the emerging economies**

- In 1900–1910 the top centile exceeded 20 percent of national income in every European country with data, Sweden and Denmark included; top incomes then were almost wholly capital income, so the explanation lies in capital concentration (p. 233).
- The New World was less unequal (US and Canada 16–18 percent, Australia and New Zealand 11–12 percent); Japan was part of the "old world" (p. 233). Higher European concentration will be explained by low demographic growth (pp. 233-234).
- Top decile: 45–50 percent in Europe before 1914, a little over 40 in the US; similar 30–35 percent in 1950–1970; by 2000–2010, 45–50 percent US, 40 Britain, about 35 France and Germany, under 30 Sweden (p. 234).
- Germany's top centile rose rapidly in 1933–1938, out of phase with others; German tax records have serious gaps (no national data before World War I, none for 1938–1950) (p. 235).
- In South Africa, India, Indonesia and Argentina the top centile took about 20 percent in 1910–1950, 6–12 percent in 1950–1980, and about 15 percent now (p. 236).
- China's top centile rose from under 5 percent in the mid-1980s to 10–11 percent in 2000–2010; Colombia has stayed around 20 percent since 1990, above the US excluding capital gains (pp. 236-237).
- Tax data in poor countries cover only a small minority, have gaps, and have deteriorated since 1990 because of computerisation and disaffection with progressive taxation; India stopped publishing detailed data in the early 2000s (pp. 237-238).
- Household surveys give "a biased and misleadingly complacent view" of distribution; official inequality measures should combine surveys with tax data, in rich and poor countries alike (pp. 238-239).

**The illusion of marginal productivity**

- US inequality exceeding that of India or South Africa at various dates casts doubt on any explanation by objective productivity differences (p. 239).
- Most top earners are senior managers; for unique jobs under imperfect information individual marginal productivity becomes hard to define and close to "a pure ideological construct" (pp. 239-240).
- CFO thought-experiment: a firm with 10 billion euros revenue and 5 billion value added cannot tell whether its CFO contributes 100,000, 500,000 or 5 million euros a year; any experimental estimate would have a margin of error larger than the salary (p. 240).
- Top pay is set by superiors, by executives themselves or by compensation committees of peers; the result is "largely arbitrary" and depends on hierarchy and bargaining power; people who set their own pay will be generous to themselves (p. 240).
- Corporate governance rules and social norms limit this; norms vary across countries and periods, and it is very hard for one firm to defy them (p. 241).
- US and British firms became much more tolerant of very high pay after 1970; European and Japanese norms moved later and less (p. 241).
- The norms explanation "only shifts the difficulty": where norms come from is a question for all the social sciences (p. 241). The conservative revolution in the US and Britain was partly a reaction to being caught up by Europe and Japan, whose catch-up was a near-mechanical consequence of 1914–1945 (pp. 241-242).
- Not all wage inequality is set by norms: technology and skills bound most wages; norms dominate only where jobs are hard to replicate, affecting a few percent of employees at most and probably under 1 percent (p. 242).
- Self-set top pay is "a powerful force for divergence": there is no obvious ceiling, and the top centile's wage share could reach 15–20, 25–30 percent or more in the US (pp. 242-243).
- Firm-level evidence: executive pay tracks external shocks ("pay for luck") more than managerial performance, especially in the US (p. 243).
- The propensity to pay for luck varies with the top marginal tax rate. Its large fall after 1980 in the English-speaking countries changed how top pay is set, and the resulting high incomes buy political influence to keep rates low, a second, political force for divergence (pp. 243-244).

## Arguments

**A. Education and technology explain the long run, not the top (pp. 220-223, 228-229)**

- **Premises**: (p. 222) Wage levels grew with output per head. (pp. 221-222) France's stable hierarchy and the US college premium are consistent with skill supply racing technology. (pp. 228-229) But the post-1980 US rise is a discontinuity between the top 1 percent and "the 9 percent" with no matching discontinuity in measured skills.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 229) Education explains the college premium; it "does not seem" to explain the take-off of the top centile, which is the larger phenomenon.
- **Assessment**: The skills evidence is asserted ("it is hard to see any discontinuity", p. 228) without data in the text or a specific source in the endnotes (note 11 points only to Figures 8.6–8.8, which show incomes, not skills). Superstar-type models predict convex pay from small skill differences, so a pay discontinuity without a skill discontinuity is not, by itself, fatal to market explanations. Piketty does not engage those models here; the cross-country argument (B) does more work.

**B. Cross-country divergence points to institutions (pp. 229-233)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 230-231) Top shares rose two to three times more in the US than in Britain, Canada and Australia, and much more than in continental Europe and Japan. (pp. 232-233) Technology, including IT, and productivity growth were similar across rich countries.
- **Unstated premise**: that the market for top executives is national rather than global, and that firm size, sector mix (finance) and the scale of stock-market capitalisation do not differ enough across these countries to explain the gap.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 229) "institutional differences between countries rather than general and a priori universal causes such as technological change played a central role".
- **Assessment**: This is the strongest argument in the chapter. Its weakness is the unstated premise: Piketty himself grants that supermanager pay "should of course be seen in relation to firm size" (p. 242) but does not test it across countries.

**C. Marginal productivity is undefined at the top, so pay is bargained (pp. 239-241)**

- **Premises**: (p. 239) Top earners are mostly senior managers in near-unique jobs. (pp. 239-240) Under imperfect information, individual marginal product cannot be estimated; experiments would cost more than the salary at stake (note 34). (p. 240) Pay is set by self-interested insiders and peer committees.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 240) Top pay is "largely arbitrary" and reflects hierarchy and bargaining power; (p. 241) its level is bounded by governance rules and social norms.
- **Assessment**: The argument shows that marginal productivity cannot be *verified*, not that pay departs from it. The empirical support comes from (D) and from the cross-country pattern (B). The norm hypothesis is explicitly admitted to move the question rather than answer it (p. 241).

**D. Pay for luck refutes the productivity justification (p. 243)**

- **Premises**: (p. 243) If pay followed marginal productivity, it should vary with the part of firm performance management controls, not with external shocks. (p. 243) In fact pay rises most when performance improves for external reasons (Bertrand and Mullainathan 2001; Bebchuk and Fried 2004, note 35).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 243) This is "the most convincing proof" of failed corporate governance and of the absence of a rational productivity basis for very high pay.
- **Assessment**: The finding is real and well cited, but it supports a governance-failure (rent-extraction) reading, which is a bargaining story, more than a pure social-norms story. Bertrand and Mullainathan's title ("The Ones without Principals Are") ties pay for luck to weak shareholder oversight, a governance variable Piketty folds into "institutions".

**E. Low top tax rates amplify bargaining and feed back politically (pp. 243-244)**

- **Premises**: (p. 243) Pay for luck varies with the top marginal tax rate. (p. 243) After 1980 top rates fell sharply in the English-speaking countries, raising the private return to bargaining for pay. (pp. 243-244) The resulting incomes fund parties, lobbies and think tanks that defend low rates.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 243-244) A second, political force for divergence, alongside self-set pay.
- **Assessment**: Here the chapter only announces the argument; the evidence (cross-country elasticities of top income shares to top rates) is in ch14. Note that this is a bargaining model: high rates reduce the gain from extracting pay, not the gain from working harder.

**F. Household surveys understate inequality (pp. 238-239)**

- **Premises**: (p. 238) In Colombia and Argentina tax data give top-centile shares around 20 percent. (pp. 238-239) The highest incomes in household surveys are only 4–5 times average, implying a top-centile share under 5 percent.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 239) Surveys, often the only source used by the World Bank and governments, are biased and complacent; they must be combined with tax data.

## Evidence offered

- **Goldin and Katz**, *The Race between Education and Technology* (2010): US college premium versus growth in college graduates, 1890–2005 (p. 222; note 1).
- **Figure 9.1**: real hourly minimum wage, France and the US, 1950–2013, in 2013 prices: US $3.80 to $7.30, France €2.10 to €9.40 (p. 224). Minimum-wage history and levels for Britain (£6.19 in 2013), Germany and Sweden (branch bargaining, about €10 in many branches) (p. 225).
- **Card and Krueger**, *Myth and Measurement* (1995), using neighbouring states with different minimum wages (p. 227; note 10).
- **Figures 9.2–9.4**: top centile share in English-speaking countries; continental Europe and Japan; northern and southern Europe, 1910–2010 (pp. 229-231). These exclude capital gains (note 13), which would widen the US gap (US top centile above 20 percent in the 2000s with capital gains).
- **Figures 9.5–9.6**: captioned "The top decile income share" but described in the caption and text as the top 0.1 percent share (pp. 231-232). A labelling error in the edition; the text's numbers (US 2 to nearly 10 percent) are top-thousandth figures.
- **Figures 9.7–9.8**: top decile share, decennial averages, US and four European countries (Britain, France, Germany, Sweden), and a four-country European average; Piketty calls the average "somewhat abusively" calculated (p. 234; note 25 says weighting or adding countries would change little).
- **Emerging economies, Figure 9.9**: income-tax data from South Africa (1913), India (1922), Indonesia (1920), Argentina (1932), China (from 1986) and Colombia (from 1993), with national income estimates (pp. 235-237; note 27). Colonial-era taxes were modelled on Britain's 1909 income tax (p. 236).
- **Data quality caveats**: tax covers a small minority in poor countries, so only top-centile shares are estimable; South Africa's top decile reaches 50–55 percent where data allow (p. 237); breaks around independence (p. 237); deterioration after 1990 (pp. 237-238; notes 29-31). India's 2010 point rests on "very imperfect data" on managers' pay (note 31).
- **India's "black hole"**: rising top share explains a quarter to a third of the 1990–2000 gap between national-accounts and survey growth (p. 238); the gap is nearly half of measured growth, about 4 versus 2 percent a year per capita (note 32; Banerjee and Piketty 2005).
- **German records**: state income taxes from 1880–1890, no national records before World War I, frequent breaks in the 1920s, none for 1938–1950, data only every three years, changing borders (p. 235).
- **CFO thought-experiment** (p. 240) and the theory of costly experimentation (note 34, no specific reference given in the text).
- **Bertrand and Mullainathan** (2001) and **Bebchuk and Fried** (2004) on pay for luck and pay without performance (p. 243; note 35). The text misspells the name as "Mullainhatan".
- **Note 12**: survey-based labor and education economics (Blank 2011; Rajan 2010) misses the top-centile take-off because only tax data show it; surveys have richer covariates but small samples.
- **Note 19**: the Pareto relation between top shares; in Sweden in the 1970s the top 0.1 percent earned barely twice the top 1 percent, in the US in the 2000s 4–5 times.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **A second engine of divergence beside r > g.** The book's headline mechanism is capital-driven. This chapter adds a labor-income mechanism (self-set pay under weak norms and low top tax rates) and calls it "a powerful force for divergence of the wealth distribution" (p. 242). The link to *wealth* is asserted, not shown here: it requires that supermanager pay is saved and turned into capital, which ch10–ch12 must establish. For syntheses, treat the US rise as the case where Piketty's evidence is about bargaining power and institutions, not about the return on capital.
- **Marginal productivity: accepted for most, rejected for the top.** Piketty uses the model for the long run and for most wages (pp. 222, 242), and calls it "a pure ideological construct" at the top (p. 240). The boundary is set by job replicability and affects "probably less than 1 percent" of employees (p. 242). The position is coherent, but the cut-off is stipulated, not derived.
- **Norms versus bargaining versus taxes.** Three explanations run together: social norms (p. 241), bargaining power and governance failure (pp. 240, 243), and top tax rates (p. 243). Piketty says tax changes are "linked to changes in social norms" but then "proceed according to a logic of their own" (p. 243). The direction of causation between norms and rates is left open; ch14 must be read for whether the tax-rate evidence can distinguish them.
- **Firm size conceded in passing.** "The explosion of supermanager salaries should of course be seen in relation to firm size" (p. 242) is the opening for the Gabaix–Landier-type market explanation, which the chapter does not engage.
- **Inconsistent spans for the top-centile contribution.** ch08 says about 11 of the 15-point rise in the top decile share went to the top 1 percent "since 1980" (pp. 214-216); here the same "nearly three-quarters" is "since 1970" (p. 229). Same figure, different base years.
- **Demographic explanation promised** (pp. 233-234): Europe's greater Belle Époque wealth concentration "fairly naturally explained by the low demographic growth rate", previewing ch10–ch11 (r > g with low g).
- **Education links**: elite schools favour privileged students (p. 223) and meritocratic extremism (p. 242) are both deferred to ch11 and ch13. The intrinsic-value point on health and education (p. 223, note 3) is a jab at "human capital" (cf. ch01–ch02 definitions of capital excluding human capital).
- **Conservative revolution** as reaction to being caught up (pp. 241-242) reuses ch02's catch-up argument.
- **Source critique continues ch08**: tax data superior to surveys at the top (pp. 238-239, note 12), but tax data deteriorating (pp. 237-238). The book's evidence base is thinning just where it matters most for the present.

## Open questions

- What evidence shows no skill discontinuity between "the 9 percent" and "the 1 percent" (p. 228)? The claim is central to rejecting skill-biased change but is not sourced in the chapter or notes.
- Can firm size, stock-market valuation and the global market for executives explain the US/continental difference, or does the institutional story survive controls for them? The chapter grants the size point (p. 242) without testing it.
- If norms "only shift the difficulty" (p. 241), what would count as evidence that norms changed first and pay followed, rather than the reverse?
- How does supermanager income become inherited wealth, and on what timescale? This is the bridge needed to fold the US labor-income story back into the capital-centred thesis (ch10–ch12).
- Does the top-tax-rate mechanism in ch14 rest on the same pay-for-luck evidence, or on separate cross-country regressions? Can it separate bargaining from labor-supply responses?
- How reliable are the China and India series given the data deterioration Piketty himself documents (pp. 237-238, notes 31 and 33)?
