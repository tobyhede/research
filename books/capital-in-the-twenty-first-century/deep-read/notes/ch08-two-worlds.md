# ch08: Two Worlds (pp. 196–220)

## Question this chapter answers

How and why did the structure of income inequality change in France and the United States between the Belle Époque and 2010? Piketty's answer has two halves. In France (standing in for continental Europe and Japan) total income inequality fell because top incomes from capital collapsed between 1914 and 1945, while the wage hierarchy stayed almost unchanged: "rentiers fell behind managers". In the United States inequality fell less in 1914–1945, then rose after 1980 to Belle Époque European levels, and this rise is mostly a rise in *wage* inequality at the very top: the arrival of "supermanagers". The chapter also sets out, more openly than most, the limits of the income-tax sources on which the whole of Part Three rests.

## Leading propositions

**France**

- The top decile's share of national income fell from 45–50 percent on the eve of World War I to 30–35 percent today, a 15-point drop (p. 196).
- That fall is "due entirely" to lower top incomes from capital; the top decile's share of wages has stayed around 25 percent, and the bottom half's around 25–30 percent of wages, with no long-run trend (p. 197).
- The top centile's share fell from over 20 percent of national income in 1900–1910 to 8–9 percent in 2000–2010 (barely 7 percent in the early 1980s); its share of *wages* stayed at about 6–7 percent throughout (pp. 197-198).
- No general structural compression of wage inequality occurred, against Kuznets's prediction; this is "no doubt the most important lesson the twentieth century has to teach" (p. 198).
- The compression is concentrated in 1914–1945 and was caused by war, depression and the policies they brought (rent control, nationalisations, inflation that ruined holders of government debt), which cut the capital/income ratio and capital's share (pp. 198-199).
- Because capital is more concentrated than labor, shocks to capital fall hardest on the top decile and centile (p. 199).
- Estate-tax data since 1791 show nothing presaging a spontaneous fall in wealth concentration before 1914 (p. 199).
- In 1932 capital income was the main source of income for the top 0.5 percent; in 2005 it dominates only in the top 0.1 percent. The capital-dominated group was 5 times larger in 1932 and 10 times larger in the Belle Époque (pp. 199-200).
- So the top centile today is barely more unequal in income than in wages, but the change came without any widening of the wage hierarchy: rentiers fell; managers did not rise (p. 200).
- Why rentiers have not come back, given that the capital/income ratio has returned to Belle Époque levels, is "the more complex" and more important question; progressive income and inheritance taxes are one answer, but not the only one (pp. 200-201).
- At the top, the rise of capital income's share is due to financial assets, mainly dividends; rent stays around 10 percent of income and even falls within the top centile (p. 203).
- The 1914–1945 fall in the top decile share (about 18 points) is three-quarters due to the top centile (about 14 points) (p. 206).
- Short- and medium-run movements are real and politically decisive: wage compression in both world wars and post-war re-widening; rising inequality 1945–1967; compression 1968–1983 driven by minimum-wage increases; re-widening after the 1982–1983 austerity turn (pp. 208-210).
- Since the late 1990s top French salaries have risen sharply: the top centile's wage share from under 6 percent to 7.5–8 percent by the early 2010s; gains of over 50 percent in ten years at the top 0.1 and 0.01 percent (p. 211).
- The real long-run issue in France is not the top decile's income share but the renewed importance of inherited wealth, which needs direct study (p. 211).

**United States**

- The US was less unequal than Europe in 1900–1910 (top decile a little over 40 percent versus 45–50 percent in France) because its capital/income ratio and capital share were lower and wealth was somewhat less concentrated (p. 212).
- Inequality rose in the 1920s, with the top decile above 50 percent in 1928–1929, and capital gains already mattered (p. 213).
- The 1914–1945 compression was smaller than in Europe: "Europe in 1914–1945 witnessed the suicide of rentier society, but nothing of the sort occurred in the United States" (p. 213).
- 1950–1980 was the US low point (top decile 30–35 percent) and, for white citizens, the US was then more equal than France (pp. 213-214).
- Since 1980 the top decile share rose from 30–35 percent to 45–50 percent, about 15 points (p. 214); with a correction for under-declared capital income it slightly exceeded 50 percent in 2007 and the early 2010s (p. 214).
- Capital gains add volatility but little to the structural trend: excluding them, the top decile still rose from about 32 to over 46 percent; the structural contribution of capital gains is about one point (pp. 214-215).
- The 2008 crisis did not halt the trend; 2010 exceeded 2007 excluding capital gains (p. 215).
- About 11 of the 15 points went to the top 1 percent, roughly half of that to the top 0.1 percent (pp. 215-216).
- Rising inequality contributed to financial instability, through stagnant lower and middle incomes and household borrowing, but it was not the sole or primary cause of 2008; a rising capital/income ratio and huge cross-border asset positions may matter more (pp. 216-217).
- The US rise is "largely" a rise in wage inequality at the top. The top decile's wage share rose from 25 to 35 percent, about two-thirds of the rise in its share of total income (pp. 217-218).
- Rising wage inequality was not offset by career mobility: measured over 10, 20 or 30 years, the increase is the same (p. 218).
- Capital income still accounts for about a third of the rise in US income inequality; the top centile shows "cohabitation", not replacement (pp. 218-219).
- In 1929 capital income dominated for the top 1 percent; in 2007 only for the top 0.1 percent, and without capital gains only at the top 0.01 percent (p. 219).
- 60–70 percent of the top 0.1 percent in 2000–2010 are top managers; athletes, actors and artists under 5 percent; finance about 20 percent of the top 0.1 percent against under 10 percent of GDP (pp. 219-220).

## Arguments

**A. The fall of the rentier explains the French compression (pp. 196-200)**

- **Premises**: (p. 196) The top decile's share of national income fell about 15 points over the century. (p. 197) The top decile's share of wages, and the bottom half's, did not change. (pp. 197-198) The top centile's share of income fell by more than half while its share of wages stayed at 6–7 percent. (p. 199) Capital income is overrepresented at the top, and 1914–1945 brought large shocks to capital.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 198) The reduction of inequality is "largely explained by the fall of the rentier"; no Kuznets-type structural compression occurred.
- **Assessment**: The decomposition is close to an accounting identity once the wage series are taken as given: if wage shares are flat and total shares fall, the fall must be in non-wage income. The weight therefore rests on the reliability of separate wage series (from the 1917 *cédulaire* wage tax, note 4) and on national-accounts denominators. Note 8 concedes private-sector pay before 1914–1917 is "little known" and is inferred from civil-service scales.

**B. The compression was political and violent, not spontaneous (pp. 198-199)**

- **Premises**: (p. 198) The compression is concentrated in 1914–1945; shares reached their low after World War II and did not recover. (p. 199) Estate-tax data show wealth concentration still rising on the eve of World War I.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 198) "it was war, and not harmonious democratic or economic rationality" that reset the distribution; the history of inequality is "chaotic and political".
- **Assessment**: The counterfactual (that without 1914 concentration would have kept rising) rests on the wealth series of ch10, not on income data, which begin only in 1915. The argument is strong on timing, weaker on mechanism: it bundles physical destruction, bankruptcy, inflation and policy without apportioning them here (that was ch05's job).

**C. Rentiers have not come back, and that needs explaining (pp. 200-201)**

- **Premises**: (p. 200) Capital dominates today only above the top 0.1 percent. (pp. 200-201) The capital/income ratio has returned to Belle Époque levels (Part Two).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 201) The non-return of the rentier is the interesting question; progressive taxation is one cause, others may be "equally important".
- **Assessment**: The puzzle is posed, not solved; ch10–ch11 take it up. It sits uneasily with the claim on p. 211 that the "real issue" is the renewed importance of inheritance: the chapter asks why rentiers stayed down and also warns they are coming back.

**D. The interwar paradox resolved by income source (pp. 205-207)**

- **Premises**: (p. 206) "The 1 percent" lived on dividends and interest, which collapsed in the Depression. (pp. 206-207) "The 9 percent" were salaried managers and civil servants, largely spared unemployment, holding nominal pay while prices fell 25 percent in 1929–1935, and receiving nominally rigid rents.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 207) The top decile share rose in 1929–1935 while the top centile share fell; the Popular Front then reversed it.
- **Methodological conclusion**: (p. 207) A Gini coefficient would have hidden all of this; decomposition by fractile and income source is necessary.

**E. US inequality since 1980 is mainly wage inequality at the very top (pp. 214-220)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 214-215) Capital gains add only about one point to the structural trend. (pp. 215-216) About three-quarters of the 15-point rise went to the top centile. (pp. 217-218) The top decile's wage share rose 10 points, about two-thirds of the total-income increase. (p. 219) 60–70 percent of the top 0.1 percent are top managers.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 219) The new US inequality is about "supermanagers" more than "superstars"; capital income still contributes about a third (p. 218).
- **Assessment**: This is the chapter's most consequential finding for the book's thesis. The main driver of the best-known modern rise in inequality is not r > g but labor income. Piketty presents it straightforwardly; the explanation (bargaining power, norms, top tax rates) comes in ch09 and ch14. The occupational evidence comes from matched tax and corporate records (Bakija, Cole and Heim, note 42) and depends on "what definitions one chooses" (p. 219). Note 42 adds a strong qualification: at the top 0.1 percent capital income is over half of income with capital gains and about a quarter without, and the origin of these fortunes is unknown.

**F. Inequality and the 2008 crisis (pp. 215-217)**

- **Premises**: (p. 216) The top 10 percent took three-quarters of US growth 1977–2007, the top 1 percent nearly 60 percent; the bottom 90 percent grew under 0.5 percent a year. (p. 216) Stagnant incomes plus deregulated lenders made household borrowing likely. (p. 217) The internal transfer (about 15 points) is nearly four times the trade deficit (about 4 points).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 216) Inequality "contributed" to instability, but (p. 217) was not the sole or primary cause.
- **Assessment**: The mechanism is asserted with "absolutely no doubt" (p. 216) but supported only by reference to Kumhof–Rancière and Rajan (note 32); no evidence on household debt is presented. The comparison of internal transfer to trade deficit sets a stock-like redistribution against a flow imbalance; both are in points of national income, but their causal links to financial fragility are different.

## Evidence offered

- **Figure 8.1**: top decile share of national income and of total wages, France, 1910–2010 (p. 196).
- **Figure 8.2**: top centile share of income and of wages, France, 1910–2010 ("the fall of rentiers") (p. 197).
- **Figures 8.3–8.4**: composition of top incomes by fractile (P90–95 up to P99.99–100) into labor, mixed and capital income, France 1932 and 2005 (pp. 199-200). Note 12: self-declared income excludes imputed rent since 1960, so "the 9 percent" appear to have under 10 percent capital income; with imputed rent it is about 20 percent.
- **Sources for France**: income-tax declarations from 1915 (general income tax adopted 15 July 1914) and the *cédulaire* wage tax from 1917; totals from national accounts using Kuznets's method; 1910–1914 completed from pre-war estimates by tax authorities and economists (p. 199; note 4). Estate-tax records since 1791 (p. 199). Detailed series in Piketty, *Les hauts revenus en France* (2001) (note 3). French wartime tax statistics were compiled without interruption and were unusually rich (note 16).
- **Tax-source limits** (pp. 203-205): capital income under-declared through evasion and legal exemption, so top shares are lower bounds; correction for France adds 2–3 points (up to 5) to capital's share, putting the top decile near 50 percent in the Belle Époque and slightly above 35 percent now (p. 204). Corrections are not applied systematically across WTID countries, so series "probably slightly" understate the post-1970 rise (p. 204). Capital gains are excluded in France but measured in the US; including them would raise capital's share at the top ten-thousandth from 60 to 70–80 percent (p. 205). Tax returns say nothing about whether capital was inherited or saved (p. 205).
- **Interwar France**: prices fell 25 percent 1929–1935; private-sector wages fell more than 50 percent; public wage bill flat in nominal terms until 1933; civil-service raises 1927–1931; Laval's 1934–1935 cuts; Matignon Accords and September 1936 devaluation (pp. 206-207). Top decile 47 to 29 percent and top centile 21 to 7 percent across 1914–1945 (note 17).
- **Post-war France**: minimum wage created 1950; Grenelle Accords raised it 20 percent in 1968; partial indexation 1970; minimum-wage purchasing power up over 130 percent 1968–1983 against about 50 percent for the mean wage, versus 25 percent against more than doubling in 1950–1968 (p. 210; note 20 cites Baudelot and Lebeaupin, INSEE 1979). 1982–1983 austerity turn (p. 210).
- **Recent top French salaries**: Landais (2007) and Godechot (2012) (p. 211; note 22).
- **Figure 8.5**: top decile share, US, 1910–2010, with and without capital gains (p. 211). **Figure 8.6**: decomposition of the top decile (top 1, next 4, bottom 5 percent) (p. 212). US federal income tax from 1913; separate wage data only from 1927 (p. 212; notes 23-24).
- **US fractile thresholds 2010** (household income): P90 $108,000; P95 $150,000; P99 $352,000; P99.9 $1.5 million (pp. 215-216; note 29).
- **US correction for capital income**: uncorrected top decile 49.7 percent (2007) and 47.9 percent (2010); correction adds 2–3 points (note 26; the note prints "1007" for 2007).
- **Growth shares 1977–2007**: Atkinson, Piketty and Saez (2011), Table 1 (p. 216; note 33).
- **Wartime wage compression in the US**: National War Labor Board approved raises 1941–1945, mostly for the lowest paid (p. 217); Piketty–Saez (2003), Goldin–Margo (1992) (note 36).
- **Figures 8.7–8.8**: top decile and top centile shares of income versus wages, US (p. 218).
- **Mobility**: Kopczuk, Saez and Song (2010), Social Security earnings data since 1937 (p. 218; note 38).
- **Figures 8.9–8.10**: composition of top US incomes, 1929 and 2007 (p. 219).
- **Who the top earners are**: Kaplan and Rauh (2009) show proxy-statement data on the five highest-paid executives per listed firm cannot account for the rise (p. 219; note 41); Bakija, Cole and Heim (2010) matched tax and compensation records give 60–70 percent managers in the top 0.1 percent (p. 219; note 42).
- **Wolff and Zacharias (2009)**: the top centile is a "cohabitation" of capital-rich and labor-rich; note 39 records Piketty conceding that his 2003 article with Saez overstated the replacement of "coupon-clipping rentiers" by "working rich".

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **The US case does not run on r > g.** The book's central mechanism (introduction, ch10) is capital accumulating faster than output and income. Yet the chapter's own decomposition gives about two-thirds of the US rise to wages (pp. 217-218) and only a structural one point to capital gains (p. 215). Piketty keeps the two in one frame by (i) insisting capital income still explains a third and dominates the very top (pp. 218-219, note 42), (ii) treating supermanager pay as a separate "force for divergence" (ch09, p. 242), and (iii) suggesting today's high earners become tomorrow's rentiers (ch11's inheritance argument). A reader should keep these as three distinct claims, of which only (i) is evidenced in this chapter.
- **"Society of managers" versus "less extreme rentier society"** (p. 200). Piketty gives both descriptions in one paragraph and calls the second "more correct". Later chapters lean on the second; this chapter's data more naturally support the first for France's top centile.
- **The non-return of the rentier** (pp. 200-201) versus the "renewed importance of inherited wealth" (p. 211). Both can be true (the rentier share of top *income* is low while inheritance's share of *wealth* is rising), but they point to different measurement objects. ch10–ch11 have to reconcile them.
- **Links back**: ch05–ch06 supply the capital/income ratio and capital-share series used to explain both 1914–1945 and the post-1980 rise (pp. 199, 211); ch07 supplies the fractile vocabulary and the critique of Gini (applied here on p. 207). The Kuznets curve from the introduction is the explicit target (p. 198).
- **Links forward**: ch09 explains supermanager pay; ch10 the concentration of wealth; ch11 inheritance and "society of rentiers"; ch13 intergenerational mobility (note 37); ch14 top marginal tax rates; the minimum-wage periodisation (p. 210) is reused in ch09 (p. 224).
- **Internal slips in the text**: p. 209 cites "Figures 8.3 and 8.3" for top-centile wage inequality (probably 8.1–8.2); p. 212 says the top decile's overall evolution is in "Figure 8.6" (the caption shows that is Figure 8.5), and its description of what each figure pair shows ("the top decile and top centile of the wage hierarchy" in the first, "wage hierarchies themselves" in the second) does not match the captions; p. 213 "Hockney" for the Titanic character Hockley. None changes the argument but they matter when checking figure citations.
- **Mobility claim** (p. 218): annual inequality is not offset by lifetime mobility. This answers a standard defence of rising inequality and will matter for any synthesis on meritocracy.

## Open questions

- How much of the US "wage" rise reflects reclassification (stock options counted as wages at exercise, note 44; the 1986 tax reform shifting corporate profits onto personal returns, note 28)? Piketty says the 1986 shift is short-term only; this deserves checking against the online appendix.
- The occupational split in the top 0.1 percent depends on how "managers" are defined (p. 219), and includes executives of firms whose pay is not disclosed. What share are owner-managers of private firms, whose "labor" income is partly a return to capital (cf. note 11 on income disguised as dividends)?
- What explains the non-return of the rentier beyond progressive taxation (p. 201)? ch10–ch11 should be checked for whether the answer is demographic, fiscal or the slow pace of re-accumulation.
- Does the evasion correction (2–3 points, pp. 204, 214) really scale equally across periods, given the post-1980 rise of tax havens that Piketty himself says are probably outside national accounts (p. 214)?
- The finance–inequality causal claim (p. 216) is asserted rather than shown. Where, if anywhere, does the book return to it with evidence?
