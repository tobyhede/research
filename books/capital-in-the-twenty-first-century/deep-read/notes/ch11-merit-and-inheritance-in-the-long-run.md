# ch11: Merit and Inheritance in the Long Run (pp. 273–313)

## Question this chapter answers

How large is inheritance, compared with work and saving, as a source of wealth and of lifetime resources, and how has that changed since 1820? Was Vautrin right that inheriting beats working, and will he be right again? Piketty's answer: in France the annual inheritance flow followed a U-curve (20–25 percent of national income in the nineteenth century, 4–5 percent in the 1950s, about 15 percent in 2010); the "end of inheritance" after 1945 was an artefact of war shocks and high growth, not of longer lives or human capital; whenever r is well above g, it is "all but inevitable" that inheritance dominates saving; and a return to rentier dominance would clash with the meritocratic hope on which democratic legitimacy rests (pp. 274-276, 307).

This is the normative core of the book's charge that r > g inequality is "arbitrary". It is also where the book's second formal device (b_y = μ × m × β) is introduced and used.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Annual inheritance flow (b_y) | p. 275 | Total value of bequests plus inter vivos gifts received in a year, as a percentage of national income (p. 275). Must include gifts (p. 277). Also expressed against household disposable income (p. 293). |
| Fiscal flow | p. 277 | Inheritance flow measured directly from estate and gift tax records, corrected for small undeclared estates and exempt assets such as life insurance (p. 277). |
| Economic flow | p. 277 | Inheritance flow computed from the private wealth stock as b_y = μ × m × β (pp. 277-278); always slightly above the fiscal flow, the gap read as an estimate of fraud or record deficiencies (p. 277). |
| b_y = μ × m × β | p. 278 | Decomposition of the economic flow; β = private wealth / national income, m = (adult) mortality rate, μ = average wealth at death / average wealth of the living (p. 278). Called "a pure accounting identity" and "a useful tautology" (p. 278). μ is corrected for gifts (note 4, p. 469). |
| μ (wealth of the dead relative to the living) | p. 278 | Depends on the age profile of wealth: the more wealth rises with age, the higher μ (p. 279). Zero under a pure Modigliani life cycle (p. 279). Above 100 percent in France since 1820 except 1940–1950 (p. 283); about 120 percent uncorrected and over 220 percent including prior gifts in 2000–2010 (p. 285). |
| Rate of estate devolution / annual rate of transmission (μ × m) | pp. 282-283 | Inheritance flow as a share of total private wealth; about 3.3–3.5 percent (about 1/30) in 1820s–1910s, about 2 percent in the 1950s, above 2.5 percent in 2000–2010 (p. 283). In low growth it tends to 1/H, where H is generation length (note 22, p. 471). |
| Life-cycle theory / Modigliani triangle | p. 279 | Wealth accumulated for retirement and consumed before death, so μ ≈ 0; inequality of wealth is just inequality of labour income shifted in time (p. 279). Rejected by the data (pp. 283-284). |
| Annuitized wealth | p. 279 | Wealth that vanishes at death (pension funds, annuities); under 5 percent of private wealth in France, at most 15–20 percent in English-speaking countries (p. 284). |
| Generational duration (H) | p. 282 | Average age at childbirth, stable at about 30; hence the roughly 30-year gap between average age at death and average age at inheritance (p. 282). |
| Rejuvenation of wealth | p. 287 | The 1914–1945 reversal of the wealth-age profile, with fifty-somethings 40 percent richer than octogenarians in 1947 (p. 287). |
| Reconstruction capitalism | p. 288 | The postwar phase of low inheritance; "by its nature a transitional phase" (p. 288). |
| Share of inherited wealth in total wealth | p. 291 | Sum of bequests and gifts received over roughly the previous thirty years (capitalised within limits; note 23, p. 471) as a share of private wealth of the living (p. 291). |
| Total (lifetime) resources | p. 294 | Inheritance (bequests and gifts) plus labour income net of taxes, capitalised over life at the average net return, by birth cohort (p. 294). |
| Lower class standard | p. 295 | Average labour income of the least well-paid 50 percent; the unit for comparing top heirs and top earners (p. 295). |
| Rastignac's dilemma | p. 295 | Whether a top-centile inheritance (or marrying one) yields more lifetime resources than a top-centile job (p. 295). |
| Volume effect / concentration effect | p. 297 | Labour income is about three times inheritance in total resources (volume); top-centile share of wealth relative to top-centile share of wages (concentration). Rentiers top managers when concentration outweighs volume (pp. 297-298). |
| Patrimonial society (classic) | p. 298 | Society in which "a truly comfortable life required the possession of a large fortune", with a threshold near 30 times average income (p. 298). |
| Meritocratic extremism | p. 303 | Use of meritocratic beliefs to justify very large wage inequalities, as more legitimate than inherited inequality (pp. 303-304). |
| Society of petits rentiers | p. 306 | Many medium inheritances (200,000 to 2 million euros) in place of a few huge ones; inheritance large compared with lifetime earnings but too small to live on (p. 306). |
| Rent / rentier (descriptive sense) | p. 308 | Income from capital of any legal form (rent, interest, dividends, profits, royalties) that is "simply remuneration for ownership of the asset, independent of any labor"; rentier = one who lives on it (p. 308). Explicitly *not* the economist's "monopoly rent" or market imperfection (p. 308). |
| Meritocratic hope | p. 307 | The democratic belief that inequality is "based more on merit and effort than on kinship and rents", needed to reconcile equal rights with unequal conditions (p. 307). |

## Leading propositions

- Whenever r is "significantly and durably" above g, it is "all but inevitable" that inheritance predominates over saving; "in strict logic, it could be otherwise" (p. 274). The past "tends to devour the future" (p. 274).
- In a low-growth, high-return twenty-first century inheritance will probably be as important as in the nineteenth; this is already visible in France and Europe, less in the US because of higher population growth (p. 274).
- The structure will still differ: less extreme wealth concentration, a wider earned-income hierarchy, and a stronger correlation of wealth and income, so that one can be "both a supermanager and a “medium rentier”" (pp. 274-275).
- French inheritance flow: 20–25 percent of national income throughout the nineteenth century, slightly rising (25 percent in 1900–1910 against barely 20 in the 1820s) (p. 275); it fell by a factor of 5 or 6 by 1950 (4–5 percent), then rose by a factor of 3 or 4 to 15 percent in 2010 (p. 276).
- The fall in the inheritance flow after 1914 was nearly twice the fall in private wealth (p. 276; quantified p. 288).
- The fiscal and economic flows, computed by "totally independent" methods, agree, which "demonstrates the robustness" of the data (p. 277).
- b_y = μ × m × β is an accounting identity; each factor carried an optimistic belief in the end of inheritance (β: capital loses importance; m: longer lives; μ: life-cycle saving), and each followed its own U-curve, acting simultaneously "in part for accidental reasons" (pp. 278-279).
- β: capital changed form but kept its importance; nonhuman capital grew in proportion with human skill (p. 280).
- m: adult mortality fell from about 2.2 percent (nineteenth century) to 1.1–1.2 percent (2000–2010) and will rise to 1.4–1.5 percent by 2040–2050 as baby boomers die; the long-run fall is about one-third (pp. 280-281). Only steady growth in cohort size can permanently lower mortality and the inheritance flow (p. 281).
- People inherit later (average age 30 in the nineteenth century, about 50 in the twenty-first) but wealth ages too, so μ × m falls much less than m (p. 282); μ rose sharply after 1950–1960 (p. 282).
- The dead have always been richer on average than the living in France since 1820, except 1940–1950; the life-cycle theory "cannot explain the observed facts" (pp. 283-284).
- Gifts rose from 20–30 percent of the value of bequests (1870–1970) to more than 80 percent in 2000–2010; including them, μ exceeds 220 percent today (pp. 284-285).
- Wealth aged dramatically in the nineteenth century: octogenarians 53 percent richer than fifty-somethings in 1820, two and a half times richer by 1900–1910, three or four times in Paris (pp. 285-286). This was "a straightforward consequence" of r > g (p. 286).
- Many Belle Époque fortunes were made by entrepreneurs, but "the entrepreneur always tends to turn into a rentier" under r > g (pp. 286-287).
- The world wars "rejuvenated" wealth because the young recovered faster from shocks; this is "the central explanation" for low postwar inheritance flows (pp. 287-288).
- Private wealth fell by more than two-thirds between 1910–1920 and 1950–1960 (seven to 2–2.5 years of national income); the inheritance flow fell by almost five-sixths (p. 288).
- Simulations: central scenario (g = 1.7 percent, net r = 3 percent) gives a flow of 16–17 percent of national income from 2030–2040; alternative (g = 1 percent, r = 5 percent) gives 24–25 percent from 2060–2070 (p. 289). These are "highly uncertain" and illustrative (p. 289).
- Savings behaviour assumed: savings rates rise with income and initial endowment but vary little by age; the elderly do not dissave massively (p. 290). The high concentration of wealth is the "missing link" Modigliani overlooks (p. 290).
- For a large class of savings behaviours, when g is low relative to r, μ × m is almost independent of life expectancy and set by generation length; 1 percent growth is close to zero growth in this respect (p. 291).
- Inherited wealth was 80–90 percent of private wealth in the nineteenth century; 10–20 percent of the wealthy are self-made in all societies (p. 291). It fell to just over 40 percent in the 1970s, regained a majority in the 1980s, was about two-thirds in 2010, and may approach 80 percent in the 2030s or 90 percent by the 2050s in the alternative scenario (p. 292).
- The inheritance flow exceeded the savings rate (about 10 percent) again in the 1980s (p. 293). Measured against disposable income it is about 20 percent today, as in 1820–1910 (p. 293).
- Inheritance was about 24–25 percent of the lifetime resources of nineteenth-century cohorts, 8–10 percent for those born 1910–1920, and 22–24 percent for those born 1970–1980 (pp. 294-295).
- Nineteenth-century top-centile heirs had resources 25–30 times the lower-class standard, top-centile earners about ten times (p. 295). For cohorts born 1910–1920 the order was reversed (about 5 against 10–12) (pp. 295-296), and for baby boomers too (6–7 against 10–12) (p. 296). For cohorts born in the 1970s top heirs and top earners are roughly equal (12–13 against 10–11) (p. 297).
- Rentier dominance needs two conditions: a large stock of mostly inherited capital (β of 6–7) and extreme concentration of inherited wealth (p. 297). Nineteenth century: a 10:1 concentration ratio outweighed a 3:1 volume ratio, making top heirs about three times better off (pp. 297-298). Today a 3:1 concentration ratio about balances it (p. 298). The "natural" structure, with low g and r well above g, favours rentiers over managers (p. 298).
- Balzac and Austen place the threshold of a dignified life at about 30 times average income, reachable only through the top 1 percent of fortunes, never through the best-paid professions (pp. 298-299).
- In a poor society, extreme inequality could be presented as a condition of civilisation; that view "deserves credit for not describing itself as meritocratic" (pp. 301-303).
- "Modern meritocratic society, especially in the United States, is much harder on the losers" (p. 303).
- Meritocratic justification has long served very high pay, from Napoleon's top civil servants to US supermanagers; the future may combine "the worst of two past worlds" (pp. 303-304).
- Inheritance is now about a quarter of lifetime resources for cohorts born in the 1970s and after (central scenario), possibly a third to four-tenths in the alternative (p. 304).
- Inheritance did not end; its distribution changed, from a few very rich rentiers to many petits rentiers (p. 306).
- About 12 percent of those born 1970–1980, and perhaps 15 percent of those born 2010–2020, inherit more than the bottom half earn in a lifetime (pp. 306-307). This is "a commonplace inequality" that is hard to represent or correct (p. 307).
- Education matters more, but it does not follow that society is more meritocratic, that labour's share has risen, or that opportunity is equal; "there is no evidence that education has really increased intergenerational mobility" (pp. 305-306).
- A return to Belle Époque concentration would cause "significant political upheaval", because democracy rests on a meritocratic hope (p. 307).
- Rent in Piketty's sense is not a market imperfection but the result of a "pure and perfect" capital market; more competition cannot remove it (pp. 308-309). The idea that unrestricted competition will end inheritance is "a dangerous illusion" (p. 309).
- Germany and Britain show a U-curve too (pp. 309-310). Germany: 16 percent (1910), 2 percent (1960), 10–11 percent (2000–2010), with the gap with France "entirely due" to β (p. 310). Britain: 20–25 percent around 1900, about 8 percent (1950–1960), 6 percent (1970–1980), just over 8 percent (2000–2010) (pp. 310-311).
- The weaker British rebound may be a statistical artefact of under-recorded gifts (p. 311).
- In the US the Modigliani versus Kotlikoff-Summers dispute (20–30 against 70–80 percent inherited) is resolved closer to Kotlikoff-Summers: at least 50–60 percent in 1970–1980 (p. 312). Europe–US differences reflect demography, not "eternal cultural differences" (p. 312).
- Inheritance will matter in emerging countries only when their growth slows; for now in China wealth comes mainly from saving (pp. 312-313).

## Arguments

**A. r > g makes inheritance dominate saving (pp. 274, 286-287, 290-291)**

- **Premises**: (p. 274) Wealth from the past grows at r without labour; wealth from work can be saved only from income growing at about g. (p. 286) Elderly owners of large fortunes earning 5 percent and reinvesting three-fifths see wealth grow at 3 percent a year and more than double between 60 and 85. (p. 290) Savings rates rise with wealth and do not fall much with age. (pp. 290-291) When wage growth is 5 percent the young can catch up; at 1–2 percent the elderly "inevitably acquire most of the available assets".
- **Conclusion**: (p. 274) It is "all but inevitable" that inheritance predominates; (p. 287) "the entrepreneur always tends to turn into a rentier".
- **Status**: Piketty labels the link as strong tendency, not logic (p. 274). It depends on the savings-behaviour premise (p. 290), which is described as an empirical regularity for France with variations in individual behaviour (note 18, p. 471).

**B. The b_y = μ × m × β decomposition dissolves the "end of inheritance" (pp. 278-283)**

- **Premises**: (p. 278) By definition b_y = μ × m × β. (pp. 279-280) β fell and recovered (Part Two). (p. 280) m falls with longer life, but (p. 281) the boomer bulge will raise m to 2040–2050. (p. 282) Longer life raises the age of inheritance, but wealth ages too, so μ rises. (p. 283) μ × m was about 1/30 in the nineteenth century, about 2 percent in the 1950s and above 2.5 percent in 2010.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 279) The French U-curve of b_y is the product of three U-curves acting together; (p. 283) longer life changes inheritance "more one of degree" than of civilisation; (p. 291) in low growth μ × m depends almost only on generation length.
- **Comment**: the identity organises the data but cannot by itself explain anything; Piketty says as much ("a useful tautology", p. 278). The explanatory load is carried by the claim that μ rises when m falls (p. 282), which is an empirical and model result (note 22, p. 471: μ × m → 1/H, so b_y → β/H, about 20–25 percent with β of 600–700 percent).

**C. The war rejuvenated wealth; hence the inheritance collapse exceeded the wealth collapse (pp. 287-288)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 287-288) Shocks of 1914–1945 hit all fortunes, but the young had little to lose and time to rebuild; the old did not. (p. 287) In 1947 fifty-somethings were 40 percent richer than octogenarians. (p. 288) So those due to inherit in 1950–1960 received little.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 288) The inheritance flow fell by almost five-sixths while wealth fell by more than two-thirds; (p. 288) reconstruction capitalism was transitional, and as β recovered wealth aged again.
- **Comment**: this is a well-specified mechanism working through μ, and Table 11.1 gives the age profiles that support it.

**D. Rastignac's dilemma and the arithmetic of rentiers and managers (pp. 295-298)**

- **Premises**: (p. 294) Lifetime resources = inheritance + net labour income, capitalised at the average net return. (p. 297) Inheritance is about a quarter of average resources when β is 6–7 and capital is mostly inherited (the "volume" ratio of about 3:1 in favour of labour). (pp. 297-298) The top centile's share of wealth (50–60 percent, 70 in Britain and Paris) was nearly ten times its share of wages (6–7 percent). (p. 298) Today the top centile holds about 20 percent of wealth, about three times its wage share.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 297-298) Top heirs lived about three times better than top earners in the nineteenth century; (p. 298) the two are roughly balanced today; (p. 298) with low growth and r well above g it is "almost inevitable (at least in the most plausible dynamic models)" that top capital incomes dominate.
- **Unstated premises**: that the concentration of *inherited* wealth equals that of *total* wealth (the 50–60 and 20 percent figures are total-wealth shares, p. 297-298); that top heirs and top earners are different people (p. 274-275 says they increasingly overlap); that capitalising at the average return is neutral (Piketty says it slightly understates inheritance, p. 294).

**E. The normative argument: rentier society is incompatible with democracy (pp. 303-309)**

- **Premises**: (p. 307) Democracies proclaim equal rights while living conditions are unequal; to bridge that contradiction inequalities must derive "from rational and universal principles rather than arbitrary contingencies" and be "just and useful to all", citing art. 1 of the 1789 Declaration (p. 307). (pp. 306-307) Inheritance is regaining its nineteenth-century weight in total resources, now spread across a petit-rentier sixth of each cohort. (pp. 308-309) Rent, as income from ownership alone, is produced by a perfect capital market and is not removed by competition. (p. 309) Universal suffrage ended the legal domination of politics by the wealthy but not the economic forces making a rentier society.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 307) A return to extreme concentration would bring "significant political upheaval"; (p. 309) "real democracy and social justice require specific institutions of their own".
- **Unstated premises**: that democratic legitimacy actually depends on inequality looking meritocratic (asserted, supported by Durkheim's 1893 prediction, p. 307, which Piketty concedes in note 55, p. 475, has so far proved no more accurate than rival theories); that inherited inequality is in itself "arbitrary" (never argued at length; taken as shared).

**F. Against meritocratic extremism (pp. 302-306)**

- **Premises**: (p. 303) Nineteenth-century defenders of inequality did not claim the rich were more meritorious. (p. 303) Meritocratic arguments were used to justify civil-service salaries of 50–100 times average income (Napoleon, Thiers, Leroy-Beaulieu). (pp. 303-304) The same argument now defends supermanager pay, with "very little factual basis" (referring back to ch09). (p. 304) Lamont's interviews show the upper middle class explaining its position by personal merit. (pp. 305-306) Education's greater role has not raised labour's share or demonstrably raised mobility.
- **Conclusion**: Modern meritocracy is "much harder on the losers" (p. 303); the future may join large inherited inequality to large merit-justified wage inequality (p. 304).
- **Comment**: Piketty attacks merit both as an ideology (when it justifies supermanager pay) and relies on it as a democratic standard (the meritocratic hope, p. 307). His actual criterion appears to be "common utility" (p. 307), of which merit is one reading. Keeping these apart is essential for synthesis.

**Status of claims: logic, evidence, conjecture**

- *Identity*: b_y = μ × m × β (p. 278); μ × m as a transmission rate (p. 282).
- *Historical evidence (France)*: the flows, μ, wealth-age profiles, gifts, share of inherited wealth, cohort resources (pp. 275-297).
- *Model results*: μ × m independent of life expectancy in low growth (p. 291; note 22); the simulated 1820–2010 path matching observations (p. 291; note 21); the claim that rentier dominance is "almost inevitable" in "the most plausible dynamic models" (p. 298; note 35, pp. 472-473, online only).
- *Scenario/conjecture*: 2010–2100 flows and shares (pp. 289, 292, 304); a return to Belle Époque concentration (p. 307); political upheaval (p. 307); inheritance in emerging countries (p. 313).
- *Normative*: the meritocratic hope and "common utility" standard (p. 307); the charge that meritocratic extremism is dangerous (p. 304).
- *Admitted uncertainty*: Britain gift data (p. 311); US sources "even more difficult" (p. 311); the reasons for the rise in gifts (p. 285: "most convincing explanation", not proven).

## Evidence offered

**French estate data (core)**

- **Inheritance flows 1820–2010** (Figure 11.1, pp. 275-276), by decade (note 2, p. 469); sources and methods in Piketty, *On the Long-Run Evolution of Inheritance: France 1820–2050* (PSE 2010; *QJE* 2011). Two estimates, fiscal and economic (p. 277). Fiscal corrections for small estates and life insurance, now nearly one-sixth of private wealth (p. 277).
- **Mortality** (Figure 11.2, p. 280) and **ages at death and inheritance** (Figure 11.3, p. 282), from French demographic series and official forecasts (pp. 280-281; note 9, p. 470).
- **μ series** (Figure 11.5, p. 283) with and without gifts; **gift/bequest ratio** (p. 284).
- **Wealth-age profiles, 1820–2010** (Table 11.1, pp. 285-288), with a "fairly large correction for differential mortality" (note 15, p. 470).
- **Share of inherited wealth** (Figure 11.7, p. 292), estimated by summing bequests and gifts over the previous thirty years (p. 291), with capitalisation rules discussed in note 23 (p. 471).
- **Cohort resources and Rastignac's dilemma** (Figures 11.9–11.10, pp. 293-296), combining inheritance distributions with labour-income distributions (pp. 294-295); **fraction of each cohort inheriting more than a bottom-half lifetime income** (Figure 11.11, pp. 306-307).
- **Simulations 2010–2100** (Figure 11.6, p. 289) from a model "which successfully accounts for the evolutions of 1820–2010" (p. 289); technical description online only (note 21, p. 471).

**Other countries**

- **Germany**: Christoph Schinke's PSE master's thesis (2012), mortality-multiplier estimates for a limited number of years (p. 310; note 59, pp. 475-476).
- **Britain**: Anthony Atkinson (LSE 2012), a fiscal not economic flow, which note 60 calls "likely to be slightly too low" (p. 476); possible under-recording of gifts (p. 311).
- **United States**: estate tax applies to under 2 percent of estates; surveys underreport bequests (in France surveys capture barely half the fiscal flow) (pp. 311-312). The 50–60 percent estimate for 1970–1980 is Piketty's re-examination of Modigliani and Kotlikoff-Summers (p. 312; note 63, p. 476: Modigliani omitted capitalised income, Kotlikoff-Summers capitalised without limit).

**Literary material**

- Balzac (*Père Goriot*, *César Birotteau*), Austen (*Sense and Sensibility*), Henry James (*Washington Square*), Welles (*The Magnificent Ambersons*), Arthur Young's travels, used to calibrate the "30 times average income" threshold and to show monetary markers (pp. 298-303). Piketty converts sums to multiples of average income (500 francs, about 30 pounds) using Chapter 2's stable money (pp. 298-300; notes 38-39, p. 473). Purchasing-power comparison: average income in 1800 about one-tenth of 2010 (p. 302).
- Contemporary TV series (*House*, *Bones*, *West Wing*, *Damages*, *Dirty Sexy Money*) as evidence of changed social representation (p. 305). Illustrative only.

**Sociological and political evidence**

- Lamont's interviews of the French and American upper middle class, late 1980s (p. 304; note 49, p. 474).
- Thiers (1831) and Leroy-Beaulieu (1881) on civil-service pay (p. 303; notes 46-47, p. 474).
- Draghi's "We must fight against rents" (*Le Monde*, 2012) as an example of the pejorative usage (p. 308; note 56, p. 475).
- Durkheim, *De la division du travail social* (1893) (p. 307; note 55, p. 475).

**Weak points in the evidence**

- The "totally independent" claim (p. 277) is overstated (my observation): the economic flow needs μ, and μ comes from wealth-at-death versus wealth-of-the-living profiles that are themselves built from estate records with mortality-multiplier corrections (Table 11.1, note 15). β comes from national accounts. The two flows share the probate source for μ, so their agreement is partly built in.
- The simulation is said to "successfully account" for 1820–2010 (p. 289) and to make observed and simulated series "so close" (p. 291); note 21 says the model reproduces the age profile "on the basis of macroeconomic and demographic data" (p. 471). Whether parameters were fitted to the same period cannot be told from the book.
- Nearly all quantitative claims are French. The European extension rests on a master's thesis for Germany and on fiscal flows for Britain (pp. 309-311); the US numbers are a reinterpretation of 1960s–1970s data (p. 312).
- The Rastignac figures depend on capitalising all resources at the average net return (p. 294), which Piketty admits slightly understates inheritance because heirs earn higher returns (p. 294; note 26, pp. 471-472).
- Text slips: "eight to eighty-five years" (p. 282) should be eighty to eighty-five; "morality rate" (p. 283) for "mortality rate". The parenthesis "this group largely coincides with the half of the population that inherits next to nothing" (p. 307) seems to refer to the bottom-half earners, not the inheriting sixth; read as such it says the bottom half by earnings and the bottom half by inheritance largely overlap.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch10**: r > g, the inheritance society (ch10 p. 254), and the 1914–1945 shocks are taken from ch10; ch11 delivers ch10's promise that most nineteenth-century wealth was inherited (ch10 p. 255).
- **Depends on ch05 and ch03**: β's U-curve and the fall of private wealth from seven to 2–2.5 years of income (p. 288, citing Figure 3.6).
- **Depends on ch07-ch09**: Vautrin's lecture (ch07), the stable top-centile wage share of 6–7 percent (p. 296), and the claim that supermanager pay has "very little factual basis" (p. 304, from ch09).
- **Feeds ch12**: unequal returns by wealth (p. 294, note 26; p. 297 "as much as a third") would raise inheritance's share; ch11 holds the return equal.
- **Feeds ch13-ch15**: pensions as "the fortunes of those who have no fortune" (note 12, p. 470); education and mobility (note 53, p. 475); labour taxed at about 30 percent against an effective inheritance tax under 5 percent (note 50, pp. 474-475), the basis for ch14-ch15's tax proposals.
- **Definitional shift on "rent" (my observation)**: Piketty's rent is all pure ownership income (p. 308); the economists' rent is above-competitive return. His claim that rent "has absolutely nothing to do with the problem of imperfect competition" (p. 308) is true under his definition. The disagreement with Draghi is partly verbal; the substantive claim is that ordinary competitive returns, compounded with r > g, are enough to produce rentier dominance.
- **Merit as standard and as ideology (my observation)**: the meritocratic hope is a democratic necessity (p. 307), yet "meritocratic extremism" is dangerous (p. 304), and the nineteenth-century elites are praised for not pretending to merit (p. 303). The coherent reading is that the standard is "common utility" (p. 307), with merit-based justifications acceptable only when they are factually true. Synthesis should state that this is a reading, since Piketty does not spell out his theory of justice here.
- **Entrepreneurs and rentiers (my observation)**: p. 287 concedes many Belle Époque fortunes were made by entrepreneurs; the charge is that r > g turns them into rentiers over time. So the inequality Piketty calls arbitrary is not the origin of fortunes but their compounding and transmission. This matters for any comparison with authors who defend the initial earnings.
- **"Inevitable" versus "it could be otherwise"**: compare "all but inevitable" and "in strict logic, it could be otherwise" (p. 274) with "almost inevitable (at least in the most plausible dynamic models)" (p. 298). The hedges are consistent; the strength comes from models not printed in the book.
- **Tension with ch10 on causes of non-recovery**: ch10 gives taxation as the main reason concentration has not returned (ch10 p. 272); ch11 explains the low postwar inheritance flow by rejuvenation of wealth and high growth (pp. 288, 290-291), with taxation mentioned mainly in the 2010–2100 scenarios (p. 289) and note 50. The two accounts are complementary (stock concentration versus flow volume) but synthesis should keep them distinct.
- **Two kinds of inheritance inequality**: volume (inheritance as a share of resources, now back near nineteenth-century levels, p. 304) versus concentration (top-centile share, far below, p. 304). The petits-rentiers argument (p. 306) is about the first; the democracy argument (p. 307) mostly about a possible return of the second.

## Open questions

- How independent are the fiscal and economic flows really (p. 277), given that μ comes from estate records? Does the online appendix estimate μ from any non-probate source (e.g. wealth-tax or survey data on the living)?
- Was the simulation model (p. 289; note 21) calibrated on the 1820–2010 data it is said to match? If so, "successfully accounts for" is a fit, not a test.
- The Rastignac comparison uses total-wealth concentration for inherited-wealth concentration (pp. 297-298) and assumes top heirs and top earners are different people. How much does the growing overlap (pp. 274-275) change the "roughly balanced" conclusion for today?
- Is the claim that the elderly do not dissave (p. 290) robust outside France, especially where annuitised pensions are larger (15–20 percent in English-speaking countries, p. 284)?
- What is the evidence behind "no evidence that education has really increased intergenerational mobility" (p. 306)? Note 53 defers to ch13; check there.
- The democratic-legitimacy claim (p. 307) is a political-sociology thesis supported by the 1789 Declaration and Durkheim. What evidence, if any, does ch13 or the conclusion give that rentier inequality causes upheaval?
- Britain's low rebound (pp. 310-311): data artefact or real behaviour? Piketty cannot say; a cross-book theme on inheritance should not treat the British figure as settled.
- How do the petits-rentiers figures (12–15 percent of a cohort, pp. 306-307) depend on the 750,000-euro threshold (fifty years at 15,000 euros, p. 306), which counts retirement years as earning years?
