# ch02: Growth: Illusions and Realities (pp. 55–82)

## Question this chapter answers

How fast has the world economy grown since antiquity, split into population growth and growth of output per head, and how fast is it likely to grow in the twenty-first century? Why does the answer matter for inequality? Piketty's answer: growth has "always been relatively slow except in exceptional periods or when catch-up is occurring" (p. 55); at the technological frontier, per capita growth has never exceeded about 1.5% for long; population growth is heading back toward zero; so g will likely fall, which strengthens inherited wealth and makes r > g more likely. The chapter also argues that 1% growth is fast in generational terms, that single price indices mislead over long periods, and that the stable money of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, ended by World War I, underpinned the rentier society.

## Leading propositions

- The catch-up of emerging countries is under way and is not primarily due to rich-country investment; the twenty-first century may return to a low-growth regime (p. 55).
- Growth must be split into population growth and per capita growth; around 2013, world growth above 3% meant per capita growth of barely 2% because population still grew about 1% (pp. 55-56).
- From 1700 to 2012 world output grew 1.6% a year, half from population (0.8%) and half from output per head (0.8%) (p. 56).
- Before 1700 growth was below 0.1% a year in both components; whatever the uncertainty, it cannot have exceeded 0.1–0.2% without implying an implausibly small population in year 0 or living standards below subsistence (pp. 56-57).
- Growth above 1–1.5% a year cannot be sustained indefinitely without vertiginous population increases (p. 57).
- 1% a year looks like stagnation year to year but transforms society over a generation (p. 57).
- The central thesis of the book is that a small gap between r and g can, over time, powerfully destabilise the structure of inequality (p. 58).
- World population growth went from about 0.4% (eighteenth century) and 0.6% (nineteenth) to 1.4% in the twentieth, with a record 1.9% in 1950–1970 (pp. 58-59). The UN central forecast has it falling to 0.4% by the 2030s and 0.1% in the 2070s (p. 59).
- Population growth after 2050 will come entirely from Africa; Europe and Asia will likely shrink, which would be unprecedented in peacetime (pp. 59-60).
- Fertility decisions are largely unpredictable; the UN forecast is only a "central scenario" (pp. 60-61), though for Europe and Asia over the next decades it is "the most reasonable forecast" (p. 62).
- Strong population growth weakens inherited wealth, as with immigration in America; very fast per capita growth does the same (pp. 62-63).
- A stagnant or falling population, or a stagnant economy, raises the influence of capital from past generations; with low growth, r is plausibly well above g (p. 63).
- Inheritance-dominated societies "can arise and subsist only in low-growth regimes"; inherited wealth will make a comeback, already visible in Europe (p. 63).
- Growth also raises social mobility by creating new occupations, but this is "less important and more ambiguous" and need not reduce income inequality (pp. 63-64).
- The idea that modern growth rewards natural talent has long been used to justify inequality (Dunoyer, 1845), often without checking facts (p. 64).
- World per capita income rose from under 70 euros a month in 1700 to 760 in 2012, about tenfold; in rich countries from about 100 to over 2,500, more than twentyfold (pp. 64-65, 70).
- Per capita growth was barely 0.1% in the eighteenth century, 0.9% in the nineteenth and 1.6% in the twentieth worldwide; Western Europe 0.2%, 1.1% and 1.9% (p. 65).
- Growth in purchasing power is mainly a change in the structure of consumption, not "more of the same"; long-run average price indices mislead (pp. 65-66).
- Purchasing power rose sixfold for carrots (France, 1900–2010), tenfold for oranges, twentyfold for bananas, less than fourfold for bread or meat, fortyfold for bicycles (1890–1970), and not at all for haircuts (pp. 66-68).
- A single index of living standards across centuries is "futile and reductive", but growth is real; generational growth rates (3%, 35%, 143% per generation for 0.1%, 1%, 3% a year) keep their meaning (pp. 67-68).
- Services are now 70–80% of employment in rich countries and too diverse to be a meaningful category: health and education about 20%, retail/hotels/leisure about 20%, business, financial, real-estate and transport services about 20%, government and security nearly 10% (pp. 68-69).
- National accounts value free public services at cost, which is consistent but has biases: private insurance that costs more for the same quality inflates GDP, and privatisation would raise GDP artificially because public capital earns no imputed return (pp. 69-70).
- Past growth rarely exceeded 1–1.5% a year; faster growth (3–4%) only came with catch-up (p. 70). Between 1990 and 2012 per capita growth was 1.6% in Western Europe, 1.4% in North America and 0.7% in Japan (pp. 70-71).
- Piketty refuses to predict growth but assumes 1.2% per capita for rich countries, more optimistic than Robert Gordon's projection of below 0.5% in 2050–2100; even this requires new energy sources (p. 71).
- 1% per capita growth is fast: over thirty years it changes lifestyles and employment, and implies a quarter to a third of today's output and jobs did not exist thirty years ago (pp. 71-72).
- Growth can create new fortunes but makes inherited wealth less decisive; growth of 1% cannot meet democratic and meritocratic hopes, which need specific institutions (p. 72).
- The Trente Glorieuses were catch-up after Europe fell behind in 1914–1945; North America never had them, growing 1.5–2% a year throughout 1820–2012 (pp. 72-73).
- Neither post-1945 state intervention nor post-1980 liberalisation deserves praise or blame for growth; catch-up would have happened anyway, and liberalisation "neither increased growth nor decreased it" (p. 74).
- Under the median scenario, global per capita growth exceeds 2.5% in 2012–2050, then falls toward 1.2%; global output growth falls to about 1.5% in the second half of the century (pp. 75-76).
- The median scenario is optimistic in two ways (continued frontier productivity growth and unbroken convergence to 2050); "the two bell curves of global growth are in large part already determined" (p. 76).
- Inflation matters for the wealth distribution: it eliminated the post-war public debt, redistributed chaotically in the twentieth century, and its absence underpinned the nineteenth-century wealth society (p. 77).
- Before 1914 inflation was zero or close to it (at most 0.2–0.3% a year in 1700–1820 and 1820–1913); the assignats were a short exception (pp. 77-78).
- The franc germinal kept the old silver parity; the same parity held 1726–1914; a pound was worth 20–25 francs for two centuries (pp. 77-78).
- Stable money and slow growth gave novels fixed monetary markers: Austen's characters needed 500–1,000 pounds a year (20–30 times the average of about 30 pounds), Balzac's 10–20,000 francs (vs an average of 400–500) (pp. 79-80).
- World War I ended this; inflation in 1913–1950 was over 13% a year in France (prices ×100), 17% in Germany (×300), about 3% in Britain and the US (×3) (pp. 80-81).
- Even 2% inflation is very different from zero; specific sums of money vanished from literature after 1914–1945 (pp. 81-82).

## Arguments

**A. Long-run growth must have been slow (pp. 56-58)**

- **Premises**: (p. 58) World population around 1700 was about 600 million; in year 0 it was over 200 million, 50 million in the Roman Empire alone. (p. 57) Per capita output cannot have been far below subsistence in year 0.
- **Arithmetic**: (p. 58) At 0.2% a year over 1,700 years, population multiplies by about 30 (1.002^1700 ≈ 29.9), implying 20 million in year 0, too low. At 0.8% the multiplier would be 1.008^1700. Piketty says "nearly 100,000", which would imply fewer than ten thousand people in year 0. The exact figure is about 760,000, which gives under 1,000 people; the conclusion is stronger than stated, but the multiplier in the text is understated by a factor of about eight.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 58) Pre-1700 population growth was "less than 0.2 percent and almost certainly less than 0.1 percent".
- **Extension**: (p. 57) "For the same reason" growth in coming centuries is likely to return to very low levels, at least for population. This is an inference from finite resources and plausibility (70 billion people by 2300 at 0.8%, pp. 57, 62), not from data.

**B. Low growth strengthens inherited wealth (pp. 62-63)**

- **Premises**: (p. 62) With many children per couple, family wealth is divided at each generation. (pp. 62-63) With mass immigration of people without wealth, inherited wealth is small relative to new saving. (p. 63) With per capita output growing tenfold a generation, parents' wealth is small relative to current income.
- **Mirror premise**: (p. 63) Stagnant or falling population or output raises the weight of capital accumulated by earlier generations, and with low growth r is likely to exceed g by a wide margin.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 63) Capital-dominated, inheritance-based hierarchies arise only in low-growth regimes, and inherited wealth will make a comeback.
- **Link**: The formal version is β = s / g (fm01, p. 29; derived in ch05) plus the inheritance flow analysis (ch11). Here the claim is intuitive. The step that with low growth "it is fairly plausible that" r will be well above g (p. 63) is the key unstated premise: it requires r not to fall as much as g, which ch06 (elasticity of substitution) and ch10 (history of r) must establish.

**C. Frontier growth is capped at about 1.5% (pp. 70-71)**

- **Premises**: (p. 70) World per capita growth 1700–2012 averaged 0.8%; the twentieth century 1.6%. (p. 70) Europe 1.9% in 1913–2012 included catch-up; America 1.5% in both 1820–1913 and 1913–2012. (pp. 70-71) Frontier countries grew 0.7–1.6% per capita in 1990–2012.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 70) "there is no historical example of a country at the world technological frontier whose growth in per capita output exceeded 1.5 percent over a lengthy period of time".
- **Assessment**: The evidence is essentially North American growth since 1820 (p. 73) and a few decades of Europe and Japan. That is a sample of about one sustained frontier economy, so the cap is an empirical regularity, not a law. Piketty's own median assumption of 1.2% for rich countries (p. 71) sits inside the cap. Note a mismatch of aggregates: p. 65 gives Western Europe 1.1% for the nineteenth century while p. 70 gives Europe 1.0% for 1820–1913; these are different regions and periods, not a contradiction, but readers comparing tables should keep them apart.

**D. A quarter to a third of jobs are new each generation (p. 72)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 71-72) Per capita output rose 35–50% in thirty years. (p. 72) Communication, transport, health and education changed profoundly since 1980.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 72) Between a quarter and a third of today's output and occupations "did not exist thirty years ago".
- **Assessment**: The arithmetic is 1 − 1/1.35 ≈ 26% and 1 − 1/1.5 ≈ 33%. But the step from "output is 35–50% higher" to "that share of output is new" assumes all growth takes the form of new goods and none takes the form of more or cheaper existing goods. It is a vivid illustration, not a derivation.

**E. Policy did not drive post-war growth differences (pp. 72-74)**

- **Premises**: (p. 73) Western Europe stagnated in 1913–1950 (just over 0.5%), grew over 4% in 1950–1970, then fell to about 2% and 1.5%. (p. 73) North America grew a fairly steady 1.5–2%. (p. 74) Once at the frontier, rich countries' growth rates equalised.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 74) Catch-up would have happened "regardless of what policies they had adopted (I say this with only slight exaggeration)"; "The most one can say is that state intervention did no harm"; liberalisation neither raised nor lowered growth.
- **Assessment**: This is a claim from one figure (Figure 2.3) without a counterfactual model. It matters for the book because it frees Piketty's later policy proposals (progressive taxes, capital tax, ch13–ch15) from the charge that they would cost growth: if growth at the frontier is set by technology, redistribution has little growth cost. That is a convenient result and deserves more evidence than the chapter gives.

## Evidence offered

- **Table 2.1 (p. 56).** World growth 1700–2012: output 1.6%, population 0.8%, per capita 0.8%. Year 0–1700: population 0.06%, per capita 0.02% (p. 56). Source: Maddison (p. 56); detail in Supplemental Table S2.1 (endnote 1, p. 440). Piketty: "the precision of such estimates is illusory" (p. 56).
- **Table 2.2 (p. 57).** Growth multipliers. 1% a year: ×1.35 after 30 years, ×3 after 100 (more exactly 2.7), ×20 after 300, over 20,000 after 1,000. These check: 1.01^300 ≈ 19.8, 1.01^1000 ≈ 20,959.
- **Table 2.3 and Figure 2.2 (pp. 58-60).** Population growth by continent and period; the 1950–1970 peak of 1.9%; UN central forecast to 2100. Endnote 3 (p. 440): adult population grew 1.9% in 1990–2012 as the adult share rose from 57% to 65%. Endnote 7 (p. 440): UN scenarios for 2070–2100 range from −1.0% to +1.2%, so long-range forecasts are highly uncertain.
- **Country population examples (pp. 59-61).** Egypt from 10 to 80 million; Nigeria and Pakistan from 20 to 160 million; US from under 3 million (1780) to over 300 million (2010); Western Europe 410m vs North America 350m in 2010, 430m vs 450m forecast for 2050; China vs India.
- **Price examples (pp. 66-68).** Carrots, dairy, oranges, bananas, bread, meat, electronics, bicycles, haircuts in France. Source: Piketty's own *Les Hauts revenus en France au 20e siècle* (2001), drawing on official indices and Jean Fourastié's price compilations (endnotes 13, 15, 16, p. 441). The bicycle quotes come from catalogues (p. 67).
- **Employment by sector (Table 2.4, pp. 68-69).** Sources: Piketty's own 1997 and 1998 papers on services in France and the US (endnote 17, p. 441).
- **Table 2.5 and Figure 2.3 (pp. 70-73).** Per capita growth by continent; Western Europe vs North America 1820–2012. Endnote 21 (p. 442): 2007–2012 growth was close to zero because of the recession, which pulls down the 1990–2012 averages.
- **Robert Gordon (p. 71).** *Is U.S. Economic Growth Over?*, NBER Working Paper 18315 (2012) (endnote 22, p. 442): per capita growth could fall below 0.5% in 2050–2100.
- **Figures 2.4–2.5 (pp. 75-76).** World per capita and total output growth from antiquity to 2100 under the median scenario. China above 9% a year in 1990–2012 "according to official statistics" (p. 75). Endnote 24 (p. 442): world per capita growth 2.1% in 1990–2012, 1.5% per adult; total output growth 3.4%. The note's wording ("demographic growth rose from 1.3 to 1.9 percent") seems to confuse total and adult population growth.
- **Inflation (Figure 2.6, pp. 77-81).** Four countries 1700–2012. Pre-1914 at most 0.2–0.3% a year; Britain and US −0.2% in 1820–1913 (p. 77); 1913–1950 France 13%, Germany 17%, UK/US 3% (p. 80); 1970–1990 Britain 10%, France 8% (p. 80); 1990–2012 about 2% (pp. 80-81). The price multipliers check: 1.13^37 ≈ 92, 1.17^37 ≈ 333, 1.03^37 ≈ 3.
- **Monetary law (pp. 77-78).** Law of 18 germinal Year III (1795), laws of 1796 and 1803; 4.5 g of fine silver; endnote 27 adds 0.29 g of gold, ratio 1/15.5 (p. 442). Endnote 28: under the gold standard (1816–1914) a pound was 7.3 g of fine gold, 25.2 times the franc (p. 442).
- **Literary evidence (pp. 79-82).** Austen (average income about 30 pounds), Balzac's *Père Goriot* (400–500 francs average), Mahfouz, Pamuk's *Snow*. Endnote 30: Austen-era figures are national income per adult, not per head (p. 443). The literary evidence illustrates monetary stability; it is not evidence of the income figures, which come from the online appendix.
- **Dunoyer (p. 64).** *De la liberté du travail* (1845), quoted via Rosanvallon, *The Society of Equals* (endnote 8, pp. 440-441).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **g is total growth, and its fall is mostly demographic.** The forecast drop in global output growth from about 3.5% to about 1.5% (p. 76) comes largely from population (to near 0%, p. 59) while per capita growth stays above 1% (p. 75). The book's r > g story for the twenty-first century therefore leans heavily on the demographic forecast, which Piketty himself calls unpredictable (p. 60). See ch10 (Figures 10.9–10.10) where these g values meet r.
- **Growth equalises, but only weakly and ambiguously.** The chapter offers two growth-based equalisers: dilution of inheritance (pp. 62-63) and mobility from new occupations (pp. 63-64). It then says growth also creates new fortunes (p. 72). The net effect depends on β = s / g (ch05) and the inheritance flow (ch11).
- **Policy irrelevance for growth vs policy decisiveness for distribution.** ch02 says policy hardly affected growth (p. 74), while fm01 says politics drove the twentieth-century distribution (p. 19). These are consistent only if distribution can be changed without affecting growth, which is the premise of Part Four. ch14's argument about top tax rates and growth (US vs other countries) returns to this.
- **The Trente Glorieuses as catch-up (to ch03–ch05).** Fast catch-up growth (high g) after 1945 is part of why β stayed low in Europe until the 1970s. The same g appears in β = s / g, so the catch-up story here is doing work later in explaining the U-curve of Figure I.2.
- **Inflation and public debt (to ch03, ch16).** The claim that inflation removed post-war public debt (p. 77) and that stable money supported the rentier society (p. 77) is developed in ch03 (public debt in Britain and France) and ch16.
- **Price indices.** Piketty insists long-run price indices are arbitrary (pp. 65-67, 76), yet the whole book uses real growth rates and real returns computed with them. r in r > g is real; if inflation is overstated, real g is understated (p. 77) and the r − g gap is affected. The chapter flags the problem but does not resolve it.
- **Measurement of public services (to ch06).** Valuing public services at cost with zero return on public capital (pp. 69-70) biases GDP comparisons between countries with public and private provision; endnote 19 says ch06 returns to the size of this bias (p. 441). It also affects α across countries.
- **Literature as data.** Novels are used again in ch03, ch07 and ch11 (Austen, Balzac, Vautrin's lesson). Here they show that monetary amounts carried social meaning under stable money (pp. 79-80); the stronger claim that such sums let a novelist "describe a civilization" (p. 80) is illustrative, not evidential.

## Open questions

- How sensitive are the r > g projections in ch10 to the UN central population scenario, given the wide range between low and high scenarios (endnote 7, p. 440)?
- Is the 1.5% frontier cap (p. 70) robust if one uses per hour growth, given that hours per head fell by about half over the long run (endnote 11, p. 441)? Productivity growth has been roughly twice per capita growth.
- What evidence would distinguish the claim that catch-up would have happened regardless of policy (p. 74) from the alternative that post-war institutions sped catch-up?
- If long-run price indices are partly arbitrary (p. 76), how much uncertainty does that add to historical real returns r, and thus to the size of r − g?
- The "nearly 100,000" multiplier (p. 58) is understated by about eight times; does the online appendix use the correct figure, and are there other arithmetic slips in the long-run tables?
