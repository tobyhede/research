# ch06: The Capital-Labor Split in the Twenty-First Century (pp. 142–170)

## Question this chapter answers

Given β (fixed in the long run by s/g, ch05), what determines the rate of return r and so capital's share α = r × β, and what will happen to α as β rises in the twenty-first century? Piketty's answer: the pure return on capital has been roughly stable at 4–5 percent for three centuries, falling slightly to 3–4 percent today; α has followed the same U-curve as β, only shallower; that pattern means the long-run elasticity of substitution between capital and labor has been above one (he puts it at 1.3–1.6); so a rising β in a low-growth world will probably raise α, and "no self-corrective mechanism" stops it (p. 161).

## Leading propositions

- α moves "transparently" from β via α = r × β, so the central question becomes how r is determined (p. 143).
- In Britain and France α was 35–40 percent in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, 20–25 percent in the mid-twentieth, and 25–30 percent in the late twentieth and early twenty-first; the matching average return was 5–6, 7–8, then 4–5 percent (p. 143).
- α traces the same U-curve as β but a shallower one: r is higher when β is lower, "which seems natural" (p. 143).
- Returns vary by asset and by size of fortune, and the variation tends to amplify inequality (pp. 143-144).
- Estimates of capital stocks are more reliable than estimates of income flows, both historically and today; hence the book's emphasis on β rather than on the capital-labor split (p. 146).
- The pure return on capital has oscillated around 4–5 percent (range 3–6) in France and Britain from the eighteenth century to the twenty-first with no pronounced trend, though it may have slipped to 3–4 percent today, a fall of about one-quarter to one-fifth (p. 147).
- Nineteenth-century novels take the 5 percent conversion of capital into rent for granted; higher returns on active investments were largely pay for labor (pp. 147-148).
- Taxes (average around 30 percent on capital income today) are the main reason for the gap between the pure pretax return and what owners receive (p. 149).
- Low-yield deposits are a small share of total wealth and barely affect the average return; housing yields 3–4 percent and financial assets more (pp. 149-150).
- Inflation mainly redistributes wealth between asset categories and among owners; its effect on the average return is "fairly limited" (pp. 151-152).
- r is set by two forces: technology (what capital is used for) and abundance of capital (p. 153).
- Diminishing marginal productivity is obvious; the question is how fast r falls when β rises (p. 155).
- If r falls less than proportionally as β rises, α rises; British and French history suggests this second case holds in the long run (pp. 155-156).
- Both cases are theoretically possible; which holds depends on the elasticity of substitution (p. 156), and the relevant line is σ = 1 (p. 157).
- The Cobb-Douglas assumption of a stable α became popular partly because it gives "a fairly peaceful and harmonious view of the social order" (p. 158), but a stable α would not imply harmony or a stable β (p. 158).
- Stable-share claims (Bowley 1920, Cobb-Douglas 1928, Keynes 1939) rested on short periods; the debate was politicized on both sides (pp. 158-159).
- The book claims to be the first to set the capital-labor split in the long-run context of β since the eighteenth century (p. 159).
- Over the very long run σ seems to have been greater than one, because capital keeps finding new uses (p. 160); historical data give 1.3–1.6, but future technology need not match the past (p. 160).
- The 1970–2010 rise in α in rich countries is consistent both with σ > 1 and with stronger bargaining power of capital (p. 160); "no self-corrective mechanism" prevents β and α rising together (p. 161).
- In traditional agricultural societies, where capital was mostly land, σ was "significantly less than one"; this is the only explanation for low land values in land-rich America (p. 161).
- The long-run fall in α from 35–40 to 25–30 percent can be read as technology favouring human capital, but it is not "a change of civilization", and α could return to early-nineteenth-century levels through rising β even if technology barely changes (pp. 161-162).
- Medium-term swings are large: α rose about 10 points in Britain 1800–1860 (Engels' pause), then fell 1870–1900 (p. 163); French profits rose 1945–1968, fell 1968–1983, rose after 1983, and housing rent's share rose steadily from 1945 (pp. 163-164).
- Marx's falling rate of profit corresponds to a real logical difficulty when g ≈ 0, and structural growth is the "only logical exit" (pp. 164-165); Marx's use of statistics was anecdotal and ignored contemporary capital-stock estimates (p. 166).
- The Cambridge capital controversy was clouded by lack of long-run data; Solow's substitutable-factor model "definitively carried the day" in the 1970s (pp. 166-168).
- Conclusions for the twenty-first century: low growth brings capital back (p. 168); global β could reach 6–7 at g = 1.5 percent and 10 at 1 percent with s = 10 percent (p. 169); with β of 7–8 and r of 4–5 percent, α could reach 30–40 percent, "and it might rise even higher" (p. 169).
- "there is no natural force that inevitably reduces the importance of capital" (p. 169); modern growth avoided Marx's apocalypse but did not reduce capital's macroeconomic weight relative to labor (p. 170).

## Arguments

**A. The capital share is the product of β and r (p. 143)**

- **Premises**: (p. 143) α = r × β is an accounting identity (from ch01). (p. 142) β is fixed in the long run by s/g, with s and g "largely independent" of each other.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 143) With β known, the question of the split reduces to how r is determined.
- **Note**: the identity holds by construction because r is computed as capital income over capital (p. 144). It carries no causal content; the causal content enters only with Argument C.

**B. The pure return has been roughly constant (pp. 143-148)**

- **Premises**: (p. 144) Observed average returns, computed from national accounts, ran 5–6, 7–8, then 4–5 percent. (p. 146) National accounts omit the informal labor of managing one's own wealth, and that labor is likely larger in fast-growth or high-inflation periods. (p. 147) Deducting an estimate of it lowers returns by 1–2 points. (pp. 147-148) Traditional conversion of capital to rent at 5 (or 4) percent, and novelists' treatment of higher yields on active investments as pay for labor, confirm a pure return of about 4–5 percent.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 147) The pure return has oscillated around 4–5 percent with no pronounced trend, possibly drifting to 3–4 percent.
- **Unstated premise**: that the correction for informal management labor is calibrated independently of the conclusion. It is admitted to be "quite difficult—and to some extent arbitrary" (p. 146), and the correction is largest exactly where observed returns depart most from 4–5 percent (postwar returns near 10 percent, p. 146). The stability of the *pure* return is therefore partly produced by the adjustment.

**C. The elasticity criterion (pp. 155-157), reconstructed**

- **Premises**: (pp. 152-153) Under competition r equals the marginal product of capital. (p. 155) The marginal product falls as β rises. (p. 156) How fast it falls is governed by the elasticity of substitution.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 157) α rises with β if and only if σ > 1; falls if σ < 1; constant at σ = 1 (Cobb-Douglas).
- **Reconstruction (mine; the text relegates the algebra to the online appendix, endnotes 15–17, p. 452)**: with a constant-elasticity production function and r equal to the marginal product, r is proportional to β^(−1/σ) and α = r × β is proportional to β^(1 − 1/σ). So "r falls less than proportionately" (p. 156) is the same statement as 1/σ < 1. Two conditions are built in: r equals the marginal product (Piketty states this assumption each time, p. 157), and β measures a volume of capital along a single production function.
- **Wording**: the English text says that with σ > 1 a rise in β "leads instead to a drop in the marginal productivity of capital, so that" α rises (p. 157). The qualifier that the drop is *small* (less than proportional) is missing; the intended sense is clear from p. 156.

**D. History shows σ > 1 in the long run (pp. 156, 160)**

- **Premises**: (p. 156) In Britain and France α followed the U-curve of β, though shallower; r rose when capital was scarce after 1945 but not enough to invert the U. (p. 160) Capital keeps finding new uses (solar panels, robots, medical technology). (p. 160) Figure 6.5: α rose in most rich countries 1970–2010 as β rose.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 160) Over the very long run σ "seems to have been greater than one", estimated at 1.3–1.6.
- **Concession inside the argument**: (p. 160) the 1970–2010 co-movement is equally consistent with rising bargaining power of capital (capital mobility, tax competition), and the two "have reinforced each other". The data therefore do not separate the elasticity explanation from the institutional one.
- **Evidential status of 1.3–1.6**: endnote 24 says only "See the online technical appendix" (p. 453). No method, sample or standard error is given in the book.

**E. Agricultural societies had σ < 1 (p. 161)**

- **Premises**: In the eighteenth–nineteenth centuries US land values (β and rents) were much lower than Europe's despite far more land. For any single form of capital the price effect eventually outweighs the volume effect.
- **Conclusion**: σ was "significantly less than one" in agrarian economies; capital substitutes readily for labor only when it takes many forms.
- **Note**: this is a cross-sectional comparison (US vs Europe) used to infer a technological parameter. Piketty says it is "the only way to explain" the land-value gap, a strong claim with no alternative (institutions, transport costs, labor scarcity, ch04's treatment of slaves as capital) considered here.

**F. Rising β will probably raise α (pp. 160-162, 168-169)**

- **Premises**: (p. 168) Slower growth, especially demographic, raises β via s/g. (p. 169) Global s ≈ 10 percent and g ≈ 1–1.5 percent give β of 6–10. (p. 169) σ is "probably greater than one". (p. 169) Technological change favouring labor is limited in size and may be offset by financial sophistication and international competition for capital.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 169) α could reach 30–40 percent of global income, near eighteenth–nineteenth-century levels, and might go higher; (pp. 160-161) nothing self-corrects.
- **Check against his own numbers (my calculation)**: take today's rich-country α ≈ 27.5 percent and β ≈ 5.5, so r ≈ 5 percent. With σ in his 1.3–1.6 range, a rise of β to 7–8 lowers r to roughly 4.0–4.3 percent and gives α of about 29–32 percent. The top of his "30 or 40 percent" (β = 8 at r = 5 percent) requires r not to fall at all, which is the infinite-elasticity case he calls irrelevant (p. 157). His own elasticity estimate supports the low end of the range, not 40 percent.

**G. Marx's contradiction is real but avoided by growth (pp. 164-166)**

- **Premises**: (p. 164) Read Marx as β = s/g with g ≈ 0. (p. 165) With s > 0 and g → 0, β → ∞, so either r → 0 or α → 1. (endnote 32, p. 453) r avoids zero only with infinite elasticity. (p. 165) Only productivity and population growth balance accumulation.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 165) The dynamic inconsistency Marx saw corresponds to "a real difficulty"; modern growth avoided the "apocalypse" (p. 170).
- **Interpretive choice**: Piketty concedes Marx's prose was not "limpid" and offers this as "one logically consistent way of interpreting" him (p. 164); the textile-factory accounts showing a capital/output ratio above ten are offered as confirmation (p. 165).

**Position versus argument.** Piketty's claim that the capital share will rise rests on two separable steps: the β forecast (from ch05, s/g) and σ > 1 (this chapter). The second is the hinge. He states it with hedges throughout ("seems", "probably", "apparently"), yet the chapter's summary claims ("no self-corrective mechanism", p. 161; "no natural force", p. 169) are stated without hedges.

## Evidence offered

- **National-accounts capital shares and returns, Britain 1770–2010 and France 1820–2010** (Figures 6.1–6.4, pp. 143-144). Britain's series rest on Robert Allen's historical work; France's on Piketty's own (endnote 4, p. 451). Method: sum of capital incomes regardless of legal form, pretax, excluding public-debt interest, divided by national income (α) or by national capital (r) (p. 144). Upper and lower bounds appear only in online supplemental figures S6.1–S6.2 (endnote 5, p. 451). All "Sources and series" lines point to piketty.pse.ens.fr/capital21c.
- **Mixed income treatment**: apportioned using the economy-wide split; two alternative methods (impute average wage to nonwage workers, or average return to their capital) said to give similar results (p. 145; endnote 2, p. 451). Individually owned businesses fell from 30–40 percent of rich-country output in the 1950s (perhaps 50 percent earlier) to about 10 percent (endnote 3, p. 451). The eighteenth–nineteenth-century capital share is therefore given as an interval, 35–40 percent (p. 146).
- **Cross-check for the early capital share**: landlords' rents alone were 20 percent of national income in Britain and France in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries; land (about half of capital) yielded slightly less than the average (pp. 145-146).
- **Pure-return series**: the observed return minus "a plausible (although perhaps too high)" estimate of informal management costs (p. 147). No method is given in the text.
- **Historical interest rates and conversion conventions**: 20 years' purchase (5 percent), sometimes 25 (4 percent) (pp. 147-148); British and French public-debt rates typically 4–5 percent, 3 percent in the late-nineteenth-century slowdown, 5–6 percent in pre-revolutionary France (Velde and Weir 1992; Béguin 2012; endnote 7, p. 451).
- **Literary evidence**: Balzac and Austen's rent-capital equivalences (50,000 francs from 1 million; 2,000 pounds from 40,000), Père Goriot's pasta factories, Sir Thomas's West Indies plantations in *Mansfield Park*, César Birotteau's Madeleine speculation (p. 148). Used as evidence of what contemporaries took for granted, not as measurement.
- **Present-day composition of wealth**: checking accounts 10–20 percent of national income (3–4 percent of wealth); with savings accounts just above 30 percent of national income (barely over 5 percent of wealth) (pp. 149-150; endnote 9 to the online appendix, p. 451). Housing rental yield 3–4 percent, 5 percent for small apartments (p. 150). Average tax rate on capital income about 30 percent in rich countries (p. 149); French livret A at about 2 percent nominal in 2013 (endnote 8, p. 451).
- **Asset prices vs consumer prices 1910–2010**: roughly equal on average; relative price of capital fell 1910–1950 and rose 1950–2010 (p. 151, relying on ch05). Endnote 12 concedes that the income-flow measure of return includes the capital gain from retained earnings capitalised in share prices (pp. 451-452).
- **Elasticity estimate 1.3–1.6** (p. 160): online appendix only (endnote 24, p. 453).
- **Rich-country capital shares 1975–2010** (Figure 6.5, p. 161): 15–25 percent in 1970, 25–30 percent in 2000–2010 (caption). Countries not listed in the text.
- **France 1900–2010** (Figures 6.6–6.8, p. 164): corporate gross profit share 25 percent (1982) to 33 percent (2010), net 12 to 20 percent; housing rent 2 percent of national income (1948) to 10 percent (2010); capital share (net profits plus rents) 15 percent (1982) to 27 percent (2010). Endnote 30 notes that most studies look only at the wage-profit split and so miss rent (p. 453).
- **Medium-term history**: Robert Allen on Britain's Engels' pause and its explanation (rural exodus plus capital-biased technical change) (p. 163; endnote 26, online appendix, p. 453); Bouvier, Furet and Gilet (1965) on French firms' profit shares (p. 163; endnote 27, p. 453); Simiand, Labrousse and Williamson on pre-revolutionary land rents, with Arthur Young's observations of peasant misery (p. 163; endnote 28, p. 453); Chabert and Postel-Vinay on wage rises 1789–1815 (p. 163; endnote 29, p. 453).
- **History of the stable-share doctrine**: Cobb and Douglas 1928 (US manufacturing 1899–1922) (p. 158; endnote 18, p. 452); Bowley 1920 (Britain 1880–1913, capital share about 37 percent, which Piketty says matches his series; endnote 19, p. 452); Bernstein outvoted at Hanover in 1899 (pp. 158-159); Kuczynski 1937, whom Piketty judges right for the first two-thirds of the nineteenth century and wrong for the whole (p. 159; endnote 20, pp. 452-453); Frederick Brown 1939 in *Economic History Review* (p. 159); Keynes 1939, relying on 1920s British manufacturing data and a "manual labor" share of 40 percent (p. 159; endnote 22, p. 453); OECD and IMF reports of the 2000s (p. 159; endnote 23, online bibliography only).
- **Marx's sources**: textile-factory accounts in *Capital* vol. 1 with a capital/output ratio above ten (p. 165); British parliamentary reports 1820–1860, profit-tax statistics, probate statistics (p. 166); *Wages, Price and Profit* (1865) factory with profits at 50 percent of value added (endnote 33, pp. 453-454). Piketty's charge that Marx ignored capital-stock estimates from Colquhoun (1800–1810) to Giffen (1870s) (p. 166).
- **Cambridge controversy**: Harrod 1939, Domar 1948, Solow 1956; Robinson, Kaldor, Pasinetti; Modigliani (pp. 166-168; endnote 36 on life-cycle vs inheritance views, p. 454). Narrated as intellectual history; no source citations beyond names.
- **Bare assertion or language outrunning evidence**: "the only way to explain" low American land values (p. 161); "no self-corrective mechanism exists" (p. 161); "this is obvious" of diminishing returns (p. 155); the "postcolonial dimension" of the Cambridge debate (p. 168); the novelty claim that this is "to my knowledge the first attempt" (p. 159).

## Concessions and qualifications

- An "average" rate of return is "a fairly abstract construct" (p. 143); the data have "limitations and weaknesses" (p. 143).
- Early capital shares can only be "approximations" because mixed incomes may have been over half of national income (p. 145).
- Valuing informal management labor is "quite difficult—and to some extent arbitrary" (p. 146); pure-return figures are "for illustrative purposes" and "should probably be regarded as minimum values" (p. 147).
- "We nevertheless lack the distance" to be sure the pure return has fallen; a rise is possible (p. 147).
- r may be above or below the marginal product, which "is not always precisely measurable" (p. 153).
- Whether owners should receive the marginal product "is clearly a crucial question, but not the one I am asking here" (p. 155).
- "both cases are theoretically possible. Everything depends on the vagaries of technology" (p. 156).
- Cobb-Douglas is "sometimes a good approximation for certain subperiods or sectors" and "a useful point of departure" (p. 158).
- The historical exercise "admittedly has its limits" given the sources (p. 159).
- The 1.3–1.6 estimate is "uncertain and imprecise", and future technology need not have the same elasticity (p. 160).
- The 1970–2010 rise in α fits rising bargaining power of capital as well as σ > 1 (p. 160).
- On human capital, "a correct answer at this stage is impossible" (p. 162).
- Technology may slightly favour labor over the very long run (p. 169).
- The Cambridge-England side was not wholly wrong: Harrod's instability intuition "was not entirely wrong" (p. 167); American economists "tended at times to exaggerate" balanced growth (p. 168).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch01 and ch05**: α = r × β (ch01) and β = s/g (ch05) are the two laws this chapter joins. The forecast of rising α inherits every uncertainty in ch05's forecast of β (s ≈ 10 percent, g ≈ 1–1.5 percent globally).
- **Market value vs volume (my observation)**: β throughout is capital at market value, and the chapter itself says the relative price of capital fell 1910–1950 and rose 1950–2010 (p. 151). The elasticity criterion (p. 157) is a statement about volumes of capital along a production function. Where β rises because asset prices rise, the co-movement of α and β in Figure 6.5 is not a movement along a production function and says nothing direct about σ. ch03 and ch05 on the relative price of capital should be read against this.
- **Housing carries much of the rise (my observation)**: in France the capital share rose from 15 to 27 percent of national income 1982–2010 (Figure 6.8), while housing rent alone rose from 2 percent (1948) to 10 percent (2010) (Figure 6.7), and Piketty says rent's rise is why α kept climbing after the profit share stabilised (p. 164). An argument from robots and many new uses of capital (p. 160) sits awkwardly with a rise driven largely by the rental value of dwellings.
- **Endpoints vs the U (my observation)**: Europe's β is now "scarcely less" than in the eighteenth–nineteenth centuries (p. 169), yet α is 25–30 rather than 35–40 percent and the pure return 3–4 rather than 4–5 percent (pp. 147, 161). Comparing endpoints, similar β has come with lower r and lower α, which Piketty attributes to a shift in technology toward labor (p. 161), not to σ. So σ > 1 is identified only from the movements within the U: the mid-century trough and the post-1970 rise. The trough was caused by war destruction and asset-price collapse (ch03–ch05), and mid-century returns near 10 percent are said to include unpaid entrepreneurial labor (pp. 146-147). Those are the periods least like a movement along a stable production function.
- **Net vs gross (my observation, from outside the book)**: national income and capital are net of depreciation throughout the book (ch01). The empirical literature on the elasticity of substitution mostly estimates it on gross output and often finds it below one. A net elasticity above one requires less than a gross elasticity above one, but the book does not state which concept its 1.3–1.6 refers to, and the method is in the appendix only (endnote 24, p. 453). For the synthesis, the claim should be recorded as resting on an estimate not shown in the book.
- **Bargaining power as a rival explanation**: conceded on pp. 153 and 160. If institutions rather than technology drive the post-1970 rise, the policy implication shifts toward ch09's institutional account of labor income and ch13–ch15's tax and regulation agenda, which Piketty favours anyway. The concession does not hurt the policy conclusion, but it weakens the claim that the rise is a law-like consequence of β.
- **Pure return vs r > g**: the stable 4–5 percent pure return here (p. 147) is the r in fm01's r > g and ch10's concentration mechanism. Here it is pretax and averaged over all assets; ch10 and ch12 need a post-tax return by size of fortune. This chapter already says returns rise with fortune size (pp. 143-144, 147; endnote 6 points to ch12) and that the average after-tax return "has decreased much more" than the pretax one (p. 149). Those two adjustments pull in opposite directions for the r > g argument; check how ch10 and ch12 handle them.
- **Inflation**: "redistributing wealth among those who have it" (p. 152), with large fortunes best protected. Feeds ch12 (endnote 13, p. 452) and ch15–ch16's rejection of inflation as a substitute for a capital tax.
- **Human capital**: the "Is Human Capital Illusory?" section (pp. 161-162) defers to Part Three; ch07's patrimonial middle class and ch08–ch09's supermanagers take up the question at the individual level. Endnote 36 (p. 454) flags Modigliani's life-cycle vs Kaldor's inheritance views, picked up in ch07 (p. 177) and ch11.
- **Marx**: the reading of Marx as g ≈ 0 (p. 164) recurs in ch17's conclusion; whether that is a fair reading of the falling-rate-of-profit argument (which in Marx turns on the organic composition of capital, not on g) is not argued here.
- **Political economy of statistics**: the Cold War politicization of the share debate (pp. 158-159) anticipates ch07's claim that measurement "is never neutral" (p. 196).

## Open questions
