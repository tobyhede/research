# ch07: Inequality and Concentration: Preliminary Bearings (pp. 170–196)

## Question this chapter answers

Before the country histories of Part Three, what are the orders of magnitude of inequality in labor income, in capital ownership and in total income, and how should inequality be measured? Piketty's answer: capital is always far more concentrated than labor income; the twentieth century's main change in wealth distribution was the rise of a "patrimonial middle class" (p. 188); very high total-income inequality can come either from a society of rentiers or from a society of supermanagers; and inequality should be measured by decile and centile shares in distribution tables, not by synthetic indices such as the Gini coefficient or interdecile ratios, because "The way one tries to measure inequality is never neutral" (p. 196).

## Leading propositions

- The impression that patrimonial capitalism is new is mistaken; it is "in large part a repetition of the past" typical of low growth (p. 170).
- Part Three will show that the world wars and the policies that followed, not a natural Kuznets process, reduced twentieth-century inequality, and that institutional differences drive the post-1970s rise (p. 170).
- Income inequality decomposes into labor inequality, capital inequality and their interaction (p. 171).
- In nineteenth-century France and Britain, and almost everywhere before 1914, work and study could not match the living standard of inherited wealth (p. 173); the northern US pioneer societies were a short-lived exception, the slave South was not (pp. 173-174).
- "democratic modernity" rests on the belief that inequalities from talent and effort are more justified than others (p. 174).
- After 1945 Vautrin's lesson "ceased to be valid" for a time in Europe; work and study became the surest route to the top (p. 174).
- Inequality of capital income may exceed inequality of capital itself if large fortunes earn higher returns, a powerful multiplier "especially" in this century (p. 175).
- Labor inequality, capital inequality and differential returns require different justifications and different explanatory mechanisms (p. 175).
- Capital is always more unequally distributed than labor income, "without exception" in every country and period with data (p. 176).
- Neither precautionary nor life-cycle saving can explain observed wealth concentration; wealth is nearly as concentrated within each age cohort; inheritance and extreme returns explain it (pp. 176-177).
- The labels lower, middle and upper class are arbitrary, and class terminology is never politically innocent (p. 179); decile analysis allows "rigorous and objective comparisons" (p. 180).
- The top centile is large enough (500,000 adults in France, 2.6 million in the US) to shape the social landscape and politics (pp. 181-182).
- Income and wealth hierarchies are different hierarchies; the top 1 percent by earnings is not the top 1 percent by wealth (p. 182).
- Labor-income inequality is smaller than wealth inequality but not negligible: labor is two-thirds to three-quarters of national income, and cross-country differences show policy matters (p. 183).
- Wealth inequality in the most equal wealth distributions (Scandinavia 1970s–1980s) exceeds labor-income inequality in the least equal (US 2010s) (p. 185).
- No society has ever had "mildly" unequal wealth, meaning the bottom half owning one-fifth to one-quarter (p. 185).
- The rise of a patrimonial middle class was the principal structural change in the twentieth-century wealth distribution (p. 188); the top centile's wealth share fell by more than half, from over 50 percent to 20–25 percent in Europe (p. 189).
- The reduction of wealth inequality "is less substantial than many people believe" and not guaranteed to be irreversible (p. 189).
- Concentration of 90 percent of wealth in the top decile and at least 50 percent in the top centile characterised the whole nineteenth century and traditional rural societies, and is a "necessary condition" for societies based on inherited wealth (p. 189).
- Total-income inequality lies between labor and capital inequality and nearer labor's (pp. 189-190).
- A top-decile share near 50 percent of national income is about the historical maximum, but no hard limit exists; sustainability depends on repression and above all on the "apparatus of justification" (p. 190).
- "the key issue is the justification of inequalities rather than their magnitude as such" (p. 191).
- There are two routes to very high total-income inequality: rentiers (Belle Époque) and supermanagers (US today) (p. 191); the contrast is "naïve and overdrawn", the two can combine, and if they do the future could be more unequal than any past (p. 192).
- The US today combines record labor-income inequality with wealth inequality below traditional or 1900–1910 European levels (p. 192).
- Gini coefficients and other synthetic indices mix levels of the distribution and mix labor with capital; distribution tables are better (pp. 192-193).
- Official reports' reliance on P90/P10 and neglect of the top end give "an artificially rosy picture" and is "hardly neutral" (p. 194).
- Shares are more stable than thresholds; P10 depends heavily on measurement conventions (pp. 194-195).
- National accounts should be broken down into bottom 50, middle 40, top 10 to show who captures growth (p. 195).
- Distribution tables revive eighteenth-century social tables in spirit; Gini and Pareto measures "tend to naturalize the distribution of wealth" (p. 196).

## Arguments

**A. Why wealth concentration points to inheritance (pp. 176-177)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 176-177) If wealth were accumulated mainly as a precaution against short-term wage shocks, wealth inequality would be smaller than instantaneous wage inequality. (p. 177) If it were mainly life-cycle saving for retirement (Modigliani), wealth inequality would mirror labor inequality, shifted in time. (pp. 176-177) Observed wealth inequality is "always and everywhere" much greater than labor inequality. (p. 177) Wealth concentration is "nearly as great within each age cohort" as overall.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 177) Concentration "is explained mainly by the importance of inherited wealth and its cumulative effects", with extreme returns also playing a role.
- **Unstated premise**: that precaution, life cycle and inheritance (plus returns) exhaust the candidate mechanisms; entrepreneurial wealth creation within a lifetime and heterogeneous saving rates are not considered here. The within-cohort claim, which does the work against the life-cycle explanation, rests on endnote 12, "See the online technical appendix" (p. 455).

**B. Why decile shares beat synthetic indices (pp. 175-176, 192-195)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 192-193) A single index cannot represent a multidimensional distribution; the social and political meaning of inequality differs between bottom, middle and top. (p. 193) Gini mixes labor and capital, whose mechanisms and justifications differ. (p. 193) Tables expressed in money force attention to actual levels and expose data anomalies and incomparabilities. (p. 194) P90/P10 ignores everything above P90, where the top decile's share can range from 20 to 90 percent. (pp. 194-195) P10 thresholds are unstable for measurement reasons while shares are stable.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 195) Distribution tables of shares are "the best tool", better than synthetic indices and interdecile ratios.
- **Note**: the tables still report Gini coefficients (p. 192), and Piketty admits they are "sometimes useful" (p. 192). The argument is for primacy, not exclusion. A share table is also a summary: it compresses a continuous distribution into four or five groups, which endnote 22 concedes biases the Gini downward (p. 456).

**C. Two routes to extreme inequality (pp. 190-192)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 189-190) Total-income inequality depends on labor inequality, capital inequality and the capital share. (p. 191) A top-decile income share near 50 percent can arise when wealth is concentrated at 90/50 and capital income dominates the top (Belle Époque), or when top labor incomes are extreme (US now).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 191) "there are two different ways for a society to achieve a very unequal distribution of total income".
- **Qualification**: (p. 192) The dichotomy is "naïve and overdrawn"; the two combine, and the higher US wealth concentration suggests they already do.

**D. Justification, not magnitude, is the key issue (pp. 190-191)**

- **Premises**: (p. 190) A top-decile share of 90 percent of income would probably provoke revolution without effective repression. (p. 190) Extreme wealth concentration is tolerable if capital income is a small share of income. (pp. 190-191) Whether extreme inequality is sustainable depends "perhaps primarily" on the "apparatus of justification": belief that the rich work harder or that restraining them hurts the poor.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 191) The key issue is justification, so the structure of inequality matters more than its level.
- **Unstated premise**: that sustainability is the relevant test. The argument shows why justification matters causally (what societies tolerate), and then uses it as if it settled what matters normatively. The chapter's normative standard is stated only as democratic modernity's belief that talent-and-effort inequality is "more justified" (p. 174).

**E. The patrimonial middle class (pp. 187-189)**

- **Premises**: (p. 188) In 1900–1910 the top decile owned 90 percent of wealth in France, Britain and Sweden, the top centile over 50 percent (over 60 percent in Britain), the middle 40 percent 5–10 percent. (pp. 188-189) Today the top decile owns about 60 percent in Europe and over 70 percent in the US; the middle 40 percent owns a third in Europe and a quarter in the US; the bottom half still owns about 5 percent.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 188) The growth of a patrimonial middle class was "the principal structural transformation" of twentieth-century wealth distribution; (p. 189) it is "fragile" and the overall reduction smaller than commonly believed.

**Position versus argument.** Piketty insists the class labels "play virtually no role" (p. 179), yet the chapter's central historical claim is about a *middle class* gaining wealth (p. 188), and the rentier/supermanager typology carries strong normative weight. The neutral vocabulary of deciles coexists with an openly normative frame (Vautrin, justification, "chaste veil" of official statistics).

## Evidence offered

- **Tables 7.1–7.3** (from p. 178): labor-income, capital-ownership and total-income distributions for illustrative societies (Scandinavia 1970s–1980s, Europe 2010, US 2010, Europe 1910, US 2030 projection, a "virtual" low-inequality society). In this source file the tables are images and their contents are not extracted; figures below come from the running text. Piketty says the figures are "approximate and deliberately rounded off" (p. 178); money levels are computed in online Supplemental Tables S7.1–S7.6 (endnotes 6, 10, 18, 19, 22, pp. 454-456).
- **Labor-income shares** (p. 183): Scandinavia 1970–1990 top 10 percent about 20 percent, bottom 50 about 35 percent; Europe (France, Germany) today top 10 at 25–30, bottom 50 around 30; US early 2010s top 10 at 35, bottom 50 at 25. Top 1 percent about 5 percent of wages in Scandinavia (p. 181). Money illustration at an average wage of 2,000 euros a month: Scandinavia 4,000 / 2,250 / 1,400 (top 1 percent 10,000); US 7,000 / 2,000 / 1,000 (top 1 percent 24,000); US 2030 projection 9,000 / 1,750 / 800 (top 1 percent 34,000) (p. 184). The arithmetic is consistent: e.g. a 20 percent share for 10 percent of people is twice the mean (p. 178), and the 2030 figures imply shares of 45 / 35 / 20 (my check).
- **Wealth shares** (pp. 185-188): Scandinavia 1970s–1980s top 10 at 50–60 percent; Europe early 2010s (France, Germany, Britain, Italy) top 10 about 60 percent, top 1 about 25 percent, middle 40 about 35 percent, bottom 50 about 5 percent. France 2010–2011: top 10 percent 62 percent, bottom 50 percent 4 percent (p. 185). US: "the most recent survey by the Federal Reserve" for the same years gives top 10 at 72 percent, bottom 50 at 2 percent, which Piketty says underestimates the top (p. 185); endnote 11 puts the true US top-decile share "closer to 75 percent" (p. 455). Europe 1900–1910: top 10 at 90, top 1 over 50 (over 60 in Britain), middle 40 at 5–10 percent (p. 188). Ancien Régime France and eighteenth-century England: the same 90/50 orders of magnitude, attributed to "All available sources" (p. 189) with no citation in the text.
- **Money illustration of wealth** at 200,000 euros average per adult, roughly today's richest European countries (pp. 185-187; endnote 16 to chapters 3–5 and the online appendix, p. 455): bottom 50 percent 20,000 euros (30,000–40,000 with durables, p. 186); middle 40 percent 175,000; top 10 percent 1.2 million; top 1 percent 5 million; next 9 percent "a little less than 800,000" (exactly 777,778, endnote 18, p. 455). Europe 1910 equivalent: 20,000 / 25,000 / 1.8 million, top 1 percent 10 million (endnote 19, p. 455). Arithmetic checks out.
- **Composition of wealth by level** (p. 187): real estate about half of wealth around 1 million euros; under one-third at 2–5 million; under 20 percent above 5 million; under 10 percent above 10 million. Source not given in the text.
- **Bottom of the wealth distribution** (p. 186): about a quarter of the population with no wealth or a few thousand euros; one-twentieth to one-tenth with negative net wealth. Stated without a source.
- **Total-income shares** (pp. 189-191): top 10 about 25 percent in 1970s–1980s Scandinavia, 30 percent in Germany and France then, over 35 percent now; about 50 percent (20 for the top centile) in Ancien Régime and Belle Époque France and Britain and in the US today; possibly 60 percent or more in pre-revolutionary France (p. 190); US 2030 projection top 10 about 60 percent, bottom half about 15 percent (p. 191).
- **Gini values** (p. 192): Scandinavian labor income 0.19; Belle Époque European wealth 0.85. Computed from grouped data and so slightly too low (endnote 22, p. 456).
- **Demography of the top centile** (p. 181): France 2013, about 50 million adults, top centile about 500,000; US 320 million people, 260 million adults, 2.6 million.
- **Historical-political comparisons**: French estates in 1789 (aristocracy 1–2 percent, clergy under 1 percent, Third Estate over 97 percent) (p. 179); Sieyès's 1789 pamphlet and the Occupy Wall Street slogan about the 99 percent (p. 182; endnote 8, p. 455); the "200 families" and Forbes 400 (p. 181).
- **Literary evidence**: Vautrin's speech quoted at length from *Père Goriot* (Livre de Poche 1983, pp. 123–35 and 131; endnotes 1–3, p. 454) (pp. 171-173); Austen's heroes; *Gone with the Wind* (pp. 173-174). Piketty calls Vautrin's figures "quite realistic" (p. 173) and defers the detail to ch11 (endnote 2, p. 454).
- **Anecdote**: a former French president's son marrying the Darty heiress (endnote 4, p. 454).
- **Precedents for measurement reform**: World Top Incomes Database (p. 194); Stiglitz–Sen–Fitoussi report (2009) (p. 195; endnote 25, p. 456); social tables from King (1688) to Colquhoun and Blodget, Quesnay's *Tableau économique*, Milanovic–Lindert–Williamson on ancient inequality (pp. 195-196; endnote 26, p. 456).
- **Bare assertion or language outrunning evidence**: "without exception" (p. 176) for all countries and periods with data; "a revolution will likely occur" at a 90 percent income share (p. 190); "All available sources agree" on 90/50 for traditional rural societies (p. 189); the within-cohort concentration claim, sourced only to the online appendix (endnote 12, p. 455); official statistics deliberately ignoring the top end "inevitably feeds the wildest fantasies" (p. 194).

## Concessions and qualifications

- Labor-income inequality is not always equitable, and social justice is not just labor vs inherited wealth (p. 174).
- Rare cases exist where aiming to inherit a fortune would still be well advised (p. 174).
- The questions will be answered "with the imperfect data at my disposal" (p. 174).
- The class labels are "arbitrary and open to challenge" (p. 179), and the middle-40 definition is "highly contestable" (p. 180).
- Any small set of categories is "doomed to be crudely schematic"; the reality is a continuous distribution (p. 180).
- The decile approach "will not allow us to eliminate every problem or settle every question" (p. 182).
- The Fed survey underestimates top fortunes (p. 185).
- The "ideal society" with low wealth inequality is a "virtual example" and how to reach it "remains to be seen" (p. 185); endnote 14 notes that even it leaves the top 1 percent 20 times richer on average than the bottom half (p. 455).
- There are "no grounds for asserting" that the top decile can never take more than 50 percent of income (p. 190); historical data "are far from perfect" (p. 190).
- The 2030 US projection carries "no certainty" (p. 184).
- The rentier/supermanager contrast is "naïve and overdrawn" (p. 192).
- Whether US top labor incomes are meritocratic is left open: "the opposite conclusion is equally possible" (p. 191).
- Gini coefficients "are sometimes useful" (p. 192); interdecile ratios "can be useful" (pp. 193-194).
- Precautionary and life-cycle saving exist and life-cycle saving matters "especially in aging societies" (pp. 176-177).
- Treating pensions as labor income is a convention; without it labor inequality would be "noticeably—and to some extent artificially—greater" (endnote 9, p. 455).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **From ch06**: ch06 ends by asking whether the "deep structures of capital" have changed at the level of individual distribution (p. 170). This chapter's answer in outline: the macro share of capital barely changed, but its distribution did (the patrimonial middle class), though less than believed (pp. 188-189).
- **Capital gains (my observation)**: capital income here includes capital gains (p. 175), while ch06's average return excludes them (ch06 p. 151). The chapters use two definitions of capital income; for the r > g argument (ch10) it matters which one r means.
- **Differential returns**: the multiplier from higher returns on large fortunes (p. 175) repeats ch06 (pp. 143-144, 147) and is deferred to ch12. It is the bridge between the macro r of ch06 and the concentration mechanism of ch10.
- **Inheritance**: the claim that inheritance explains wealth concentration (p. 177) is asserted here and argued in ch10–ch11; the Modigliani vs Kaldor contrast was flagged in ch06 endnote 36 (p. 454). Vautrin's figures are revisited in ch11 (endnote 2, p. 454).
- **Feeds ch08–ch09**: the supermanager society (p. 191) is the subject of ch08's US story and ch09's account of executive pay; the "highly political chronology" of French profit shares in ch06 (p. 164) and the policy-driven compression announced here (p. 170) set up ch08.
- **Feeds Part Four**: the "ideal society" wealth distribution (p. 185) and the redistribution question deferred to Part Four (p. 183) lead to ch13–ch15. The call to break national accounts into decile groups (p. 195) anticipates ch15's demand for financial transparency.
- **Justification and Hayek (cross-book)**: the "apparatus of justification" (p. 190) and the claim that democratic modernity accepts talent-and-effort inequality (p. 174) can be set against *The Road to Serfdom* ch06, where Hayek concedes that the Rule of Law produces economic inequality and defends it because that inequality is not designed to affect particular people (see the road-to-serfdom deep-read note ch06-planning-and-the-rule-of-law.md). Piketty's argument is that tolerance of inequality depends on belief in its justification; Hayek's that impersonal inequality is easier to bear. Both treat legitimacy as decisive, from opposite directions.
- **Measurement is political**: "never neutral" (p. 196) and the "chaste veil" of official statistics (p. 194) extend ch06's account of the politicized capital-share debate (ch06 pp. 158-159). By the same standard Piketty's own choices (deciles, adults, individual vs household, pensions as wages) are not neutral either; endnotes 5, 9 and 24 (pp. 454-456) show he knows they matter.
- **Internal inconsistency (minor)**: the middle 40 percent's wealth share is 35 percent in the European example (p. 187), "scarcely more than a third" of Europe's wealth (p. 188) and "one-quarter to one-third" collectively (p. 189). The range covers Europe and the US, but the three phrasings are not reconciled.
- **Decomposition is verbal (my observation)**: the "three terms" decomposition (p. 171) is not a formal decomposition (e.g. of a variance into two variances and a covariance). Shares of total income cannot be additively split into a labor part and a capital part plus interaction without a stated method, so the decomposition is a heuristic, not a measurement.

## Open questions

- What sources underlie Tables 7.1–7.3 for each society? The text gives only the France 2010–2011 and US Federal Reserve figures; the rest is in the online appendix. The table contents are not reproduced in this source file and should be checked in a print edition.
- Is wealth concentration really "nearly as great within each age cohort" (p. 177)? This is the decisive evidence against the life-cycle explanation and is sourced only to the online appendix (endnote 12, p. 455).
- How does the US 2030 projection (top decile about 60 percent of income, p. 191) get made? It appears to be a trend extrapolation; check ch08–ch09.
- Does "justification rather than magnitude" (p. 191) give Piketty a normative standard, or only a theory of what is politically sustainable? Track how Part Four uses it.
- If income and wealth hierarchies differ and are only positively correlated (pp. 182-183), how is the correlation measured historically? The claim of negative correlation in traditional societies (p. 182) is asserted without data.
- What explains the rise of the patrimonial middle class (p. 189: "essential to understand why it occurred")? ch10 should answer this; note whether the answer is war shocks, taxation, housing prices, or all three.
