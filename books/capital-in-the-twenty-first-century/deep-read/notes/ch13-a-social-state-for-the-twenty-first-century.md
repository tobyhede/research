# ch13: A Social State for the Twenty-First Century (pp. 342–360)

## Question this chapter answers

What role should government play in producing and distributing wealth in the twenty-first century, and what kind of social state suits the age (p. 343)? The chapter opens Part Four and its normative turn. It does three things:

- **History**: in the twentieth century the rich countries went from taxing under a tenth of national income to taxing between a third and a half. Almost all of the increase paid for a "social state" of education, health, pensions and transfers (pp. 345-348).
- **Principle**: modern redistribution follows a "logic of rights" to fundamental goods, which Piketty grounds in the 1789 Declaration's "common utility", read in a Rawlsian way (pp. 348-350).
- **Policy**: the social state should be modernised, not expanded further or dismantled. The test cases are unequal access to higher education and PAYGO pensions under low growth. Poor countries need to build fiscal capacity (pp. 350-360).

The progressive capital tax is set aside as "only one component of an ideal social and fiscal system" (pp. 342-343) for later chapters.

## Leading propositions

### Factual and historical claims

- The wars of the twentieth century largely wiped away the past and transformed inequality. Wealth inequality is now close to regaining or surpassing its historical highs (p. 342).
- The 2008 crisis was less severe than 1929–1935. Output fell no more than 5 points in 2009, against a quarter in 1929–1935, because governments and central banks prevented financial collapse (p. 343).
- Pragmatic monetary policy did not address the structural causes: lack of financial transparency and rising inequality. 2008 was "the first crisis of the globalized patrimonial capitalism" (p. 344).
- Roosevelt raised the top federal income tax rate from 25 percent under Hoover to over 80 percent. The Obama-era debate was about moving from about 35 to about 40 percent (p. 344).
- Taxes took less than 10 percent of national income in the US, Britain, France and Sweden before 1914 (p. 345).
- From 1920 to 1980 the tax share rose by a factor of at least 3 or 4 (more than 5 in the Nordic countries). It stabilised in 1980–2010 at just over 30 percent in the US, about 40 percent in Britain, 45 in Germany, 50 in France and nearly 55 in Sweden (p. 346).
- So in tax and spending terms the state has "never" been larger, and talk of a "return of the state" is misleading. Deregulation and privatisation did reduce the state's role on other dimensions (p. 346).
- Social functions consume a quarter to a third of national income, split about evenly between education and health on one side and replacement incomes and transfers on the other (p. 347).
- Education and health cost 10–15 percent of national income. Public spending covers about three-quarters of their cost in Europe and half in the US (p. 347).
- Pensions cost 12–13 percent of national income in continental Europe and 6–7 percent in the US and UK. Public pensions are the main income source for at least two-thirds of retirees (pp. 347-348).
- Public pensions eradicated old-age poverty, which was "endemic as recently as the 1950s". They are the "third social revolution", after education and health (p. 348).
- Unemployment insurance costs 1–2 percent of national income and income support less than 1 percent. Income support is the most contested despite its small size, and stigma and complexity deter take-up (p. 348).
- The growth of the fiscal state "basically reflects the constitution of a social state" (p. 348).
- In the nineteenth century, the regimes born of the US and French Revolutions concentrated mainly on protecting property rights (p. 350).
- Broad consensus, especially in Europe, supports the social state. No major force proposes returning to 10–20 percent of national income in tax, and none proposes continuing 1930–1980 growth rates, which would mean 70–80 percent by 2050–2060 (p. 350).
- Education and health account for about 20 percent of employment and GDP in developed economies, more than all of industry (p. 351).
- Despite mass education, earned income inequality did not fall, and intergenerational mobility shows no long-run rise and may have fallen recently (pp. 352-353).
- Mobility is highest in the Nordic countries and lowest in the US. The US correlation coefficient is about two-thirds higher than Sweden's, with France, Germany and Britain in between (p. 353).
- In the US, the share of college degrees earned by children of bottom-half parents stayed at 10–20 percent in 1970–2010, while for top-quartile children it rose from 40 to 80 percent (p. 353).
- Harvard parents' average income is about $450,000, the average of the top 2 percent. Sciences Po parents' is about 90,000 euros, roughly the top 10 percent (p. 354).
- Most continental European tuition is under 500 euros. Quebec's planned rise from $2,000 to nearly $4,000 was reversed after a 2012 student strike (p. 354).
- French grandes écoles spend more public money on more advantaged students (Bourdieu and Passeron) (p. 354).
- PAYGO's return was close to 5 percent in postwar continental Europe and is about 1.5 percent now. r is expected at 4–5 percent (p. 356).
- Wage growth is "5–10 times less volatile" than the return on capital (p. 357). Note 45: interwar savers ruined by markets were the reason PAYGO replaced funded schemes, such as France's laws of 1910 and 1928 (p. 487).
- In 1970–1980 poor countries (Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia) taxed 10–15 percent of national income and intermediate ones 15–20 percent. Poor-country tax levels then fell, to a little over 10 percent in the 1990s, while rich-country levels rose (pp. 358-359).
- The fall in poor countries' revenue in 1980–1990 was largely due to lower customs duties, which had been about 5 percent of national income (Cagé and Gadenne) (p. 359).
- China has a broad income tax raising substantial revenue. India has had more difficulty (p. 360).

### Normative claims and principles

- The ideal policy is a progressive global tax on capital, which is "no doubt a utopian ideal". A regional, especially European, version is the fallback (p. 342).
- Confiscatory top income tax rates "deserve" to be revived, especially in the US (p. 344). A capital tax suits the twenty-first century better than the income tax (p. 344).
- Both the antimarket and the antistate camps are "partly correct": new instruments are needed to control finance, and tax and transfer systems need modernising (p. 345).
- Citizens will not accept new supranational tools unless existing ones are seen to work (p. 345).
- Modern redistribution rests on rights and equal access to fundamental goods, not on explicit transfers from rich to poor (pp. 348-349).
- Article 1 of the 1789 Declaration puts the burden of proof on inequality. "Common utility" can reasonably be read as allowing inequalities only if they serve all, especially the most disadvantaged, which is close to Rawls and Sen (p. 349).
- Abstract principles command a "partly artificial" consensus. Real disagreements about means, the extent of rights and the line between luck and effort can be settled only by "democratic deliberation and political confrontation" (pp. 349-350).
- "Taxation is neither good nor bad in itself" (p. 350). A much larger tax share is neither realistic nor desirable for the foreseeable future. Low growth makes durable tax increases hard, private consumption needs are legitimate, and large public sectors face organisational problems (pp. 350-352).
- The agenda is modernising and consolidating the social state, not dismantling or expanding it (p. 352).
- Tuition fees create "an unacceptable inequality of access". Publicly financed incentives to autonomous universities, on the Nordic model, are preferable to income-related fees or income-contingent loans (p. 355).
- Transparency about selection and parental income should come first. Meritocratic discourse often justifies existing inequalities (p. 355).
- PAYGO should remain part of the ideal social state everywhere. Moving to a funded system is "totally unthinkable" in an extreme form, because of the transition generation and volatility (pp. 356-357).
- Retirement ages should rise in a way that distinguishes intellectual from arduous work (p. 357). A unified scheme of individual accounts should replace fragmented regimes (p. 358). People of modest means should be encouraged to save (p. 358).
- Building a fiscal and social state is part of state-building and development. The rich countries and international organisations share blame for poor countries' falling tax capacity (p. 359).

## Arguments

**A. There has been no "return of the state" because it never left (pp. 344-347)**

- **Premises**: (p. 344) Today's crisis differs from 1929 because the state is already far larger. (p. 346) Taxes went from under 10 percent to 30–55 percent of national income and stabilised. (p. 346) No downward trend is visible in taxes or outlays.
- **Qualification**: (p. 346) The state also acts by rules and ownership, and on those dimensions (financial deregulation, privatisation) its role shrank after 1980.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 346-347) "Return of the state" is a misleading frame. "There will be no second leap".
- **Note**: the conclusion is drawn on the tax/spending dimension alone, which he admits is partial. His later programme (capital tax, financial transparency) is largely about the rule and ownership dimensions, where the state did retreat.

**B. The fiscal state is the social state (pp. 347-348)**

- **Premises**: (p. 347) Regalian functions cost a little under a tenth of national income today, about what they cost in total before 1914 (p. 345). (pp. 347-348) Education and health (10–15 percent) plus replacement and transfers (10–15, up to 20 percent) total 25–35 percent.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 348) Social spending accounts for "nearly all" the twentieth-century increase in revenue.

**C. From the 1789 Declaration to a Rawlsian principle (pp. 348-350)**

- **Premises**: (p. 349) The Declaration of Independence asserts an equal right to the pursuit of happiness. (p. 349) Article 1 of 1789 asserts equal rights and allows social distinctions only "on common utility". (p. 349) The second sentence reverses the burden of proof onto inequality. (p. 349) The drafters meant the abolition of hereditary orders and privileges, but the phrase "can" be read more broadly.
- **Interpretive step**: (p. 349) "One reasonable interpretation": inequalities are acceptable only if in the interest of all, "in particular of the most disadvantaged". Hence rights and advantages should be extended to everyone as far as it serves those with the fewest rights and opportunities.
- **Supporting moves**: (n. 20, p. 484) The drafters were not utilitarians, and the "concave utility" justification of redistribution "bears little apparent relation to the way most people think". Rights are more pertinent. (n. 21, pp. 484-485) Disadvantage is defined by factors beyond one's control, so reducing inequalities of family endowment and luck is just. (n. 22, p. 485) Rawls quoted.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 349) This is "similar in intent" to Rawls's difference principle and close to Sen. There is a "certain (partly artificial) consensus" on abstract principles.
- **Status (my observation)**: this is a normative reconstruction, not a historical claim. Piketty marks it as an interpretation ("One can interpret the phrase more broadly"). Moving from the drafters' anti-privilege meaning to a maximin principle is a large step: abolishing legal orders is compatible with any distribution produced by equal legal rules. The step gives a French revolutionary pedigree to what is essentially Rawls, and it does the work of making the capital tax look like an application of an old consensus. Note too that note 21 adds luck-egalitarianism (factors beyond control), which is not in Rawls's difference principle as quoted in n. 22.

**D. Principles do not settle policy; democracy does (pp. 349-350)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 349-350) Disagreements concern means, the extent of affordable rights, and the boundary between luck and effort. (p. 350) These "will never be answered by abstract principles or mathematical formulas".
- **Conclusion**: (p. 350) Democratic institutions and the relative power and persuasiveness of social groups decide.
- **Tension**: this sits uneasily with the confident policy conclusions elsewhere (the capital tax as "the ideal policy", p. 342; confiscatory rates, p. 344). Piketty presents these as following from his historical analysis, not as one side's position in a democratic contest.

**E. Why the social state should not keep growing (pp. 350-352)**

- **Premises**: (p. 350) In theory, a country could tax two-thirds or three-quarters of national income, since taxation is neither good nor bad in itself. (pp. 350-351) Postwar expansion was eased by 5 percent growth, while with growth of just over 1 percent per capita durable rises in the average tax rate are hard to imagine. (p. 351) People legitimately want private goods, and there is "no obvious reason" all needs should be tax-financed. (p. 351) Beyond a certain size the public sector faces organisational problems. New participatory forms might solve them, but that is speculative.
- **Conclusion**: A drastic increase is "neither realistic nor desirable, at least for the foreseeable future" (p. 350). (p. 352) The task is to improve the existing public sector, about half of national income.
- **Note**: the first reason is a political-economy claim about consent under low growth, supported by the uniform stabilisation since 1980 ("no accident", p. 351). The second is a mixed normative and organisational claim. Neither rules out more progressive redistribution at a constant total (p. 351, explicitly allowed).

**F. Education does not deliver mobility (pp. 352-355)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 352-353) Educational upgrading shifted all qualification levels upward without reducing wage inequality (Part Three). (p. 353) Intergenerational correlations show no long-run trend to more mobility, though the data are "highly imperfect". (p. 353) US mobility is lowest among the rich countries. (pp. 353-354) US elite access tracks parental income through tuition and donations. Harvard parents average top-2-percent incomes. (p. 354) In France, cultural selection and grandes écoles funding do similar work.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 355) Real equality of opportunity in higher education has no easy solution. Meritocratic rhetoric often justifies existing inequality. The Boutmy quotation shows elites inventing meritocracy to defend their position against universal suffrage (p. 355).
- **Inference to check**: (p. 353) "One possible explanation" of low US mobility is high tuition. This is offered as a possibility, but by the end of the paragraph parents' income "has become an almost perfect predictor of university access" (p. 353). The underlying data are degree completion by parental income quartile (Duncan and Murnane, n. 30, p. 486). A 10–20 percent against 80 percent gap is strong, but "almost perfect predictor" overstates what quartile shares show.

**G. Keep PAYGO despite r > g (pp. 355-357)**

- **Premises**: (p. 356) PAYGO's return equals the growth rate g. Under r > g a funded system seems to pay more. (p. 356) But the transition leaves one generation with nothing, because it paid for its predecessors and would see its successors' contributions invested instead. (p. 357) r > g holds on average, not for each investment. Returns are volatile, and betting a generation's necessities on markets is irrational. (p. 357) Wage growth is 5–10 times less volatile than capital returns.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 357) PAYGO "will therefore continue to be part of the ideal social state of the future everywhere". (p. 357) Parameters such as retirement age must adapt to longer lives and low growth.
- **Note**: the transition argument is a classic double-payment problem. The volatility argument depends on the risk-return tradeoff that ch12 argued is weak for the largest portfolios (p. 328 in ch12). Piketty's position stays consistent because the relevant investor here is a generation of small savers. Note 47 links back to ch12: modest savers struggle even to achieve the average return (p. 487).

**H. Poor countries need fiscal capacity, and the rich countries undermined it (pp. 358-360)**

- **Premises**: (p. 359) With 10–15 percent of national income in tax, a state can do little beyond regalian functions. (p. 359) Underpaying public servants produces a vicious circle of poor services and low trust. (p. 359) Since 1980, "ultraliberal" pressure cut public sectors, and trade liberalisation removed customs revenue (about 5 percent of national income) before replacement taxes existed. (p. 359) Today's rich countries cut tariffs at their own pace with alternatives in mind.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 359) Part of the blame lies with the rich countries and international organisations, who use the developing world as "a field of experimentation" (pp. 359-360).
- **Evidence status**: one working paper (Cagé and Gadenne 2012, n. 48, pp. 487-488) supports the customs-revenue claim. The broader "ultraliberal wave" causal claim is asserted.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Opens Part Four on ch10–ch12**: the capital tax is introduced as the ideal answer to the "inegalitarian spiral" (p. 342) established in ch12 (pp. 320, 340 there), then deliberately set aside. The chapter's own reforms (education, pensions) do not address capital concentration directly.
- **Feeds ch14**: confiscatory top income rates (p. 344) are promised for ch14. Ch14 opens by describing ch13 as having treated "the overall level of taxes as a given" (p. 360).
- **Feeds ch15 and ch16**: the "utopian" global capital tax and the European fallback (p. 342). Central banks as lender of last resort (pp. 343-344) anticipate ch16.
- **Links to ch12**: Harvard's endowment and elite admissions (n. 29, pp. 485-486). Note 38's "800 US universities" points back to ch12 (p. 487). Note 47 on small savers' low returns (p. 487) recalls ch12 (p. 332 there). ch12's funded-pension scenario for China (p. 338 there) is answered by the case for PAYGO here.
- **Links to ch09**: the education-technology race (pp. 352-353 refer back to Part Three). The upward shift of qualifications without lower wage inequality is ch09's result, reused here to show education did not deliver mobility.
- **Links to ch02**: low growth, about 1–1.5 percent per capita at the frontier, is the premise for both the tax-stabilisation argument (p. 351) and the PAYGO problem (p. 356).
- **Tension: "modern redistribution" against progressive taxation (my observation)**: p. 348 says modern redistribution is not transfers from rich to poor but equal provision. ch14–ch15 make steep progressivity central. The two fit together only if equal provision is financed progressively, which ch13 does not discuss (deferred to ch14).
- **Tension: the burden of proof (my observation)**: Piketty's reading of article 1 puts the burden of proof on inequality (p. 349). In ch12, though, he refuses to judge individual fortunes (in ch12: "Every fortune is partially justified" (p. 323)) and proposes a tax by size, not by justification. The common-utility test therefore works at the level of the tax schedule, not individual fortunes. This is coherent, but it means the principle never decides any particular case.
- **Tension: consensus against democratic contest**: the "partly artificial" consensus on abstract principles (p. 349) and the claim that only democratic deliberation can settle substance (p. 350) sit beside strong policy verdicts throughout Part Four.
- **Number check (my observation)**: "a correlation coefficient two-thirds higher than in Sweden" (p. 353) matches the ranges in note 28 only at their nearest ends (0.3 against 0.5). Taking midpoints (0.25 against 0.55), the US coefficient is more than double Sweden's. The text understates the gap compared with its own note.
- **Number check**: "Recruitment is thus 5 times broader than at Harvard" (p. 354) compares top 10 percent with top 2 percent average-income positions. As note 32 (p. 486) and note 37 (p. 486) say, these are averages, not recruitment ranges, so "5 times broader" is loose.
- **Cross-book link (Hayek, Road to Serfdom ch06)**: Piketty's "logic of rights" to equal access (p. 349) is a rule-based, categorical form of redistribution: universal entitlements, not person-by-person allocation. It bears on the Road to Serfdom ch06 open question of whether general rules can carry a distributive aim. Piketty's universal provision is a test case for Hayek's criterion that policy must not aim directly at distributive justice. Note also Piketty's open line that "taxation is neither good nor bad in itself" (p. 350) against Hayek's focus on the form, not the size, of state action.

## Open questions

- How far does the list of fundamental rights extend (culture, housing, travel, p. 349)? Piketty raises the question and does not answer it. Is the answer meant to come only from democratic deliberation (p. 350)?
- Does the Rawlsian reading of "common utility" do real work in Part Four, or is it decorative? Check whether ch14 (optimal top rates) or ch15 (capital tax) argues from the interest of the most disadvantaged, or from efficiency, transparency and democratic control.
- What exactly is the evidence for the "5–10 times less volatile" claim (p. 357)? No source is given in text or note.
- The tax-stabilisation argument (p. 351) treats low growth as limiting consent to higher average tax rates. Is the post-1980 plateau evidence of a growth constraint, or of a political shift (the "ultraliberal wave" Piketty himself invokes for poor countries, p. 359)? He uses ideology to explain the poor-country decline but growth to explain the rich-country plateau.
- Is the proposed unified individual-accounts PAYGO scheme (p. 358) compatible with Beveridgian capped pensions, or is it implicitly Bismarckian (proportional to lifetime earnings)? Note 46 points to the Swedish notional-account model (p. 487), which is contributory.
- How does the case against income-contingent loans (n. 41, p. 487), which calls them a surtax on students of modest background, square with the progressive income tax Piketty defends in ch14? Both are taxes on later earnings.
- Is "ultraliberal" pressure as the cause of the poor-country revenue decline (p. 359) supported beyond Cagé and Gadenne's customs-duty result?
