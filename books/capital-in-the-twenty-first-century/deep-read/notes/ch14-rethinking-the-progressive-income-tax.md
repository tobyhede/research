# ch14: Rethinking the Progressive Income Tax (pp. 360–378)

## Question this chapter answers

What role has the progressive income tax (and its twin, the progressive estate tax) played in shaping twentieth-century inequality, why is it now under threat, and how high should the top marginal rate be? Piketty's answer: progressive taxation was born of the world wars more than of democracy, and its purposes were never thought through (p. 360), (p. 364); the United States and Britain invented "confiscatory" top rates, whose aim was to end very high incomes and fortunes rather than to raise revenue (p. 370); the collapse of those rates after 1980 explains the explosion of top executive pay through a bargaining mechanism, not a productivity one (pp. 373-376); and the optimal top rate on incomes above $500,000 or $1 million is "probably above 80 percent" (p. 376).

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proportional tax (flat tax) | p. 362 | A tax whose rate "is the same for everyone" (p. 362). |
| Progressive tax | p. 362 | A tax whose rate is higher for those who earn, own, or consume more (p. 362). Defined by the rate, not by the amount paid. |
| Regressive tax | p. 362 | A tax whose rate falls for richer people, either through legal or illegal exemption (optimization, evasion) or because the schedule itself is regressive; Thatcher's poll tax is the example (p. 362). Piketty's note 3 explains the poll tax was a flat per-adult payment, so its rate was lower for the rich (p. 489). |
| Taxes on income / capital / consumption | p. 361 | The standard tripartite classification, which Piketty calls ambiguous: the income tax also taxes capital income; capital taxes include levies on capital income flows (corporate tax) and on the stock (real estate, estate, wealth taxes); consumption taxes are "indirect" because paid as part of the price (p. 361). |
| Social contributions | p. 361 | A "fourth category" that appeared in the twentieth century: a special tax on (usually labour) income earmarked for social insurance funds (p. 361). The legal categories are "partly arbitrary" (p. 362). |
| Top (marginal) rate | p. 365 | "the tax rate on the highest income bracket" (p. 365). Piketty's note 14 adds that it applies only to income above a threshold, usually to under 1% of the population, and gives only an upper bound on the effective rate of the wealthiest (p. 489). |
| Fiscal secession | p. 363 | The wealthiest escaping progressive taxation so that the system becomes regressive at the top; threatens "fiscal consent" (p. 363). |
| Fiscal consent | p. 363 | The general willingness, especially of the middle class, to pay for the fiscal and social state; undermined when the top pays less than the middle (p. 363). |
| Rastignac's dilemma | p. 363 | Reused from ch11: inherited wealth is lightly taxed relative to labour income, so ranking people by lifetime resources (earned income plus capitalised inheritance), which he calls "a more satisfactory criterion" for progressivity, would show the system even more regressive at the top (p. 363). |
| Overall (total) income | p. 364 | The base of the early progressive income tax: the sum of labour income (wage and nonwage) and all capital income (rent, interest, dividends, profits, sometimes capital gains) (p. 364). |
| Categorical / schedular tax | p. 366 | Pre-progressive income taxes applying separate, usually flat, rates of 5–10% to each category ("schedule") of income; Britain from 1842 until the 1909 "supertax" (p. 366). |
| Indicial tax system | p. 367 | The French revolutionary system of taxing on external indices of ability to pay (doors and windows, rental value) rather than declared income (p. 367). |
| Confiscatory tax on "excessive" incomes | p. 370 | Rates of 70–80% or more whose primary goal is not revenue but "to put an end to such incomes and large estates" or make them very costly; an "American invention" (p. 370). |
| Earned / unearned income | p. 372 | Anglo-American distinction between labour income and capital income; the top rates in Figure 14.1 applied to unearned income, which was taxed more heavily (p. 372). |
| Bargaining power explanation | p. 374 | Piketty's account of rising top pay: lower top rates raise executives' payoff from pushing boards for raises, and because individual contributions are hard to measure they succeed (pp. 374-376). Set against the marginal productivity / labor supply explanation (pp. 373-374). |
| Elasticity with respect to "luck" vs "talent" | p. 376 | Sensitivity of executive pay to firm performance explained by sector-wide movements ("luck") versus performance not explained by sector variables ("talent") (p. 376). |
| Optimal top tax rate | p. 376 | The rate Piketty, Saez and Stantcheva estimate for the top 1% or 0.5%: "probably above 80 percent" (p. 376); Piketty's note 50 gives 82% (p. 494). He disclaims the precision. |

## Leading propositions

- The progressive income tax was the major twentieth-century fiscal innovation and helped reduce inequality; it is now threatened by international tax competition and by never having had its foundations "clearly thought through", since it was adopted in an emergency (p. 360). The same is true of the progressive estate tax (p. 360).
- Taxation is "preeminently a political and philosophical issue"; every major political upheaval has a fiscal revolution at its heart (p. 360).
- In modern fiscal states total tax payments are close to proportional to income for most people; financing half of national income requires everyone to contribute (p. 362).
- Progressivity at the very top still matters: progressive taxation of top incomes and estates "partly explains" why wealth concentration never regained Belle Époque levels after 1914–1945, and the post-1980 fall in US and UK progressivity "probably explains much" of the rise of the highest earned incomes (p. 362).
- Tax competition has led governments, especially in Europe, to exempt capital income, producing a race to the bottom (p. 362); in most countries taxes have become or will become regressive at the top (p. 362).
- A system regressive at the top risks "fiscal secession" by the wealthy and erodes the middle class's fiscal consent; the social state needs at least a minimum of progressivity (p. 363).
- Measuring progressivity by income alone ignores lightly taxed inheritance; by lifetime resources the system is more regressive still (p. 363).
- Globalization hurts the least skilled in rich countries; a more progressive system could improve their position, and without it the losers from free trade may turn against it (pp. 363-364).
- Progressive taxation was "as much a product of two world wars as it was of democracy" (p. 364). Before 1914 top rates were everywhere tiny (below 10%, generally below 5%), similar to eighteenth- and nineteenth-century rates (pp. 365-366).
- The French Third Republic, despite universal male suffrage and an egalitarian self-image, refused full progressivity for nearly half a century; the 1901 estate tax reform barely touched accumulation (pp. 367-368).
- Opponents such as Leroy-Beaulieu argued, without data, that France was already egalitarian and needed no progressive tax; the 1901 probate statistics showed French wealth nearly as concentrated as British (pp. 368-369). Even a low-rate tax produces knowledge and transparency (p. 369).
- The United States, followed by Britain, invented confiscatory top rates: first above 70% on income (1919–1922) and estates (1937–1939) (p. 370). The aim was to end incomes judged "socially unacceptable and economically unproductive", not revenue (p. 370).
- The progressive tax is "a relatively liberal method for reducing inequality": it respects competition and private property while changing incentives under democratically debated rules (p. 370). Continental Europe used nationalization and direct salary-setting as partial substitutes (p. 370).
- US top federal income tax rate averaged 81% over 1932–1980 (p. 371); no continental European country sustained such rates (pp. 371-372). Top US estate rates stayed at 70–80% from the 1930s to the 1980s (p. 372); Britain reached 98% on top incomes and estates in the 1940s and 1970s (p. 372).
- Top rates applied to very few people: thresholds equivalent to 500,000–1 million euros in 2000–2010 terms, i.e., 0.1–0.5% of the population (p. 372).
- After 1980 US and UK top rates fell from 80–90% to 30–40% (28% after 1986), while France and Germany stayed at 50–60% (p. 373). Part of the reason was the sense of being overtaken in the 1970s, though the catch-up was "largely a mechanical consequence" of the 1914–1945 shocks to Europe and Japan (p. 373).
- Across developed countries, the size of the fall in top marginal rates since 1980 is "perfectly correlated" with the rise in the top centile's income share (p. 373).
- The marginal-productivity explanation of this correlation is implausible (pp. 373-374); the bargaining explanation fits better (p. 374).
- There is no statistically significant relation between the fall in top rates and productivity growth since 1980; per capita GDP growth has been about the same in all rich countries (p. 374).
- Piketty, Saez and Stantcheva's executive-pay data favour the bargaining model over managerial productivity (p. 375). Executive pay responds more to luck than to talent, the luck elasticity is higher where top rates are lower, and firm size, finance, or weak competition do not explain the cross-country pattern (p. 376). Only dissuasive taxation of the pre-1980 kind "can do the job" (p. 376).
- The optimal top rate in developed countries is "probably above 80 percent" for the top 1% or 0.5%, but no formula can settle the rate; only "collective deliberation and democratic experimentation" can (p. 376).
- An 80% rate on incomes over $500,000 or $1 million would not reduce US growth and would yield little revenue because it would reduce such pay; revenue would need rates of 50–60% on incomes above $200,000 (pp. 376-377).
- The United States is big enough to do this alone; small European countries without coordination are not (pp. 376-377).
- Adoption is unlikely; the US political process may have been captured by the 1%, though Piketty prefers to credit ideas and better (tax) data (p. 377). Since war, not universal suffrage, created progressive taxation, there is "little reason for optimism" (p. 377), and the US risks becoming the Old Europe of the twenty-first century (p. 378).

## Arguments

**A. Progressivity at the top is necessary for the social state (pp. 362-364)**

- **Premises**: (p. 362) The modern fiscal state is roughly proportional for most people. (pp. 362-363) Capital income escapes progressive taxation through tax competition, so the top centiles pay lower overall rates (France 2010: 35% for the top 0.1% against 40–50% for the rest). (p. 363) A middle class paying more than the rich will withdraw consent: "why continue to pay for others?"
- **Conclusion**: (p. 363) The social state requires that the tax system "retain a minimum of progressivity, or at any rate that it not become overtly regressive at the top".
- **Unstated premise**: that perceived fairness of the overall schedule, rather than the services received, drives the middle class's fiscal consent. The claim about consent is asserted, not evidenced.

**B. Progressive taxation was a product of war, not of democracy (pp. 364-370)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 364-366) Progressive income taxes existed before 1914 in many countries but with top rates below 10%. (pp. 365-366) Top rates jumped only after the war (France 2% to 50% in 1920; Prussia 4% to 40%; US to 77%; UK 8% to over 40%). (p. 365) The French law of 1920 was passed by the most right-wing Chamber, whose members had opposed the 2% rate. (p. 367) The Third Republic had universal male suffrage for decades without embracing progressivity. (p. 369) The German estate tax stalemate of 1906–1909 was broken only in 1919.
- **Conclusion**: Progressive taxation was "as much a product of two world wars as it was of democracy" (p. 364); and later, "It was war that gave rise to progressive taxation, not the natural consequences of universal suffrage." (p. 377)
- **Note**: the conclusion hardens across the chapter. On p. 364 war and democracy are co-causes and "a movement had clearly been launched" before the war (p. 366); by p. 377 war is the cause. Piketty's note 26 cites Scheve and Stasavage (2012) on war and inheritance taxation as support (p. 491).

**C. Confiscatory rates as a liberal instrument (p. 370)**

- **Premises**: (p. 370) Rates of 70–80% raise little revenue, so their purpose is to end or discourage very high incomes and estates. (p. 370) They involve no prohibition or expropriation and are set by democratic rules.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 370) The progressive tax is "a relatively liberal method for reducing inequality" and "an ideal compromise between social justice and individual freedom".
- **Unstated premise**: that discouraging an income through price (tax) is categorically different from prohibiting it, even at rates where the effect is the same. He signals this by calling the US invention "confiscatory" while classing it as liberal.

**D. Falling top rates caused rising top pay through bargaining, not productivity (pp. 373-376)**

- **Premises**: (p. 373) Across developed countries the fall in top rates since 1980 is "perfectly correlated" with the rise in the top centile's share. (pp. 373-374) The marginal-productivity story (lower rates spur effort, raising productivity and pay) runs into the difficulties shown in ch09. (p. 374) If productivity had risen, growth should differ; but there is no statistically significant relation between rate cuts and per capita growth, and the US and UK have not grown faster than Germany, France, Japan, Denmark, or Sweden since 1980. (p. 374) When 80–90% of a raise went to the state, executives had little reason to fight for it; after 1980 they did, and individual contributions are hard to measure while compensation committees are "incestuous". (p. 376) Pay responds more to luck than to talent, and more so where top rates are low.
- **Conclusion**: Rising executive pay is "fairly well explained by the bargaining model" (p. 375) and "does not have much to do with a hypothetical increase in managerial productivity" (p. 375); only dissuasive taxation can stop it (p. 376).
- **Unstated premises**: that the direction of causation runs from rates to pay. Piketty himself raises political capture by the 1% (p. 377), which would run the other way (rising top incomes buying lower rates). The macro growth test assumes that a productivity effect at the top would be large enough to show up in national growth rates.

**E. Optimal top rate above 80% (pp. 376-377)**

- **Premises**: (p. 376) Bargaining, not productivity, drives top pay, so confiscatory rates reduce "economically useless (or even harmful)" pay without reducing output (p. 376). Piketty's note 40 says the labor supply elasticity can be ruled out above 0.1–0.2 (p. 493). (p. 377) Executives will not flee en masse; historical and firm-level data contradict it.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 376) The optimal top rate is "probably above 80 percent" on the top 1% or 0.5% (82% in Piketty's note 50) (p. 494); (p. 377) an 80% rate on incomes above $500,000 or $1 million in the US, plus 50–60% above $200,000 for revenue.
- **Design**: Piketty's note 51 separates two functions: confiscatory 80–90% rates on the top 0.5–1% "would end indecent and useless compensation", while 50–60% rates on the top 5–10% raise revenue (p. 494).
- **Qualification**: (p. 376) the estimate's precision is not to be trusted; rates are for democratic deliberation; social norms may influence pay independently of tax.

**Position versus argument.** The historical record (when and how rates rose and fell) is well documented and separable from the policy claim. The policy claim rests almost entirely on the Piketty-Saez-Stantcheva paper (cited as forthcoming), summarised in a few sentences; the reader of this chapter cannot evaluate its identification strategy.

## Evidence offered

- **French incidence study, 2010** (p. 363): overall taxes 47% of national income; bottom 50% pay 40–45%, next 40% pay 45–50%, top 0.1% pay 35%. Source: Landais, Piketty and Saez, *Pour une révolution fiscale* (2010), in Piketty's note 4 (p. 488). Piketty's note 5 says the estimate omits income in tax havens and assumes tax shelters are equally common at all levels, so it probably overstates the effective top rate (pp. 488-489). The generalisation to Europe and the US is "All signs are" (p. 363), not a study.
- **Estate taxation in France**: average estate and gift tax "barely 5 percent", 20% for the top centile of inheritances (Piketty's note 7) (p. 489).
- **Figure 14.1** (top income tax rates, US, UK, Germany, France, 1900–2013) and **Figure 14.2** (top inheritance tax rates, same countries) (p. 365), (p. 369). Sources: online appendix only ("piketty.pse.ens.fr/capital21c"). Captions: US top income rate 70% (1980) to 28% (1988) (p. 365); US top inheritance rate 70% (1980) to 35% (2013) (p. 369). Piketty's note 15 says French rates exclude the 25% surcharge on the childless (pp. 489-490); notes 25 and 32 say the estate rates are for direct-line heirs (pp. 491-492).
- **Dates of adoption** of progressive income taxes: Denmark 1870, Japan 1887, Prussia 1891, Sweden 1903, Britain 1909, US 1913, France July 15, 1914 (p. 364).
- **Rate histories**: France 2% (1914), 50% (1920), 60% (1924), 72% (1925) (p. 365); Prussia 3% (1891–1914), 4% (1915–1918), 40% (1919–1920); US 67% then 77% in 1918–1919; Britain 8% (1909), over 40% after the war (p. 366); French tax on financial income 3% (1872–1890), 4% (1890–1914) (p. 367); French estate tax top rate 5% (1902–1910), 6.5% (1911–1914), effective rate on top centile of estates 3% after 1901 vs 1% before (p. 368); German estate tax on family bequests 0% to 35% in 1919 (p. 369); British top estate rate 8% (1896) to 15% (1908) (pp. 369-370); US top income rate 25% (late 1920s), 63% (1933), 79% (1937), 88% (1942), 94% (1944), about 90% to the mid-1960s, 70% early 1980s, average 81% 1932–1980 (p. 371), with state income taxes of 5–10% on top (Piketty's note 30) (p. 492); Germany 90% in 1947–1949 under occupation (p. 371); Japan 85% under occupation, 55% in 1950 (Piketty's note 31) (p. 492); Britain 98% in 1941–1952 and 1974–1978 (Piketty's note 33) (p. 492), with 83% on labour income when capital income faced 98% (Piketty's note 34) (p. 492). Most rate series sit in Piketty's own earlier work (*Les hauts revenus en France*, 2001) and the online appendix.
- **Historical testimony**: Leroy-Beaulieu's *Essai* (1881) as an example of data-free argument (p. 368), with Piketty's note 21 dissecting his indigence statistics (pp. 490-491); Joseph Caillaux in 1907–1908 conceding from estate statistics that "a small number of people possess the bulk of this country’s wealth" (Piketty's note 24) (p. 491); Willford King (1915) on US concentration (p. 370); Irving Fisher's 1919 AEA presidential address, proposing estate taxes of two-thirds rising to 100% after three generations, inspired by Rignano (p. 371) (Piketty's note 29) (pp. 491-492); Josiah Wedgwood (1929, preface 1938) and Bertrand Russell on "plutodemocracies" (pp. 372-373).
- **Cross-country correlation** of top-rate cuts with top-share increases: eighteen OECD countries in the WTID, from Piketty, Saez and Stantcheva, "Optimal Taxation of Top Labor Incomes: A Tale of Three Elasticities" (forthcoming), fig. 3 (Piketty's note 38) (p. 493). The text's "perfectly correlated" (p. 373) is not given a coefficient.
- **Growth comparison**: no significant relation between top-rate cuts and per capita growth, robust to start year (Piketty's note 39) (p. 493); US per capita GDP 20% higher in level but growing at about the same rate as Europe (pp. 374-375), the level gap due to hours worked (Piketty's note 42) (p. 493); US per capita growth 2.3% (1950–1970), 2.2% (1970–1990), 1.4% (1990–2012) (Piketty's note 44) (p. 493). Piketty warns that comparisons over a few years can justify anything and need ten to twenty years (p. 374) (Piketty's note 41) (p. 493).
- **Executive pay database**: nearly three thousand listed firms in fourteen countries (Piketty's note 46) (p. 494), supporting the luck/talent and tax-rate findings (pp. 375-376).
- **Rival explanations named and rejected**: Gabaix and Landier (2008) on firm size, rejected because firm size grew similarly everywhere but pay did not and their data are US-only (Piketty's note 47) (p. 494); Rajan and Zingales, Acemoglu-Robinson-Verdier, Grusky on competition (Piketty's note 48) (p. 494); Acemoglu-Robinson-Verdier on US innovation spilling abroad, which Piketty calls "essentially theoretical" and based on patents per capita (Piketty's note 45) (pp. 493-494); the claim that 1950–1980 executives took pay in kind, which he says the evidence contradicts (Piketty's note 49) (p. 494).
- **Political capture literature**: Hacker and Pierson, Schlozman-Verba-Brady, Noah (Piketty's note 52) (p. 494). **Skill-gap literature** criticised for relying on surveys: Goldin and Katz, Blank, Rajan (Piketty's note 53) (p. 494).
- **Bare assertion**: that the flight of executives to Canada and Mexico is "devoid of common sense" and contradicted by "all the firm-level data at our disposal" (p. 377), with no data shown; that US politicians are wealthier than European ones, offered with the admission that "data on this are sparse" (p. 378).

## Proposals: design, justification, objections

- **Top marginal income tax rate**
  - *Design*: about 80% on incomes above $500,000 or $1 million (top 0.5–1%) (p. 376); 50–60% above $200,000 for revenue (p. 377). Optimal estimate 82% (Piketty's note 50) (p. 494).
  - *Justification*: top pay above that level is bargained, not produced, so taxing it away costs no output and spreads growth more widely (p. 376); restore progressivity needed for fiscal consent (p. 363) and for public support of globalization (p. 364).
  - *Evidence*: the Piketty-Saez-Stantcheva cross-country and firm-level results (pp. 373-376) (Piketty's notes 38-40, 46) (pp. 493-494).
  - *Objections anticipated*: (1) marginal-productivity / supply-side: answered with the growth comparison and luck/talent results (pp. 373-376); (2) flight of talent: answered by country size and history (p. 377); (3) competition and governance could cure excessive pay: "seems unrealistic" (p. 376); (4) precision of the estimate: conceded, deferred to democratic deliberation (p. 376); (5) small open economies: conceded, deferred to international coordination in ch15 (pp. 376-377).
- **More steeply progressive tax on the bottom 90%**: instead of 40–45% / 45–50% for the bottom 50% / next 40%, 30–35% / 50–55% (Piketty's note 9) (p. 489); justified by the effect of globalization on the least skilled (pp. 363-364).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch09**: the rejection of marginal productivity at the top (pp. 373-374) and the luck/talent finding (p. 376) are explicitly carried over from ch09. The top-rate argument is only as strong as ch09's critique.
- **Depends on ch11**: Rastignac's dilemma and the lifetime-resources criterion (p. 363) come from ch11; so does the claim that inheritance is gaining importance.
- **Depends on ch10**: the claim that progressive taxation of top incomes and estates "partly explains" why concentration never returned to Belle Époque levels (p. 362) is the policy side of ch10's account of why 1914–1945 broke concentration.
- **Feeds ch15**: three lines lead straight into the capital tax: (a) capital income escaping the income tax through competition (p. 362); (b) the knowledge-producing function of even a low tax (p. 369), which becomes the 0.1% "compulsory reporting" tax in ch15 (p. 382); (c) the deferral of small-country coordination to "the next chapter" (p. 377).
- **Contradicted in ch15 (my observation)**: the closing prophecy that the US "may be on the verge of becoming the Old Europe" (p. 378) is walked back in ch15, where immigration keeps the US "still a long way" from it in ch15 (p. 398), referring back to this chapter explicitly.
- **Hardening of the causal claim (my observation)**: p. 362 says the fall in progressivity "probably explains much" of the rise in top earned incomes; p. 376 says only dissuasive taxation "can do the job". The hedge disappears once the PSS results are introduced, though p. 376 itself acknowledges social norms as an independent factor.
- **Causation versus capture (my observation)**: Argument D treats tax cuts as the cause of rising top pay; p. 377 raises the possibility that the 1% captured the US political process. If capture is real, the correlation on p. 373 is partly reverse causation, which the chapter does not address.
- **War versus deliberation (my observation)**: the historical thesis is that war, not universal suffrage, created progressive taxation (p. 377); the policy thesis relies on "collective deliberation and democratic experimentation" (p. 376) and on "ideas and intellectual debate" (p. 377). Piketty acknowledges the gap ("natural optimism", p. 377) but does not resolve it. This is the book's general problem of agency for its proposals; compare ch16 and ch17.
- **"Liberal" confiscation (my observation)**: p. 370 calls confiscatory rates liberal because they work through incentives under democratic rules. The same move recurs in ch15, where the capital tax is "the liberal form of capital control" in ch15 (p. 397). Useful for a cross-book theme against Hayek (road-to-serfdom ch06 treats general tax rules aimed at distributive outcomes as the pressure point of the Rule of Law).
- **Revenue versus regulation**: confiscatory rates are explicitly not for revenue (p. 370), (p. 377); revenue comes from 50–60% rates lower down. The same split between regulating and financing reappears in ch15 (p. 381).

## Open questions

- What does the PSS paper's identification actually rest on? The chapter gives only "perfectly correlated" (p. 373) across eighteen countries and a firm-level panel (Piketty's note 46) (p. 494). How are reverse causation (capture) and common shocks (globalization, finance) handled? Check the paper and the online appendix.
- Is the macro growth test (p. 374) powerful enough? Differences of "a few tenths of a percent" (p. 375) are what is at issue, and Piketty admits such comparisons are hard to draw precisely (p. 375). Absence of a significant relation is not evidence of absence.
- The French incidence study (p. 363) is Piketty's own co-authored work and, by note 5, probably overstates top rates (pp. 488-489). Have later incidence studies (e.g., for the US) confirmed a regressive top?
- Does the war thesis (pp. 364-370), (p. 377) survive Piketty's own pre-war examples: Denmark, Japan, Prussia, Sweden, Britain, the US all adopted progressive taxes in peacetime "after due deliberation" (p. 364)? The thesis is about rates, not adoption; keep that distinction in the synthesis.
- What is the base for the 80% rate: labour income only (the PSS paper is about "top labor incomes", Piketty's note 38) (p. 493) or total income? The chapter's earlier argument (p. 362), (p. 372) wants capital income taxed at least as heavily.
- How does the claim that 80% rates would lift pay "at lower levels" (p. 377) work? It is asserted as a consequence of the bargaining model but no mechanism or evidence is given.
- If the US is "big enough" to tax alone (p. 377) and executive flight is implausible, why is capital flight so central in ch15? The answer presumably is that capital is more mobile than people; check ch15's treatment.
