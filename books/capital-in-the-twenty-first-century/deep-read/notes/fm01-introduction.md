# fm01: Introduction (pp. 2–31)

## Question this chapter answers

Does private capital accumulation drive wealth into ever fewer hands (Marx), or do growth, competition and technical progress eventually compress inequality (Kuznets)? Piketty's answer, stated up front and then defended across the book: neither. Modern growth and the diffusion of knowledge avoided the Marxist apocalypse, but did not change the "deep structures of capital and inequality" (p. 3). The twentieth-century compression was an accident of war and policy, not a law of development, and whenever the return on capital (r) exceeds the growth rate (g) for long periods, inherited wealth tends to outrun earned income and concentration reaches levels incompatible with meritocratic democracy (pp. 3, 22-23). The Introduction also sets out what the book counts as evidence (tax, estate and national-wealth sources), the minimal formal apparatus (α = r × β, β = s / g, r > g), and the normative standard (inequality is acceptable only if "founded only upon common utility" (p. 27)).

| r > g | pp. 22-23 | The "fundamental inequality" and "main force of divergence in my theory" (pp. 23-24). Not a market imperfection: the more perfect the capital market, the more likely it holds (p. 24). More likely when g is low, "though not automatic" (p. 22). |

| Diffusion of knowledge | p. 19 | The main force for both productivity growth and reduced inequality, within and between countries; "the public good par excellence—rather than a market mechanism" (p. 19). Depends on education policy and institutions (p. 20). |

## Leading propositions

- The book claims answers that are "imperfect and incomplete" but rest on data covering three centuries and more than twenty countries, plus a new theoretical framework (p. 3).
- When r exceeds g, as in the nineteenth century and likely again in the twenty-first, capitalism "automatically generates arbitrary and unsustainable inequalities" that undermine meritocratic values (p. 3).
- Democracy can regain control over capitalism without protectionism or nationalism; the policy chapters aim at this (p. 3).
- Debate on distribution has long rested on "an abundance of prejudice and a paucity of fact" (p. 3). Novels (Austen, Balzac) carry real intuitive knowledge about the hierarchy of wealth (pp. 3-4).
- There will always be a subjective, political dimension to inequality that no science can remove; social science can still inform debate and "unmask certain preconceived or fraudulent notions" (p. 4).
- Malthus and Young read fragmentary evidence through fear of revolution; Young's account was "overdetermined by his fear of revolution in France" (pp. 5-6).
- Ricardo's land-rent prediction failed because agriculture's share of income fell, but his scarcity principle stays relevant for urban property and oil (pp. 6-7).
- Marx's prediction failed because wages rose from the late nineteenth century and because he "totally neglected the possibility of durable technological progress" (p. 10). His accumulation principle still holds a "key insight": under low growth accumulated wealth can reach destabilising proportions (p. 10).
- Kuznets's 1913–1948 US series were accurate and well documented (pp. 11-12, 15), but his 1955 curve was speculative, driven by Cold War aims, and wrong about the cause: the 1914–1945 compression was due to wars and shocks, not intersectoral mobility (pp. 13-14).
- US income concentration in the 2000s regained, and slightly exceeded, the level of the 1910s (p. 14).
- Economists neglected distribution because of Kuznets's optimism and the "undue enthusiasm" for representative-agent models (p. 15).
- First major result: distrust economic determinism. The 1910–1950 fall in inequality came from war and the policies adopted to cope with it; the post-1980 rise came largely from political shifts in taxation and finance (p. 19).
- Second major result, "the heart of the book": wealth distribution has forces pushing both toward convergence and divergence, and "no natural, spontaneous process" stops the divergent forces from prevailing permanently (p. 19).
- The US rise in top income shares is mainly an explosion of very high *labour* incomes for top managers; Piketty prefers the explanation that they largely set their own pay over a productivity explanation (pp. 21-22).
- The European U-curve in private wealth (6–7 years of income around 1910, 2–3 by 1950, 4–6 by 2010) is mainly explained by the return of slow growth (pp. 22-23).
- When r > g, inherited wealth grows faster than output; heirs need save only part of their capital income for it to outgrow the economy (p. 23).
- Divergence can be reinforced by saving rates rising with wealth, returns rising with fortune size, arbitrary returns, and Ricardian scarcity (p. 23).
- Piketty's model is less apocalyptic than Marx's: divergence is "not perpetual and is only one of several possible future directions" (p. 23).
- r > g "has nothing to do with any market imperfection"; a progressive global tax on capital could counter it but would need international coordination (p. 24).
- The book relies mainly on the US, Japan, Germany, France and Britain; France matters for its estate records, its early demographic transition, and its test of legal equality (pp. 24-26).
- French data show wealth was as concentrated under the Republic as in aristocratic Britain, so "equality of rights in the marketplace cannot ensure equality of rights tout court" (p. 26).
- Piketty frames economics as a social science: he attacks the discipline's "childish passion for mathematics" (p. 27) and calls the book "as much a work of history as of economics" (p. 28).
- Income, capital, growth and r are "theoretical constructs rather than mathematical certainties"; a small set of equations is enough to account for the history (pp. 28-29).
- The title is provisional: logically it should have been "Capital at the Dawn of the Twenty-First Century", and the future is "deeply political, chaotic, and unpredictable" (p. 30).

## Arguments

**A. Kuznets's curve rested on an accident (pp. 11-14)**

- **Premises**: (p. 12) Kuznets's series show the top decile falling from 45–50% of US national income to 30–35% between 1913 and 1948. (p. 13) Kuznets knew the compression was "largely accidental", due to the Depression and World War II, and warned against generalising in 1953. (p. 13) In the 1954 AEA presidential address he nonetheless suggested development itself would produce falling inequality. (p. 14) He stated the aim was to keep underdeveloped countries "within the orbit of the free world."
- **Further premise**: (p. 14) The sharp fall across almost all rich countries between 1914 and 1945 was "due above all to the world wars".
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 13-14) The curve was "formulated in large part for the wrong reasons", its empirical base was "extremely fragile", and the post-1970s rise in inequality refutes the automatic-decline story (p. 14).
- **Assessment**: The empirical half (what caused the 1914–1945 compression) is argued in later chapters (ch08, ch10, ch11), not here. The motive argument, that Kuznets was pushing Cold War optimism, is a genetic critique: it explains why the curve spread but does not by itself refute it. Piketty relies on the historical record (US inequality back at 1910s levels by the 2000s, p. 14) to do the refuting.

**B. Why r > g produces divergence (pp. 22-23)** (reconstruction)

- **Premises**: (p. 22) In slowly growing economies a small flow of new saving is enough to raise the stock of wealth relative to income. (pp. 22-23) r is the average return on capital, g the growth of income. (p. 23) "People with inherited wealth need save only a portion of their income from capital to see that capital grow more quickly than the economy as a whole."
- **Reconstruction**: A fortune W earning r, whose owner consumes a fraction c of the return, grows at r(1 − c). Average income grows at g. The fortune grows relative to the economy whenever r(1 − c) > g, i.e. whenever the owner reinvests a share of returns greater than g/r. With r = 5% and g = 1%, the heir can consume up to four-fifths of the return and still outpace the economy. The divergence is in the ratio of inherited wealth to income, not necessarily in the wealth of every dynasty; taxes, division among heirs and shocks all lower the effective r.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 23) Under r > g "it is almost inevitable that inherited wealth will dominate wealth amassed from a lifetime’s labor by a wide margin", and capital concentration will reach extreme levels.
- **Unstated premises**: that the r relevant to large fortunes is at least the average r (Piketty in fact suggests it is higher, p. 23); that r does not fall enough as β rises to close the gap (taken up in ch06); that the economy-wide r is what inheritors earn after tax. Each is examined later in the book.

**C. r > g is not a market imperfection (p. 24)**

- **Premises**: (p. 24) The fundamental inequality is the main force of divergence. (p. 24) A more perfect capital market makes r > g more likely.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 24) The remedy cannot be better markets; it must be public institutions such as a progressive global tax on capital.
- **Assessment**: The middle premise is asserted, not argued, in the Introduction. One reading: perfect markets let owners earn the full marginal product and diversify risk, so r stays high, while frictions (expropriation, poor information) depress it. The case rests on ch10's argument that r > g held through most of history.

**D. Why France is the key case (pp. 25-26)**

- **Premises**: (p. 25) Post-1790s French estate records are probably the richest long-run wealth source in the world. (p. 25) France's population merely doubled after 1789 while the US grew a hundredfold; fast population growth weakens inheritance. (p. 25) World population will not grow a hundredfold over the next two centuries.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 25-26) The French case is "more typical and more pertinent for understanding the future" than the US case.
- **Assessment**: This rests on the demographic premise of ch02 (pp. 62-63) that rapid population growth dilutes inherited wealth. It is a projection: the US is treated as the exception, France as the norm for a low-growth world.

## Evidence offered

- **US top decile share, 1910–2010 (Figure I.1, pp. 21-22).** Extends Kuznets's 1913–1948 series using federal income tax returns plus national income estimates. Top decile 45–50% in the 1910s–1920s, 30–35% by the late 1940s, stable 1950–1970, back to 45–50% by 2000 (p. 21). Provenance: Piketty and Saez (2003), endnote 22 (p. 434); data at the online appendix (p. 21). Kuznets's own source: *Shares of Upper Income Groups in Income and Savings* (1953), endnote 11 (p. 433).
- **European capital/income ratio, 1870–2010 (Figure I.2, pp. 22-23).** Private wealth net of debt in Britain, France and Germany, in years of national income: about 6–7 in 1910, 2–3 in 1950, 4–6 in 2010 (p. 23). Provenance: Piketty and Zucman (2013), endnote 31 (p. 435); extends Goldsmith's national balance sheets (p. 18; endnote 32, p. 435).
- **Three source families (pp. 15-18).** (1) Income tax returns plus national accounts, compiled in the WTID by about thirty researchers; series start when an income tax began, mostly 1910–1920, earlier in Japan and Germany (p. 16). (2) Estate tax returns, following Lampman (US 1922–1956) and Atkinson and Harrison (Britain 1923–1972); French estate data go back to the Revolution, built with Postel-Vinay and Rosenthal (p. 17; endnotes 27-28, p. 434). (3) National wealth estimates, back to the early eighteenth century for Britain and France (pp. 17-18).
- **Why tax records rather than surveys.** Endnote 19: household surveys rarely go back before 1970 and "tend to seriously underestimate higher incomes" (p. 434); tax records cover high incomes and a century of history. Endnote 12 (p. 433): national accounts are needed for the denominator because only part of the population files returns.
- **Wage stagnation, early nineteenth century (p. 8).** Wages stagnated from the 1800s to the 1850s in Britain and France while growth accelerated, and the capital share rose. Endnote 5 (p. 432) cites Robert Allen's term for this, Engels’s pause, from his 2007 and 2009 papers. Piketty says this is estimated "with the imperfect sources available today" (p. 8).
- **France vs Britain wealth concentration around 1900 (p. 26).** Asserted here; evidence deferred to Part Three.
- **Population figures (pp. 5, 25).** France 20 million in 1700 vs Britain 8 million; France about 30 million in 1780; US 3 million at independence, 100 million by 1900, above 300 million today.
- **Stock-market capitalisation (p. 25).** Rich countries regained the market capitalisation relative to GDP of Paris and London in the early 1900s only in the twenty-first century. No source given here.
- **Source caveat.** Piketty admits that estate data exist for fewer countries than income data (p. 17), that long-term trends are "far better documented in the rich countries" (p. 24), and that pre-1910 income-inequality series do not exist (p. 17).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Two divergence mechanisms, one headline.** The Introduction calls r > g the summary of "the overall logic" (p. 23), yet its first exhibit (Figure I.1, US top decile) is explained by top managers' labour income, which has nothing to do with r > g (pp. 21-22). Piketty says plainly that the two figures reflect "two distinct underlying phenomena" (p. 21). Readers should keep the US income story (ch08–ch09) apart from the European wealth story (ch03–ch05, ch10–ch11). r > g explains wealth concentration; it does not explain the rise in US top income shares.
- **Political vs mechanical explanation.** The first result says inequality history is "deeply political" (p. 19); the second says there are mechanisms (r > g, β = s / g) that operate automatically (pp. 19, 23). The book has to show how much of the 1914–1945 fall was political choice (taxation, rent control, nationalisation) and how much was physical destruction and slow growth. See ch03–ch05 for the decomposition of β's fall and ch10 for after-tax r vs g.
- **Marx caricature.** Piketty says Marx's theory implicitly assumes zero productivity growth (p. 23) and that Marx "totally neglected" technical progress (p. 10). Marx is in fact usually read as stressing technical change (rising organic composition of capital). Piketty's own reconstruction of Marx's falling-rate-of-profit dilemma (p. 9) depends on β rising without limit, which only follows when g → 0. ch06 returns to Marx.
- **r measured or inferred?** r is defined as an average across all capital (pp. 22-23) but is in practice inferred from α and β (ch01, pp. 42-43). Whether r > g is an observed fact or a modelled quantity depends on later evidence (ch06, ch10, Figures 10.9–10.10).
- **β = s / g stated, not derived.** The Introduction attributes the return of high β to slower growth (p. 22) before the second law has been shown. The derivation, and its "long run" qualifier, come in ch05. Also, β in Figure I.2 is measured at market value, so asset-price changes (capital gains) also move it; ch03–ch05 have to separate volume from price effects.
- **The normative standard.** "Common utility" (p. 27) is left vague on purpose. ch13 returns to it, and the meritocracy argument in ch11 depends on it.
- **Demography and inheritance.** The case for France as the typical future (pp. 25-26) depends on ch02's claim that population growth dilutes inherited wealth (pp. 62-63) and on ch11's inheritance flow series.
- **Kuznets's top-decile drop.** The text says the fall of "nearly 10 percentage points" was equal to half the income of the poorest 50% (p. 12), but its own figures (45–50% falling to 30–35%) imply about 15 points, and endnote 13 says the bottom 90% rose from 50–55% to 65–70% (p. 433). A small internal inconsistency in the arithmetic.

## Open questions

- Is r in r > g pre-tax or post-tax, and is it the economy-wide average or the return on large fortunes? The Introduction's definition is pre-tax and average (pp. 22-23); the divergence argument needs the return actually earned and kept by heirs.
- What evidence supports the claim that a more perfect capital market raises the likelihood of r > g (p. 24)? Nothing in the Introduction backs it.
- How much of the post-1950 rise in β is volume (saving relative to growth) and how much is relative asset prices? The answer bears on whether β = s / g or Ricardo's scarcity principle (pp. 6-7) explains the U-curve.
- Is the managerial-power explanation of US top pay (p. 22) tested against alternatives (superstar effects, financialisation, tax incentives)? See ch09 and ch14.
- How robust is the WTID's homogenisation of tax data across changing tax laws and avoidance (p. 16, "corrected in various ways")? Details are in the online appendix, not the book.
