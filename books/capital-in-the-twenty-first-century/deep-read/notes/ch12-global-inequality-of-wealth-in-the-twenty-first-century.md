# ch12: Global Inequality of Wealth in the Twenty-First Century (pp. 313–342)

## Question this chapter answers

Will financial globalization concentrate capital more than ever before, and if so, in whose hands: the world's billionaires, the petroleum states, or China (p. 313)? Piketty's answer has two parts. First, returns on capital rise with the size of the fortune, which adds a second divergence force on top of r > g and lets the largest fortunes grow at 6–7 percent a year in real terms (pp. 313-314, 316). Second, the threat that matters is "oligarchic divergence", in which every country comes to be owned by its own and the world's richest people. "International divergence", in which rich countries are bought up by oil funds or China, is less likely and less dangerous (pp. 338-339). Only a progressive tax on capital, coordinated at least regionally, can check the oligarchic kind (pp. 320, 340).

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |

| Inequality of returns on capital (unequal returns) | p. 313 | The hypothesis, then the claim, that wealthier owners get a higher average return than less wealthy ones. Illustration: if the average is 4 percent, the wealthy may get 6–7 and the less wealthy 2–3 (p. 314). | HNWI | p. 318 | "High net worth individuals", the millionaires studied by financial institutions (p. 318). |

## Leading propositions

- Wealthier people plausibly get higher average returns, mainly through economies of scale in portfolio management (pp. 313-314).
- Unequal returns can drive the distribution of capital to diverge without limit, and compound interest makes this fast. Unequal returns amplify r > g (p. 314).
- Growth is the only "natural" brake. With global growth around 3.5 percent (1990–2012, perhaps to 2030), divergence at the top stays moderate. When catch-up ends and growth slows, inequality of returns will matter far more (pp. 314-315).
- In the long run, unequal wealth within nations is more worrying than unequal wealth between nations (p. 315).
- Magazine rankings are biased and methodologically weak, but in the absence of official data they must be used and improved, not ignored (p. 315).
- Read with fixed fractiles, the Forbes data show the top 1/20 million's average wealth growing 6.4 percent a year in real terms (1987–2013) and the top 1/100 million's growing 6.8 percent. Average wealth per adult grew 2.1 percent and average income 1.4 percent (p. 316).
- The result depends on the endpoints: 1990–2010 gives about 4 percent. But in every window the largest fortunes grow at least about twice as fast as the average fortune (pp. 316-317).
- Billionaires hold only a little over 1 percent of global wealth, so the rankings leave out nearly 99 percent of capital (p. 317).
- From combined estimates, global wealth inequality in the early 2010s is comparable to Europe in 1900–1910: the top 0.1 percent own nearly 20 percent, the top 1 percent about 50 percent, the top 10 percent 80–90 percent, and the bottom half less than 5 percent (p. 318).
- These figures are "highly uncertain", orders of magnitude only (pp. 318-319). Much of the concentration is international, and catch-up may for now outweigh divergence. Whether global wealth inequality is rising is not settled (p. 319).
- Divergence at the top is nevertheless already powerful, probably for fortunes of 10–100 million euros as well as for billionaires (p. 319).
- Illustration: a 6 percent return for the top thousandth against 2 percent growth in average wealth would triple its share to 60 percent in thirty years. At 4 percent it would nearly double to about 40 percent (p. 319).
- r > g, amplified by unequal returns, can produce "explosive trajectories". Only a progressive tax on capital can stop this (p. 320).
- Above a threshold, large fortunes grow at very high rates whether inherited or entrepreneurial, and whether or not the owner works. Gates and Bettencourt both grew at over 13 percent a year in nominal terms in 1990–2010, about 10–11 percent real (p. 320).
- Mechanism: very large fortunes can reinvest nearly all their income, because their owners' consumption is a tiny fraction of capital (p. 320; n. 16, p. 478).
- Wealth "is not just a matter of merit": dozens of inherited fortunes exceed Steve Jobs's (p. 321).
- Magazine methods underestimate inherited fortunes. Journalists start from listed companies, while heirs often hold diversified portfolios and avoid publicity. Counting families as one unit biases the other way (pp. 321-322).
- Forbes has an ideological bias towards celebrating entrepreneurs. Its owner is himself an heir (p. 322).
- Inherited wealth probably makes up more than half of the largest fortunes worldwide, "60–70 percent" as a realistic a priori estimate, against 80–90 percent in Belle Époque France (p. 323).
- The entrepreneurial argument cannot justify every inequality. Fortunes outgrow any justification by social utility, and entrepreneurs become rentiers, even within one lifetime (p. 323).
- This is "the main justification" for a progressive annual tax on the largest fortunes worldwide, the only democratic way to control the process while preserving entrepreneurship and openness (p. 323).
- Uniform taxation is better than case-by-case moral judgment (Gates against Slim, Mittal, Lagardère, Obiang), which is arbitrary and often ethnocentric or racially coloured (pp. 324-326).
- The return on capital mixes entrepreneurial labour, luck and theft. The arbitrariness of accumulation is broader than the arbitrariness of inheritance (p. 326).
- US university endowments returned 8.2 percent a year in real terms, net of fees, in 1980–2010 (7.2 percent in 1990–2010) (p. 327).
- Returns rise with endowment size: 6.2 percent below $100 million, 8.8 percent above $1 billion, and 10.2 percent for Harvard, Yale and Princeton (1980–2010) (p. 327).
- The gradient comes from alternative investments, not from greater risk. Year-to-year volatility is no higher for the largest endowments (p. 328).
- Endowment growth comes mostly from returns. Alumni gifts are one-tenth to one-fifth of the annual return (p. 329).
- Caveats: the 1980–2010 asset-price rebound may not continue, scale economies may weaken for fortunes of 10–50 million euros, and families, unlike institutions, have prodigal children. So the distribution converges to an equilibrium instead of diverging without limit (p. 329).
- Those caveats are "not altogether reassuring". A modest r − g gap is enough for extreme inequality (p. 329), and trusts and foundations blur the line between mortal families and permanent institutions (pp. 329-330).
- Inflation does not lower the average return on capital, because asset prices track consumer prices. It redistributes returns, mainly towards the wealthiest, who can pay for management and get access to real assets (pp. 330-332).
- Modern inflation makes it harder for small savers to earn 3–4 percent real, as nineteenth-century government bonds once allowed, and housing is hard to buy without initial wealth (p. 332).
- Inflation is "an extremely blunt instrument" against rentiers. A progressive capital tax is better on transparency and efficacy (p. 333).
- Sovereign wealth funds: Norway publishes detailed reports (over 700 billion euros in 2013) and had lower returns. Abu Dhabi reports over 7 percent real (1990–2010). Saudi Arabia earned 2–3 percent, mainly in US Treasuries (pp. 333-335).
- Saudi Arabia's holdings of US Treasuries are plausibly partly a return on military protection: capital distribution is "at once economic, political, and military" (p. 335).
- In 2013 sovereign funds held about $5.3 trillion, roughly the same as the Forbes billionaires (about $5.4 trillion), each about 1.5 percent of global private wealth (p. 335).
- Oil funds also grow by investing new oil revenue. With natural resource rent at 5 percent of global GDP and possibly $200 oil, they could hold 10–20 percent or more of global capital by 2030–2040. At least two to three times today's share is "almost inevitable" (pp. 336-337).
- Large foreign ownership would likely provoke political reactions, up to expropriation (p. 337).
- China is a different case. Catch-up ends rapid growth, and under the central scenario capital/income ratios equalise across continents (Africa excepted). A Chinese buyout of the West would require persistently higher Chinese saving, for instance through funded pensions. That is possible but not plausible (pp. 337-338).
- Oligarchic divergence is more likely than international divergence and already under way. With r > g and returns rising with size, the top centile and thousandth will pull ahead (p. 339).
- Fears of Chinese ownership are "a pure fantasy". European households own about 70 trillion euros net, against about 3 trillion euros for China's sovereign funds plus reserves (p. 339).
- Foreign buyers explain barely 3 percent of the rise in Paris property prices (p. 339).
- The feeling of dispossession comes from concentration at home and the loss of democratic sovereignty over capital. Europe's small, competing states make this worse (pp. 339-340).
- Combating oligarchic divergence requires coordinated international action, at least at a broad regional level (p. 340).
- Officially, the rich countries' combined net foreign position is slightly negative (−4 percent of global GDP in 2010). The world as a whole is officially in net debt, which is impossible (pp. 340-341).
- Zucman estimates unreported tax-haven assets at nearly 10 percent of global GDP, probably a lower bound, at least three-quarters owned by rich-country residents. So the rich countries' true net position is positive, and the rise of private wealth and of top shares is larger than official accounts show (pp. 341-342).

## Arguments

**A. Unequal returns as a second divergence mechanism (pp. 313-315)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 313-314) Larger portfolios get economies of scale in management and can bear more risk. (p. 314) Wealth growing faster at the top for structural reasons makes concentration rise without limit, and compound interest makes this fast. (p. 314) Growth is the only non-governmental brake. (pp. 314-315) Global growth will slow once catch-up ends.
- **Unstated premises**: that returns are reinvested and not consumed or dissipated (made explicit for the very largest fortunes on p. 320); that scale effects hold across the range from 10 million euros to tens of billions (later doubted, p. 329); that higher measured returns are not compensation for risk (argued only for endowments, p. 328).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 314) Unequal returns are "a force for divergence" that amplifies r > g. (p. 315) In the long run they will matter more than inequality between nations.

**B. Reading the Forbes data (pp. 315-317)**

- **Premises**: (p. 315) Counts of billionaires in nominal dollars mislead because of inflation and growth. (p. 316) A fixed fraction of the adult population gives a comparable group over time. (p. 316) That group's average wealth grew 6.4–6.8 percent real, against 2.1 percent for average wealth per adult.
- **Qualification**: (pp. 316-317) Endpoint sensitivity: 1987–2013 runs from trough to peak (1990 was an asset-price peak and 2010 a low), and 1990–2010 gives about 4 percent.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 317) Whatever years are chosen, the largest fortunes grow at least about twice as fast as the average fortune.
- **Note**: the growth figures come from the online Table 12.1 (image only in this file) and Supplemental Table S12.1 (n. 4, p. 476). The comparison rests on Piketty's own estimate of global private wealth, about four years of global GDP (n. 1, p. 476).

**C. The top thousandth projection (pp. 319-320)**

- **Premises**: (p. 319) The top thousandth holds about 20 percent of global wealth. (p. 319) Suppose its fortunes return 6 percent while average wealth grows 2 percent. (p. 319) After thirty years its share more than triples, to 60 percent. At 4 percent it nearly doubles, to about 40 percent.
- **Check (my arithmetic)**: (1.06/1.02)^30 ≈ 3.2 and (1.04/1.02)^30 ≈ 1.8, so the numbers are internally correct. The scenario assumes full reinvestment, no taxes and no dissipation, and a return for 10-million-euro fortunes taken from billionaire and endowment evidence that he later says may not carry over to fortunes of 10–50 million euros (p. 329).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 320) Global wealth dynamics may follow "explosive trajectories". Only a progressive capital tax can stop this.
- **Political premise**: (p. 319) A 60 percent share is "hard to imagine" without repression or persuasion, and the impoverishment of the middle class would likely cause a violent reaction. This is asserted, not argued.

**D. Heirs grow as fast as entrepreneurs (pp. 320-321)**

- **Premises**: (p. 320) Gates went from $4 billion to $50 billion and Bettencourt from $2 billion to $25 billion (1990–2010, Forbes). Both grew over 13 percent a year nominal. (p. 320) Bettencourt "never worked a day in her life", and Gates's wealth kept growing after he stopped working. (p. 320) At this scale nearly all income can be reinvested.
- **Conclusion**: Once established, a fortune grows "according to a dynamic of its own" (p. 320), and wealth "is not just a matter of merit" (p. 321).
- **Unstated premise**: that two cases from a list the author calls careless and piecemeal (p. 320) show a general law. He says so himself: "one should be careful not to overestimate the precision" (p. 320). He offers the endowment data as the systematic test (p. 321).

**E. Inheritance share of top fortunes (pp. 321-323)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 321-322) Magazine methods start from listed company shareholdings and so miss diversified inherited portfolios. (p. 322) Family groups counted as single fortunes bias the other way. (pp. 322-323) Forbes's own split is about one third pure heirs, one third partial heirs, one third pure entrepreneurs.
- **Assumption**: (p. 323) Half of partial heirs' wealth is inherited, plus an allowance for the net downward bias.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 323) Inherited wealth is "more than half", and 60–70 percent "seems fairly realistic a priori".
- **Status**: an informed guess. The two biases run in opposite directions and are not quantified. The arithmetic from the Forbes split alone (one third plus one sixth) gives exactly one half, so everything above half rests on the assumed net underestimation of heirs.

**F. From the moral hierarchy of wealth to taxation (pp. 323-326)**

- **Premises**: (p. 323) Entrepreneurship matters, but fortunes can grow "beyond any possible rational justification in terms of social utility". (p. 323) Entrepreneurs become rentiers within a lifetime. (pp. 324-326) Case-by-case judgments of merit (Gates against Slim, Mittal, Lagardère, Obiang) are arbitrary, uninformed and often ethnocentric. (p. 326) Returns mix labour, luck and theft inextricably.
- **Conclusion**: "Every fortune is partially justified yet potentially excessive" (p. 323). A progressive capital tax treats all cases in a "supple, consistent, and predictable manner" (p. 324), and is "a less blunt and more systematic instrument" than the courts (p. 326).
- **Unstated premise**: that since merit and rent cannot be separated case by case, the right response is a schedule by size, not by origin. This assumes size correlates with the undeserved component, which follows from Argument A, not from the moral argument.

**G. Endowments show that returns rise with size (pp. 326-329)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 326-327) Endowment data are complete, audited, collected by NACUBO since 1979, and published by the institutions. (p. 327) Returns are net of fees, taxes and inflation. (p. 327) Returns rise monotonically with size, from 6.2 to 10.2 percent. (n. 27, p. 479) Universities are ranked by endowment at the start of each decade to avoid look-ahead bias. (p. 328) Volatility is similar across sizes, so the gap is not a risk premium. (pp. 328-329) Management costs fall as a percentage of assets with scale.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 329) Large initial endowments yield higher returns, a clear case of inequality of returns on capital.
- **Limits he concedes**: (p. 329) asset-price rebound, possible fading of scale effects lower down, family dissipation.
- **Limit not addressed (my observation)**: alternative assets such as private equity, real estate and unlisted stocks are valued by appraisal, not market prices, which smooths reported year-to-year returns. Similar measured volatility (p. 328) may therefore understate the real risk of the largest, most illiquid portfolios. Note 29 offers rank stability as confirmation (p. 479), which speaks to persistence, not risk.

**H. Inflation redistributes but does not reduce returns (pp. 330-333)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 330-331) With β = 6 and α = 30 percent, r = 5 percent. If asset prices rise with consumer prices, 2 percent inflation leaves r unchanged. (p. 331) Holding real assets escapes the inflation tax. (pp. 331-332) Access to good managers, high-minimum products and home ownership rises with wealth.
- **Conclusion**: Inflation redistributes returns "mainly to the detriment of the least wealthy" (p. 332). (p. 333) "Inflation does not do away with rent".
- **Status**: the redistribution claim is supported by mechanism and by the endowment results (p. 331). No distributional data on inflation's incidence are given.

**I. Oligarchic divergence matters more than international divergence (pp. 337-340)**

- **Premises**: (p. 337) Diffusion of knowledge equalises. China will slow once it catches up, and capital/income ratios converge in the central scenario. (p. 338) Funded Chinese pensions could buy the West, but Chinese society would prefer PAYGO. (p. 339) European household wealth is about 70 trillion euros against about 3 trillion euros for Chinese funds and reserves. (p. 339) r > g plus size-dependent returns favour the top thousandth everywhere.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 338-339) Oligarchic divergence is more probable and "much more difficult to combat" (p. 340). Fear of foreign ownership is largely misplaced, and its real source is the loss of democratic sovereignty over mobile capital (p. 340).
- **Exception**: (p. 338) the oil states are different. Windfalls far larger than tiny populations need, combined with r > g, make "endless accumulation" possible.

**J. The rich countries are not net debtors (pp. 340-342)**

- **Premises**: (p. 340) The official rich-country net position is about −4 percent of global GDP. (p. 341) The world as a whole is officially a net debtor. (p. 341) Zucman: unreported tax-haven assets are about 10 percent of global GDP, more than twice the official deficit. (p. 341) At least three-quarters belong to rich-country residents.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 342) The rich countries' true net position is positive, and private wealth and top shares are underestimated in official accounts.

## Evidence offered

- **Forbes global billionaire list, 1987–2013** (pp. 315-317). About 140 billionaires in 1987 and 1,400 in 2013. Total wealth rose from $300 billion to $5,400 billion (Figure 12.1, p. 316). Billionaires per 100 million adults rose from 5 to 30, and their share of private wealth from 0.4 to 1.5 percent (Figure 12.2, p. 316). Top fractile shares rose from 0.3 to 0.9 percent (1/20 million) and from 0.1 to 0.4 percent (1/100 million) (Figure 12.3, p. 317). Provenance: Forbes journalists, by Piketty's account working from "all kinds of sources" (p. 315), "collected in a somewhat careless and piecemeal fashion" (p. 320), starting from listed company shareholdings, by telephone and e-mail (p. 321). Piketty's processing is in the online appendix (Table 12.1, Supplemental Table S12.1; n. 4, p. 476). The denominators (global private wealth of about $340 trillion, four years of global GDP) are his own estimates (n. 1, p. 476).
- **Extrapolation in note 5** (p. 476): if the 1987–2013 rate of divergence applies to the 1,400 billionaires, their share goes from 1.5 percent (2013) to 7.2 percent (2050) and 59.6 percent (2100). A mechanical projection.
- **Global wealth reports** (pp. 317-318): Crédit Suisse annual report since 2010, Merrill Lynch, Allianz. Note 8 says they draw on Davies, Sandström, Shorrocks and Wolff (Economic Journal 2011) (p. 477). Note 9 says country distributions are dated and updated mainly with aggregate national-accounts data (p. 477). The headline global shares (top 0.1 percent about 20 percent, top 1 percent about 50, top 10 percent 80–90, bottom half under 5) come from comparing "the various available estimates" of this kind (p. 318). Piketty calls them orders of magnitude (p. 319). Note 13 adds the top ten-thousandth at about 50 million euros average and about 10 percent of global wealth (p. 477). Note 13 says "450 adults out of 45 billion"; 450,000 out of 4.5 billion must be meant (p. 477). This looks like a typographical slip.
- **Individual fortunes** (pp. 320-321, 324-326): Gates, Bettencourt, Jobs (about $8 billion in 2011), Slim, Tsutsumi and Mori, Mittal, Lagardère, Obiang. All Forbes figures or press reports. Note 21 quotes Capital magazine on Mittal's London homes (pp. 478-479). Note 20 criticises Acemoglu and Robinson's Why Nations Fail for asserting the relative merits of Slim and Gates "without any precise factual basis" (p. 478).
- **Literary sources as illustration**: Balzac's Goriot and Birotteau (pp. 320-321), Alexei Tolstoy's Ibiscus (p. 326). These illustrate; they are not evidence.
- **Challenges magazine (France)** (p. 322): catalogues only "business-related fortunes" with undefined thresholds. Note 18 compares it with wealth-tax declarations and finds too few fortunes in the 50–500 million euro range (p. 478).
- **US university endowments** (pp. 326-329): about 800–850 institutions holding nearly $400 billion in 2010 (mean under $500 million, median under $100 million) (p. 326). Sources are NACUBO surveys since 1979 and university financial reports (Harvard, Yale, Princeton) (p. 327; n. 24, p. 479). Table 12.2 (image only in this file; results by subperiod in Supplemental Table S12.2, n. 25, p. 479) gives 8.2 percent overall (1980–2010), 6.2 below $100 million, 8.8 above $1 billion, 10.2 for the top three. Alternative-investment shares rise from 10 percent below 50 million euros to over 60 percent above 1 billion (p. 328). Note the switch from dollars to euros for these size bands. Harvard's accounts (n. 30, pp. 479-480): about 10 percent real return plus about 2 percent gifts, 5 percent spent, endowment from $5 billion (1990) to nearly $30 billion (2010). Note 31 says the asset-price rebound adds "no more than a point" of annual return (p. 480).
- **Note 26** (p. 479): the gap between small endowments' 6.2 percent and private wealth's return arises mainly because private owners pay taxes. The pre-tax real return on US private wealth was about 5 percent in 1980–2010. So the comparison with "all private wealth" (p. 327) is a pre-tax against post-tax one.
- **Foundations and trusts** (pp. 329-330): Gates Foundation assets counted in Gates's fortune by Forbes (n. 32, p. 480), Bernard Arnault's Belgian foundation (n. 33, p. 480, from Le Monde), and Fack and Landais on declared gifts falling when oversight tightens (n. 34, p. 480).
- **Inflation**: a stylised numerical example (β = 6, α = 30 percent) (pp. 330-331). Housing access is described qualitatively (p. 332). No incidence data.
- **Sovereign wealth funds** (pp. 333-337): Norway's fund reports (nominal 5 percent in 1998–2012, partly depressed by krone appreciation; n. 39, p. 480). Abu Dhabi Investment Authority self-reported returns, unverifiable by Piketty's own account: "in the absence of detailed annual information" (p. 334). Saudi official documents (2–3 percent). Aggregates ($5.3 trillion, $3.2 trillion oil, $2.1 trillion non-oil) are "notoriously imperfect" estimates (p. 335; n. 41, p. 481). Natural resource rent of about 5 percent of global GDP (p. 336; n. 44 notes it also exceeded 5 percent from the mid-1970s to the mid-1980s, p. 481). The $200 oil price is from unnamed "forecasting models" (p. 336).
- **Military-protection argument** (p. 335): counterfactual reasoning about the 1991 Gulf War. "To my knowledge, no one has ever attempted to calculate precisely the return on such an investment" (p. 335). Speculative.
- **Central scenario simulations** (Figures 12.4 and 12.5, p. 338): the world capital/income ratio near 700 percent by 2100, and Asia owning about half of world capital. Piketty's own projections from ch05 (p. 337), not data.
- **China comparison** (p. 339): about 70 trillion euros of European household net wealth against about 3 trillion euros of Chinese funds plus reserves. Note 50 gives the GDP context (pp. 481-482).
- **Paris property** (p. 339): Aurélie Sotura's 2011 PSE study of Notaires data, 1993–2008 (n. 51, p. 482). Foreign buyers explain about 3 percent of the price increase.
- **Net foreign positions and tax havens** (pp. 340-342; Figure 12.6, p. 341): IMF-collated national statistics, and Gabriel Zucman, "The Missing Wealth of Nations", QJE 2013 (n. 57, p. 482). NGO estimates two to three times larger are mentioned (Henry for the Tax Justice Network; Palan, Murphy and Chavagneux; n. 56, p. 482). Roine and Waldenström on Sweden's top centile once assets held abroad are counted (n. 58, p. 482).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Builds on ch10 and ch11**: ch10's explanation of concentration through r > g assumed one average return. This chapter adds return heterogeneity as a second mechanism (p. 314). Note 19 admits that ch11 applied a uniform return to all fortunes, which makes Bettencourt look like "a very partial heir" (p. 478). So the ch11 heir/self-made decomposition and this chapter's size-dependent returns pull against each other: high returns on inherited fortunes get counted as "accumulated", not "inherited", wealth.
- **Uses ch05**: the central scenario for global β (Figures 12.4–12.5) and the 10 percent long-run saving rate (p. 337). Rising gross foreign positions (p. 340) refer back to ch05.
- **Uses ch01 and ch02**: compound interest (p. 314) and inflation stable at about 2 percent since the 1980s (p. 330).
- **Feeds ch13**: the funded-pension scenario for China (p. 338) and note 47's pointer to ch13 (p. 481) anticipate ch13's defence of PAYGO. Small savers' difficulty in getting the average return (p. 332) is cited in ch13 n. 47 (p. 487). Harvard's endowment reappears in ch13's discussion of elite university access (ch13 n. 29, p. 486).
- **Feeds ch15**: each section ends by pointing to a progressive capital tax (pp. 320, 323-324, 326, 333, 340). The case for it is built mostly on unequal returns and the need for transparency. Annual wealth declarations are proposed already on p. 321.
- **Feeds ch16**: inflation's virtues are deferred to the discussion of central banks (p. 333).
- **Tension on data quality (my observation)**: the global wealth reports are called "useless for studying long-term evolutions or even reliably detecting trends" (p. 318), yet the headline comparison of today's global concentration with Europe in 1900–1910 (p. 318) rests on them. A comparison of levels across a century is a trend claim in all but name.
- **Tension on direction of global inequality**: p. 319 says it is "by no means certain" that global wealth inequality is rising, while p. 339 says oligarchic divergence is "already well under way". The two are reconciled only by restricting the second to the very top. The evidence for the very top is the Forbes data the chapter calls unreliable, plus endowments, which are institutions, not families.
- **Tension on prodigality**: the family-dissipation argument (p. 329) implies an equilibrium distribution, yet the chapter's projections (p. 319; n. 5, p. 476) assume no dissipation. Piketty concedes convergence "toward a certain equilibrium" (p. 329) but does not say where that equilibrium lies or how it compares with today.
- **Ironic symmetry**: he criticises others for asserting the merits of Gates and Slim without facts (p. 324; n. 20, p. 478). In the same paragraph he asserts that Gates profited from "a virtual monopoly" right after saying he knows "virtually nothing" about how Gates became rich (p. 324).
- **Cross-book link (Hayek, Road to Serfdom ch06)**: Piketty's case for taxation over moral judgment uses rule-of-law language: rules "that apply equally to everyone, regardless of nationality" (p. 325), a tax that treats situations "in a supple, consistent, and predictable manner" (p. 324). This is close to Hayek's formal-rule criterion, used for a redistributive end Hayek thought formal rules excluded. Useful for a theme on general rules and distributive aims.
- **Oil states as oligarchic**: note 49 reclassifies the oil-state threat as oligarchic, since the rents go to a small number of people (p. 481). This weakens the clean contrast between international and oligarchic divergence drawn in the text (pp. 338-339).

## Open questions

- Do endowment returns generalise to family fortunes of 10–100 million euros, the group that holds about 20 percent of global wealth and drives the projection (p. 319)? Piketty himself doubts it (p. 329). Check whether ch15 relies on the endowment gradient for its rate schedule.
- Does appraisal-based valuation of alternative assets make the "no greater volatility" claim (p. 328) unreliable? This would weaken the inference that the return gap is not a risk premium.
- The 6.4 percent figure (p. 316) uses trough-to-peak endpoints. The 4 percent figure for 1990–2010 runs peak-to-trough. Is "at least twice" average wealth growth (p. 317) robust across all windows, or only across these two? Supplemental Table S12.1 is not in this file.
- How is 60–70 percent inherited (p. 323) derived beyond the a priori judgment? Can it be reconciled with ch11's method, given note 19 (p. 478)?
- Is the "Earth owned by Mars" anomaly fully accounted for by Zucman's tax-haven wealth, and does the conclusion that rich countries are net creditors (p. 342) depend on the three-quarters ownership share, which is asserted from "all the evidence" (p. 341)?
- Under what conditions does Piketty think a 60 percent top-thousandth share would provoke "a violent political reaction" (p. 319)? Compare with ch17 and ch16 on the politics of capital and with the war-shock account in ch10.
- The oil-fund projection (10–20 percent of global capital by 2030–2040, p. 336) depends on unnamed forecasts of $200 oil and on a reinvestment fraction "considerably larger than it is today" (p. 336). How load-bearing is this for later chapters?
