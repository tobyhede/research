# ch15: A Global Tax on Capital (pp. 378–399)

## Question this chapter answers

What new institution could let democracy regain control over globalized patrimonial capitalism, how would it work, and is there any alternative? Piketty's answer: a progressive annual tax on individual net wealth, applied worldwide (or at first regionally, in Europe), backed by automatic international exchange of banking information (pp. 378-380). Its primary purpose is not revenue but to regulate capitalism: to halt the indefinite rise of wealth inequality and to make finance transparent (p. 381). Alternatives (protectionism, capital controls on the Chinese model, redistribution of oil rents, immigration) are inferior or only partial (pp. 395-399). The tax is a "useful utopia" (p. 378): unrealistic globally, but a standard against which other proposals are measured.

## Key terms

Global in two senses: worldwide coordination, and covering all asset types "no exceptions" (p. 380). |
| Useful utopia | p. 378 | An ideal unlikely to be enacted soon but serving as "a worthwhile reference point, a standard against which alternative proposals can be measured", approachable incrementally (p. 378). |

Contrasted with declared (taxable) income. |
| Exceptional (one-time) capital levy | p. 389 | A tax on capital at high rates (e.g., France 1945, up to 25%, 100% on gains during the Occupation) that cannot be repeated annually (p. 389); developed in ch16. |

| Petroleum rents | p. 397 | Income from natural resources whose distribution depends on arbitrary borders; an ideal capital tax in a global democracy would redistribute it (p. 397). |

## Leading propositions

- Updating the social state and progressive income tax is not enough; democracy needs new tools, the ideal being a progressive global tax on capital with high financial transparency (p. 378).
- The global tax is utopian but useful: a reference point, and approachable step by step, regionally first (Europe) (pp. 378-379).
- Without such cooperation, nationalist reactions (protectionism, capital controls) are likely; these are "seldom effective" and "unsatisfactory substitutes" (p. 379).
- Current bank data-sharing proposals are incomplete and lack sanctions; they will achieve little without heavy sanctions on banks and on countries that thrive on opacity (p. 379), and without a clear purpose for the data, namely the progressive wealth tax (p. 379).
- The base is individual net worth at market value, all asset types included (pp. 379-380). Illustrative rates: 0% below 1 million euros, 1% from 1 to 5 million, 2% above 5 million; possibly 5 or 10% above 1 billion; possibly 0.1% below 200,000 and 0.5% from 200,000 to 1 million (p. 380).
- Existing capital taxes are flawed: property taxes ignore financial assets and debt and are flat (p. 380); European wealth taxes are "riddled with exemptions" and use arbitrary valuations (p. 380).
- The capital tax is a modest supplement, a few points of national income ("three or four at most") (pp. 380-381). Its primary purpose is to regulate capitalism: stop rising wealth inequality and regulate the financial system to avoid crises (p. 381).
- Even a 0.1% flat tax would be useful because it produces information on the distribution of wealth, for democratic debate and for financial regulators (pp. 381-382). It would be "more in the nature of a compulsory reporting law than a true tax" (p. 382).
- A tax is also a way of defining norms and categories; the capital tax would force definitions and valuation rules for assets and liabilities, as the World War I income taxes fostered accounting standards (p. 382).
- Automatic transmission of banking data, domestic then international, with precomputed statements, is technically easy (pp. 382-383); tax havens' objections are pretexts, and their real reason is to share in evasion (pp. 383-384).
- FATCA (2010) is the most ambitious attempt, far beyond the 2003 EU savings directive, but still insufficient: trusts and foundations may escape and the 30% sanction is too weak (pp. 384-385). Sanctions should hit countries, e.g., a tariff of 30% or more on their exports (p. 385).
- Europe, the region most dependent on tax revenue, has done the least; nation-states are "too small" to enforce rules on globalized capitalism (pp. 384-385).
- Income tax, estate tax and capital tax play distinct, complementary roles; each is a pillar of an ideal system (p. 386).
- Contributive argument: for the very rich, declared income is a tiny fraction of economic income (Bettencourt: declared at most 5 million euros on more than 30 billion of wealth) without any evasion, so even very high income tax rates accomplish nothing; capital is the better base (pp. 386-388).
- Since larger fortunes earn higher returns, a direct progressive wealth tax is preferable to presumptive-income schemes like the Dutch one (pp. 387-388).
- Incentive argument: a stock tax is light on high-return owners and heavy on low-return ones; but returns depend on fortune size and on chance, and a pure stock tax can bankrupt loss-making firms, so the ideal system compromises with taxes on capital income (p. 388).
- Because the lifetime value of an inheritance is unknown at transmission, it is more efficient to tax heirs throughout their lives than once at death (pp. 388-389).
- An annual tax must have moderate rates ("a few percent") since it is levied every year on the stock; this is still substantial (p. 389).
- A 0/1/2% schedule applied across the EU would hit about 2.5% of the population and raise about 2% of European GDP, because private wealth exceeds five years of GDP and is concentrated (p. 389).
- Single countries cannot do this alone without bank data sharing; hence the exemptions in France's wealth tax and the regressive design of Italy's 2012 tax (pp. 389-390).
- Ideal lower brackets (0.1% below 200,000 euros, 0.5% from 200,000 to 1 million) would replace the property tax and could be enacted nationally (pp. 390-391).
- Top rates should be set by observed returns per wealth bracket over prior years; about 5% on the largest fortunes to avoid divergence, 10% or more on billionaires to reduce concentration (p. 391). The yield on public debt is the wrong benchmark (p. 391).
- A European wealth tax faces no technical obstacle; the obstacle is political institutions (p. 391).
- Historically, usury bans were illogical forms of social control that never questioned returns to capital as such (pp. 391-392); Soviet abolition of private capital was logically consistent but disastrous, because markets coordinate millions of actions (pp. 392-393).
- The capital tax is "less violent and more efficient", treats all capital equally, and is "the most appropriate response" to r > g and to returns rising with fortune size (p. 393).
- As a progressive annual tax on total wealth at market value, the capital tax is a new idea; earlier German and Swedish capital taxes failed because of stale assessments, not tax competition (pp. 393-394).
- Protectionism is useful for infant industries and against rule-breakers, but not a source of prosperity and does nothing about r > g (p. 395).
- Capital controls will likely return in rich countries after 2008; emerging countries have led since 1998 (p. 395). China's controls are opaque and probably unstable, but are one way to contain wealth inequality (pp. 396-397). For Europe, the capital tax is "the liberal form of capital control" (p. 397).
- An ideal capital tax in a global democracy would redistribute petroleum rents; instead they are distributed by force (pp. 397-398). The norm against redistribution by arms should carry an obligation to redistribute by sanctions, taxes, or aid (p. 398).
- Immigration redistributes and keeps the US from becoming the new Old Europe (p. 398), but it only postpones the problem of wealth concentration (p. 399).
- Less developed countries, especially in Africa where capital outflows exceed aid, would benefit most from transparency and a global capital tax (p. 399).

## Arguments

**A. Transparency is the first purpose of the tax (pp. 381-383)**

- **Premises**: (p. 381) Even a 0.1% flat tax would oblige reporting and so generate data on the distribution of wealth. (p. 381) Democratic debate on the social state, energy transition, and development is impaired by opacity, with people relying on Forbes and wealth-manager reports. (p. 382) Regulators such as the IMF do not know who owns what (Cyprus 2013). (p. 382) Taxes define legal categories and valuation rules.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 382) The capital tax would be "a sort of cadastral financial survey of the entire world"; (p. 382) it would force governments to broaden automatic bank data sharing.
- **Unstated premise**: that the information would be published or used for statistics and regulation rather than kept within tax administrations. Piketty's note 26 complains that the French tax authorities publish "very few detailed statistics" on the existing wealth tax (p. 498), which shows the premise can fail.

**B. Automatic exchange of bank information is feasible and must be enforced by sanctions on countries (pp. 383-386)**

- **Premises**: (p. 383) Banks already report automatically in the US, France, and Germany; adding Switzerland and the Caymans would not radically increase data volume. (pp. 383-384) The misuse argument would apply equally to domestic accounts; the real motive for secrecy is to share in evasion. (pp. 384-385) The EU directive covers only interest-bearing deposits at European banks and is not enforced (Luxembourg, Austria exemptions); FATCA is broader but its sanctions fall only on banks with US operations, so small offshore banks can thrive.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 385) Automatic sanctions must hit countries, e.g., a 30% or higher tariff on exports of non-cooperating states.
- **Qualification**: (p. 385) The aim is not a general embargo; "Protectionism does not produce wealth". Transparency should have been part of the free-trade and capital-liberalisation agreements since 1980 (p. 385).
- **Moral premise**: (p. 384) "No one has the right to set his own tax rates"; profiting from integration while siphoning neighbours' tax base is "outright theft".

**C. The contributive justification (pp. 386-388)**

- **Premises**: (p. 386) Top fortunes grew at 6–7% a year in real terms (Forbes; ch12), so their economic income is at least 6–7% of capital if little is consumed. (p. 387) Declared incomes at the top are "no more than a few tens of millions"; Bettencourt declared at most 5 million on a fortune above 30 billion. (p. 387) No evasion is needed: returns accumulate in trusts or holding entities. (p. 387) If declared income is 1% or 10% of economic income, a 50% or 98% income tax rate achieves nothing, and effective rates at the top are extremely low, which accentuates the wealth dynamic.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 388) Capital is "a better indicator of the contributive capacity of very wealthy individuals than is income", so a capital tax is needed in addition to the income tax.
- **Choice between remedies**: (pp. 387-388) Tax income accumulating in trusts and holding companies; or impute a flat yield and add it to taxable income (the Netherlands; Piketty's note 15 says it assumes 4% on all assets and exempts much) (p. 496); or tax total wealth progressively. The last is preferred because rates can vary with fortune size, and larger fortunes earn more (p. 388).
- **Note**: the argument is strongest for billionaires and very large fortunes; it does not by itself justify the lower brackets (0.1–1%), which rest on the transparency and property-tax-replacement arguments (pp. 390-391).

**D. The incentive justification, and its limits (pp. 388-389)**

- **Premises**: (p. 388) A 1–2% tax is light for someone earning 10% and heavy for someone earning 2–3%, pushing assets toward dynamic investors. (p. 388) But returns depend on fortune size and on chance; a stock tax taxes losing firms as heavily as winning ones.
- **Conclusion**: The ideal system is "a compromise between the incentive logic" (p. 388) (stock tax) "and an insurance logic" (p. 388) (income tax); all three taxes are useful "even if income is perfectly observable for all taxpayers" (p. 389).
- **Source**: Piketty and Saez, "A Theory of Optimal Capital Taxation" / "A Theory of Optimal Inheritance Taxation" (Piketty's notes 19 and 21) (p. 497). Piketty's note 17 calls Maurice Allais's proposal to replace all taxes with a capital tax "extravagant" (pp. 496-497).

**E. Rates must be moderate but can rise with observed returns (pp. 389-391)**

- **Premises**: (p. 389) An annual tax hits the whole stock each year; a 25% annual tax would leave nothing after a few years. (p. 389) Property taxes of 0.5–1% already take a tenth to a quarter of rental value. Piketty's note 23 adds that a 4–5% tax on all capital would absorb nearly all of capital's share of income (p. 497). (p. 391) But real returns on the largest fortunes are 6–7% or more.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 391) Rates of about 5% on the largest fortunes to prevent divergence; 10% or more on billionaires to reduce concentration; rates indexed to observed bracket returns.
- **Link to r > g**: Piketty's note 33 gives, "under certain hypotheses", the optimal inheritance tax t = 1 − G/R (generational growth over generational return), and says the formulas are to clarify debate, not provide solutions (p. 499).

**F. The capital tax beats the historical alternatives (pp. 391-397)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 391-392) Usury bans selectively targeted some capital as social control and never questioned rent. (pp. 392-393) Soviet abolition of private capital removed r > g but destroyed market coordination. (p. 395) Protectionism does not create wealth or touch r > g. (pp. 396-397) Chinese-style capital controls contain wealth inequality but are opaque and tied to a different, unstable notion of property rights.
- **Conclusion**: A progressive capital tax reasserts the general interest "while relying on the forces of private property and competition" (p. 393); it is "the liberal form of capital control" (p. 397).

**Position versus argument.** The historical record (usury, French revolutionary and Third Republic taxation, German and Swedish capital taxes, ISF, Italy, Spain, FATCA) is separable from the proposal. The proposal's quantitative case rests on two inputs Piketty supplies himself: top fortunes' 6–7% returns (ch12) and the European wealth distribution (Piketty's notes 24-25) (pp. 497-498), with simulations in the online appendix only.

## Evidence offered

- **Revenue arithmetic, 0.1% flat tax**: with global private capital at about five years of global income, revenue of 0.5% of global income (p. 381). Simple multiplication, assuming collection by residence of owner (p. 381).
- **European schedule simulation**: 0% below 1 million euros, 1% from 1 to 5 million, 2% above 5 million: about 2.5% of the population, revenue 2% of EU GDP (p. 389). Piketty's note 24: 2.5% of European adults above 1 million and 0.2% above 5 million in 2013; about 300 billion euros on GDP of nearly 15 trillion; Supplemental Table S5.1 and an online simulator (pp. 497-498). Piketty's note 25: top centile holds about 25% of wealth (125% of GDP), top 2.5% nearly 40% (200% of GDP) (p. 498). With the lower brackets replacing property taxes, 3–4% of GDP, of which 1–2 points come from the replaced property tax (Piketty's note 28) (p. 498). Provenance: Piketty's own estimates; the wealth distribution data for Europe rest on the sources discussed in ch10 and ch12.
- **Bettencourt case**: declared income never above 5 million euros a year on wealth above 30 billion; the source is given only as "According to information published in the press and revealed by Bettencourt herself" (p. 387). Piketty's note 13: even at 2% return, economic income would be 600 million (p. 496). Piketty's note 14 adds the political scandal around the family trust and the reduction of the wealth tax by two-thirds (p. 496).
- **Top fortune growth**: 6–7% real a year for the largest fortunes, from the Forbes rankings (p. 386) (Piketty's note 11 points to Table 12.1) (p. 496).
- **Property taxes**: 1–2% of national income in English-speaking countries (p. 380); typical rates of 0.5–1% of value (p. 389); worked example of a 500,000-euro property paying 2,500–5,000 euros against rental value of about 20,000 (Piketty's note 23) (p. 497); US assessment by hedonic regression on transaction data (Piketty's note 5) (p. 495).
- **Self-reporting bias**: French wealth tax returns bunch just below the threshold; real estate typically understated by 10–20% (Piketty's note 6) (p. 495). No source cited for the figure.
- **Existing wealth taxes**: French ISF: threshold 1.3 million euros after a 30% primary-residence deduction, rates 0.7–1.5%, revenue under 0.5% of GDP, business-asset and "stockholder agreement" exemptions (Piketty's note 26) (p. 498); introduced 1981, abolished 1986, reintroduced 1988 (Piketty's note 38) (p. 500). Italy 2012: 0.8% on real estate, 0.1% on deposits and financial assets, stocks exempt, no progressivity (p. 390). Spain: suspended 2008–2010, restored 2011–2012 (p. 394), threshold 700,000 euros, top rate 2.5% (2.75% in Catalonia) (Piketty's note 39) (p. 500). Switzerland: cantonal capital tax under 1% (Piketty's note 39) (p. 500). Germany: from Prussia to suspension in 1997; Sweden: 1947 to 2007, peak 4% in 1983 on assessed values; rates generally under 1.5–2% (Piketty's note 37) (pp. 499-500), sources Dell (2008) and Ohlsson and Waldenström.
- **Information-exchange regimes**: FATCA (2010, phased in 2014–2015), reporting by all foreign banks on US taxpayers' accounts and investments, 30% surtax sanction (pp. 384-385); EU savings directive (2003), interest-bearing deposits only, European banks only, exemptions for Luxembourg and Austria, information only on request (p. 384); Luxembourg and Switzerland announce FATCA compliance in 2013 (p. 384).
- **Tax havens' stakes**: losses from transparency of 10–20% of national income for Luxembourg or Switzerland, 50% or more for exotic havens, up to 80–90% for pure domiciles of fictitious corporations (Piketty's note 9) (pp. 495-496). Presented as guesses ("It is difficult to estimate").
- **Gains from financial liberalisation**: Gourinchas and Jeanne (2006) find modest gains (Piketty's note 41) (p. 500).
- **China**: income tax more progressive than Russia's flat tax, revenues invested in education, health, infrastructure on a larger scale than India's (p. 396), from Qian and Piketty (2009) (Piketty's note 43) (p. 501). The Telecom China billionaire is a hypothetical (p. 396). The House of Representatives vs. People's Assembly wealth comparison is criticised for comparing 535 legislators with the seventy richest delegates (Piketty's note 42) (pp. 500-501).
- **Petroleum rents**: Iraq 35 million people vs Kuwait 1 million; about 900,000 troops in 1991 (p. 397); Egypt's 2012 education budget under $5 billion for 85 million people, Saudi oil revenue $300 billion for 20 million, Qatar over $100 billion for 300,000 (p. 398). Source for Egypt: online appendix (Piketty's note 45) (p. 501).
- **Immigration**: US population from about 3 million to over 300 million (p. 398); permanent integration rates 2000–2010 of 0.6–0.7% a year in Italy, Spain, Sweden, Britain vs 0.4% in the US and 0.2–0.3% in France and Germany (Piketty's note 46) (p. 501).
- **Historical and textual authority**: Aristotle on *tocos* (p. 392) (Piketty's note 30 cites a 2010 newspaper article) (p. 498); Thomas Paine's *Agrarian Justice* 10% inheritance tax (Piketty's note 34) (p. 499); Hicks's definition of income (Piketty's note 12) (p. 496).
- **Bare assertion**: protectionism over long periods leads to "serious disappointment" by "Historical experience" (p. 395), no cases given; German and Swedish abolitions owed "more to" archaic design than tax competition (p. 394), qualified in his own note (see Tensions).

## Proposals: design, justification, objections

- **Progressive annual tax on global net wealth**
  - *Design*: base is all assets at market value net of debt, no exemptions (pp. 379-380); collected where the owner resides (p. 381); rates 0.1% below 200,000 euros, 0.5% from 200,000 to 1 million, 1% from 1 to 5 million, 2% above 5 million, about 5% on the largest fortunes, possibly 10% or more on billionaires (p. 380), (pp. 390-391); rates indexed to observed returns by bracket over prior years (p. 391); valuation by precomputed statements from bank and transaction data (pp. 382-383). Piketty's note 4 adds a low-rate tax on net corporate capital to force public accounting standards (p. 495).
  - *Justification*: transparency for democracy and regulation (pp. 381-382); contributive capacity of the very rich (pp. 386-388); incentive to use capital well, within limits (p. 388); halting divergence driven by r > g and returns rising with size (p. 391), (p. 393).
  - *Revenue*: modest by design, 2% of EU GDP for the 0/1/2 schedule, 3–4% with the lower brackets (p. 389) (Piketty's note 28) (p. 498); "three or four at most" points of national income (pp. 380-381). Piketty's note 1 says revenue could reduce other taxes or pay for aid or debt reduction (p. 495).
  - *Objections anticipated*: (1) utopian: conceded, answered by incrementalism and the reference-point function (pp. 378-379); (2) "dangerous illusion": compared with past rejection of the income tax (p. 379); (3) capital flight from single countries: conceded, hence regional scale and bank data sharing (pp. 389-390); (4) existing wealth taxes have failed: due to exemptions and stale assessments, which market-value assessment fixes (p. 380), (pp. 393-394); (5) bankrupting loss-making firms: conceded via the insurance logic; the stock tax is complemented by income taxes (p. 388); (6) volatile market values: Piketty's note 38 accepts market values as "the only objective and universally acceptable basis" while warning that rates must be adjusted so receipts do not rise automatically with property prices, citing Proposition 13 (p. 500); (7) the yield on public debt as a cap on rates: rejected because large fortunes are not in government bonds (p. 391).
- **Automatic exchange of bank information**
  - *Design*: domestic automatic reporting by all financial intermediaries, extended internationally, feeding precomputed statements (pp. 382-383); covering all asset types including trusts and foundations (pp. 384-385).
  - *Justification*: prerequisite for any national or regional capital tax (p. 390) and for income-tax enforcement (p. 386).
  - *Enforcement*: sanctions on banks (FATCA's 30% surtax judged too weak) and on countries (tariffs of 30% or more on exports) (p. 385).
  - *Objections anticipated*: government misuse of information (answered: applies equally to domestic accounts, p. 384); data volume (answered: no technical obstacle, p. 383); legitimate financial industries in havens (conceded, but havens will suffer losses and need sanctions to agree, pp. 385-386).
- **Alternatives considered**
  - *Protectionism*: legitimate for infant industries and as a weapon against rule-breakers, but no source of prosperity and no answer to r > g (p. 395).
  - *Capital controls*: likely to return; China's model contains wealth inequality but is opaque, possibly unstable, and bound to non-Western property rights (pp. 395-397). Europe should prefer the capital tax as "the liberal form of capital control" (p. 397).
  - *Redistribution of petroleum rents*: an ideal global capital tax would do it; in practice, sanctions, taxes or aid (p. 398).
  - *Immigration*: redistributes globally and stabilises the US, but only postpones wealth concentration (pp. 398-399).
  - *Inflation and public-debt measures* (exceptional capital levies, inflation, austerity) are announced for ch16 (p. 382), (p. 389), (p. 391); the exceptional 1945 French levy is mentioned only to explain why annual rates must be low (p. 389). Regulation through competition and corporate governance was rejected for executive pay in ch14 (p. 376).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch12**: the 6–7% real returns on top fortunes (p. 386), (p. 391) and the claim that returns rise with fortune size (p. 388), (p. 393) are the empirical core of both the contributive argument and the 5–10% top rates. They come from Forbes rankings and university endowments, sources Piketty himself calls deficient (p. 381 refers back to Part Three). The tax is partly proposed to replace those sources (p. 379), (p. 381), so its rates are set on data whose unreliability it is meant to cure.
- **Depends on ch10 and the r > g framework**: the tax is "the most appropriate response to the inequality r > g" (p. 393); the 5% "avoid divergence" rate (p. 391) is a back-of-envelope version of reducing the after-tax return on top fortunes toward g. The formal link is only in Piketty's note 33 (p. 499).
- **Builds on ch14**: the income tax fails at the top because declared income is a sliver of economic income (p. 387), which completes the account of regressivity at the top in ch14 (pp. 362-363). The informational role of low-rate taxes in ch14 (p. 369) becomes the compulsory-reporting tax (p. 382).
- **Contradicts ch14 (my observation)**: ch14 ends with the US "on the verge of becoming the Old Europe" in ch14 (p. 378); this chapter says immigration keeps the US "still a long way" from that, explicitly citing ch14 (p. 398). Piketty does not reconcile the two.
- **Purpose shifts (my observation)**: "The primary purpose of the capital tax is not to finance the social state" (p. 381), yet the European blueprint is sold on revenue: "could bring in significant revenue" and "the additional revenues it would generate are nevertheless significant" (p. 389). The two are compatible (moderate revenue as by-product), but the regulatory goal of "about 5 percent" on the largest fortunes (p. 391) and the revenue estimate of 2% of GDP (p. 389) use different schedules; no revenue figure is given for the more steeply progressive schedule.
- **Text versus note on tax competition (my observation)**: the text says German and Swedish abolitions had "more to do with the archaic design" (p. 394) than tax competition, but Piketty's note 37 concedes that "the perception of fiscal competition also played a role in Sweden" and calls the 2005 Swedish estate-tax abolition "a good example of the growing inability of smaller countries to maintain an independent fiscal policy" (p. 500). The note supports the chapter's broader small-country argument while weakening the specific claim in the text.
- **Insurance logic versus stock tax (my observation)**: the case against a pure stock tax (losing firms pay as much as profitable ones, p. 388) applies most to large business stakes, which are exactly what the top brackets (5–10%) would hit. Piketty's answer is the complementarity of the three taxes (p. 389), but he does not say how a 5–10% stock tax on a billionaire whose firm makes losses would be paid without forced sales, which the incentive logic welcomes and the insurance logic warns against.
- **"Liberal" control**: the capital tax as "the liberal form of capital control" (p. 397) and as relying "on the forces of private property and competition" (p. 393) repeats ch14's framing of confiscatory income taxation as liberal in ch14 (p. 370). Cross-book theme with Hayek (road-to-serfdom ch06): is a general, rule-bound tax schedule aimed at a distributive outcome compatible with the Rule of Law?
- **Agency (my observation)**: the chapter repeatedly says the obstacle is political, not technical (p. 383), (p. 385), (p. 391), and that Europe's leaders have failed (p. 385). As in ch14 (war, not suffrage, in ch14 (p. 377)), the book gives no account of what political force would enact the tax; ch16's argument for European political union is the intended answer.
- **Feeds ch16**: the exceptional capital levy (p. 389), progressive levies in banking crises like Cyprus (p. 382), the ECB as the only strong European institution (p. 391), and stakeholder voting rights and financial transparency (Piketty's note 32) (pp. 498-499) are all developed there; inflation as an alternative to the capital tax is treated in ch16, not here.

## Open questions

- How sensitive is the 2%-of-GDP European revenue estimate (p. 389) to valuation of unlisted business assets and to behavioural responses (avoidance, emigration, asset re-labelling)? The simulation is in the online appendix only (Piketty's note 24) (pp. 497-498). Does the estimate assume zero avoidance?
- Is the residence principle (p. 381), which Piketty says "can by no means be taken for granted", workable without global agreement? What happens to wealth held by non-residents in a taxing region?
- The precomputed-statement model works well for real estate and bank-held securities (pp. 382-383). How would unlisted firms, private equity, art, and trusts be valued annually at market value? The chapter names this as a design question (pp. 394-395) but does not answer it.
- The 30% tariff on non-cooperating states (p. 385) is a trade sanction; is it compatible with WTO rules, and does Piketty address retaliation?
- What exactly is the relation between top rates and r − g? Piketty's note 33 gives only the inheritance-tax formula (p. 499); the "about 5 percent" figure (p. 391) is not derived in the text.
- Do Chinese capital controls actually contain wealth inequality (pp. 396-397)? The evidence offered is a hypothetical (p. 396) and the absence of observed outflows "at least for now" (p. 396).
- Immigration "postpones the problem" (p. 399) but also prevents the US becoming Old Europe (p. 398). Which of ch14's and ch15's predictions about the US does the conclusion (ch17) endorse?
