# ch04: From Old Europe to the New World (pp. 101–118)

## Question this chapter answers

Do the British and French patterns of ch03 hold elsewhere, and what explains the twentieth-century collapse of the capital/income ratio? Piketty extends the series to Germany (from 1870), the United States (from 1770) and Canada (from 1860). Germany repeats the European U-curve and public-private story, with lower private wealth today that he attributes to low stock-market valuation under "Rhenish capitalism". The 1914–1945 collapse in Europe was mainly a matter of lost foreign assets, low saving and low asset prices, and only to a small extent physical destruction. The United States had a lower and more stable ratio, because land was abundant and cheap and immigrants arrived without capital, *unless* slaves are counted as capital, in which case the antebellum South held more wealth relative to income than old Europe. The chapter ends by arguing that putting a market value on "human capital" makes sense only in a slave society.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rhenish capitalism / stakeholder model | pp. 104-105 | A model in which firms are owned not only by shareholders but by "stakeholders": worker representatives with real board votes, regional governments, consumer and environmental groups (pp. 104-105). It "inevitably implies a lower market valuation but not necessarily a lower social valuation" (p. 105). Reused in ch05 to explain a Tobin's Q below 1 (pp. 136-137). |
| Shareholder model | p. 105 | "Anglo-Saxon market capitalism", in which all power lies in theory with shareholders (p. 105). |
| Book value | p. 104 | A firm's cumulative investments minus its debt (p. 104). Using it instead of stock-market value would raise German private wealth from about four to five or six years of national income (p. 104). Defined more fully in ch05 (p. 135). |
| Volume effect vs price effect | p. 108 | Changes in β from quantities (saving, foreign asset losses, destruction) versus changes in relative asset prices (real estate, shares) (p. 108). Applied to land on pp. 108-109: an abundant asset can have so low a price that its total value is lower than if it were scarcer. |
| Physical destruction | p. 105 | War damage to buildings, factories and infrastructure; shown to explain only a fraction of the 1913–1950 fall (pp. 105-106). |
| Euthanasia of European capitalists | p. 107 | Piketty's summary of 1913–1950, adapting Keynes's "euthanasia of the rentier" (ch03, p. 97): "the history of Europe's suicide" (p. 107). |
| Patrimonial capitalism | p. 111 | Capitalism in which inherited private fortunes are again large relative to income; Europe "in the avant-garde" of it in the early twenty-first century (p. 111). Developed in ch05 (p. 125) and Part Three. |
| Net national wealth | p. 111 | Domestic capital minus foreign-owned assets net of residents' foreign assets; used to state how far a country is "owned" by itself, e.g. the US 98 percent US-owned in 1913 (p. 111). |
| Slave capital | p. 114 | The market value of slaves, estimated from probate, tax and census data and slave-market prices (p. 114). Counting it as capital "is obviously a dubious thing to do" but measures its importance to slave owners (p. 115). |
| First law applied to slaves (β = α / r) | p. 116 | Rewriting α = r × β: if slaves yield 60 percent of national income and r = 5 percent, slave capital is twelve years of income (pp. 116-117). |
| Human capital | p. 117 | Capitalised labour income. Piketty rejects monetary estimates of it (e.g. World Bank "wealth of nations" reports) as obvious by construction and meaningful only where people can be owned (pp. 117-118). |

## Leading propositions

- German data before 1870 are too fragmented to use; after 1870 the evolution is similar to Britain and France: land gives way to real estate and business capital, and β has climbed since 1945 toward its pre-1914 level (pp. 101-102).
- Germany in 1913 had foreign assets of about 50 percent of national income, half the French and a quarter of the British level, because it had no colonial empire; colonial rivalry contributed to the climate of 1914 (p. 102).
- By 2010 Germany's net foreign assets were close to 50 percent of national income through trade surpluses, while the former colonial powers were near zero, an inversion of positions (p. 102).
- Germany drowned its public debt in inflation more than any other country, yet now refuses inflation above 2 percent, while Britain, which always paid its debts, is more relaxed about it (p. 103).
- As in France, the German state owned 25–30 percent of national capital during postwar reconstruction; net public wealth was about zero by 2010 (p. 103).
- German private wealth rose from about 1.5 years of national income in 1950 to more than four, but remains below France and Britain (five to six) and Italy and Spain (above six) (p. 104).
- Most of that gap comes from the low stock-market value of German firms, not from housing; at book value the "German paradox would disappear" (p. 104). These accounting choices "are in fact highly political" (p. 104).
- The stakeholder model implies lower market value but not necessarily lower social value, and can be "at least as efficient" as the shareholder model (p. 105).
- The European pattern is general: Italy, Spain, Belgium, the Netherlands and Austria show the same U-curve (p. 105).
- Between 1913 and 1950 national capital in Britain, France and Germany fell from 6.5–7 to about 2.5 years of national income (p. 105). Physical destruction accounted for about one-fifth to one-quarter of the fall in France, about one-third in Germany, and almost nothing in Britain, where β fell just as much (p. 106).
- Volume effects (lost foreign assets, low national saving, destruction) explain two-thirds to three-quarters of the 1913–1950 fall; low asset prices under rent control, regulation and the new postwar politics explain one-quarter to one-third (pp. 106, 108).
- Low saving 1914–1945 led wealthy Europeans to sell foreign assets to maintain living standards, and private saving was absorbed by wartime deficits later expropriated by inflation (p. 107).
- The low postwar ratio was partly "a positive thing", reflecting deliberate policies to reduce asset values and owners' power (p. 107).
- Around 1770–1810 US national capital was barely more than three years of national income, with farmland at one to one and a half years, versus about seven in Britain and France (p. 108).
- Land was so abundant in America that its price effect outweighed its volume effect, so its total value was low (pp. 108-109); new arrivals brought no capital and needed time to accumulate it (p. 109).
- Low American β meant a different structure of inequality: accumulated wealth mattered less and newcomers could close the wealth gap faster than in Europe (p. 109).
- US national capital rose to nearly five years by 1910 and has oscillated between four and five since (pp. 109-110); the American U-curve is shallower, which may explain why textbooks treated a stable capital/output ratio as a universal law (pp. 110-111).
- Europe's higher β today is "fairly well explained" by its lower economic and especially demographic growth (p. 111).
- The United States never became a colonial power, and its net foreign position stayed within a few percent of its capital stock throughout (pp. 111-113): in 1913 "the United States owned itself" (p. 112).
- Canada was different: around 1910 foreigners (mainly British) owned net assets worth 120 percent of national income, about a fifth to a quarter of domestic capital, mainly in natural resources (p. 113). The difference from the US is political, not economic (p. 113).
- Slaves in the US rose from about 400,000 in the 1770s to 1 million in 1800 and more than 4 million in 1860 (p. 114); they were nearly 20 percent of the population in 1800 and 40 percent in the South (p. 114).
- The market value of slaves was about one and a half years of US national income, roughly equal to farmland; including them, total US wealth was stable at about four and a half years from the colonial era to today (pp. 114-115).
- In the South slaves were worth two and a half to three years of income and total capital more than six years, close to Britain and France; in the North total wealth was barely three years (p. 115).
- The US combined a relatively egalitarian North and a South where "one half of the population owned the other half"; this contradictory relation to inequality persists and shapes the US welfare state (pp. 115-116).
- Under the first law, a society where slaves receive 60 percent of income and r = 5 percent would hold slave capital of twelve years of income and total capital of twenty (pp. 116-117).
- US slave capital was far below that bound because slaves were 20 percent of the population, slightly less productive than free labour, and returns on slave capital were nearer 7–8 percent; a prime-age male slave cost ten to twelve years of a free worker's wage (p. 117).
- Capitalising labour income as "human capital" is meaningless outside slavery and the result that it dominates other capital is true by construction (p. 117).

## Arguments

**A. Why German private wealth is low (pp. 104-105)**

- **Premises**: (p. 104) German private wealth (about four years) is below France and Britain (five to six) despite high saving. (p. 104) Cheap housing, partly from post-reunification supply, explains a little. (p. 104) Most of the gap is in "other domestic capital", i.e. firms valued at market prices. (p. 104) At book value German private wealth would match French and British levels.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 104-105) The gap reflects the stakeholder model's lower market valuation, not lower productive capital or lower social value.
- **Unstated premise**: that book value is a reasonable measure of "social" value. Book value is itself an accounting convention, and note 4 (p. 445) admits the gap may partly be "statistical biases". The argument also concedes, in effect, that β measured at market value depends on corporate governance law.

**B. Decomposing the 1913–1950 collapse (pp. 105-108)**

- **Premises**: (p. 105) National capital fell by more than four years of income in all three countries. (p. 106) Measured destruction: about one year in France, one and a half in Germany, under 10 percent of income in Britain. (p. 106) Britain lost about two years of income in net foreign assets. (p. 107) Saving was low and absorbed by public deficits; foreign assets were sold. (pp. 107-108) Rent control, financial regulation and taxation lowered real estate and share prices.
- **Conclusion**: "the budgetary and political shocks of two wars proved far more destructive to capital than combat itself" (p. 106); volume effects two-thirds to three-quarters, price effects one-quarter to one-third (p. 108).
- **Note**: the decomposition is stated as a result, with the detailed estimates deferred to the online appendix (note 9, p. 445). The reader cannot reproduce it from the book.

**C. Why land in America was worth little (pp. 108-109)**

- **Premises**: (p. 108) Hectares per head were far greater than in Europe, so capital per head in volume was higher. (pp. 108-109) Past some threshold, abundance lowers the price so much that price × volume is lower than with less volume.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 108) "the price effect more than counterbalanced the volume effect".
- **Unstated premise**: that the demand for land was price-inelastic (an elasticity below one), so that more land reduces its total value. This is the same kind of claim that ch06 makes about the elasticity of substitution between capital and labour, but here it is asserted without estimate. Supporting evidence: land prices in probate records and wills (p. 109).

**D. Slavery and the first law (pp. 116-117)**

- **Premises**: (p. 116) α = r × β holds by definition; with α = 40 percent for nonhuman capital and r = 5 percent, nonhuman β = 8. (p. 116) Apply the same capitalisation to the 60 percent of income that slaves yield their owners: 0.60 / 0.05 = 12. (p. 117) Total = 1 / 0.05 = 20 years.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 116-117) slave capital can exceed all other wealth; actual US values were lower for the reasons on p. 117.
- **Extension**: (p. 117) Any capitalisation of labour income at a rate close to r makes "human capital" exceed nonhuman capital whenever the labour share exceeds half. So "There is no need for amazement" (p. 117); the comparison should be of flows, not capitalised stocks.
- **Note 15 (p. 445)**: if slaves are treated as persons, slavery is a debt between individuals and nets out of national wealth, like public debt. This is the same accounting logic as ch03's treatment of government bonds (ch03, p. 83).

## Evidence offered

- **National balance sheets** for Germany 1870–2010, the US 1770–2010 and Canada 1860–2010 (Figures 4.1-4.11). All cite only "piketty.pse.ens.fr/capital21c"; figures show decennial averages only (note 1, p. 444).
- **Germany**: data "more diverse" because of late unification and territorial changes (p. 101). Inflation of nearly 17 percent a year with prices multiplied by 300 (p. 103); note 2 (p. 444) says this omits 1923, when prices rose by a factor of 100 million. Public debt above 100 percent of GDP in 1918–1920 and 150 percent in 1943–1944 (p. 103). Lower Saxony holds more than 15 percent of Volkswagen shares and 20 percent of votes (p. 103).
- **Europe-wide**: Britain, France and Germany were more than two-thirds of Western European GDP in 1910 and 2010 (p. 105); estimates for Italy, Spain, Belgium, the Netherlands and Austria are referred to the appendix (note 7, p. 445).
- **War destruction and decomposition**: stated magnitudes (p. 106) and shares (p. 108); details in the online appendix (note 9, p. 445).
- **United States**: national capital "based on numerous contemporary estimates I have collected and compared" (p. 108); land prices confirmed by "probate records and wills" (p. 109); Tocqueville (1840) on scarce capital and cheap land (p. 109, note 10, p. 445). Net foreign position: 10 percent of national income in 1913 against 500 percent domestic capital (p. 111); about 20 percent against 450 percent today (p. 112). The better return on US foreign assets than on US liabilities is attributed to confidence in the dollar (p. 112).
- **Canada**: domestic capital 530 percent and net foreign-owned 120 percent of national income in 1910 (p. 113); 410 percent and under 10 percent today (p. 113).
- **Slavery**: Alice Hanson Jones's probate records, Raymond Goldsmith's tax and census data, Robert Fogel's slave-market transactions, described as "quite consistent with one another" (p. 114). Census counts of slaves and population (p. 114). 1860 prices: about $2,000 for a prime-age male slave against $200 annual wage for a free farm labourer (p. 117). Details in the online appendix (note 17, p. 445).
- **Cultural illustration**: *Titanic*, Henry James, *Mad Men*, *Django Unchained* (pp. 110, 112, 117). These carry no evidential weight.
- **Authorities**: Lenin's *Imperialism* (p. 102); Michel Albert's *Capitalisme contre capitalisme* on varieties of capitalism (note 5, p. 445); Samuelson's textbook on stable capital/output ratios (p. 111); Mira Wilkins on reactions to foreign investment in the US (note 13, p. 445); World Bank "wealth of nations" reports (p. 117, unnamed).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch03**: the European comparison, the market-value rule (ch03, p. 86) and the treatment of debt as an internal claim (ch03, p. 83) are carried over.
- **Anticipates ch05**: Europe's higher β "automatically" follows from lower demographic growth (p. 111), and the asset-price rebound is said to explain a "significant part" of the post-1970 recovery, though less than volume effects (p. 108). Both are claims whose mechanism (β = s / g) is introduced only in ch05.
- **Feeds ch05 and ch06 on prices**: the land argument (pp. 108-109) and Rhenish valuation (pp. 104-105) both show that the value of capital depends on prices that respond to abundance and institutions. ch05 (p. 135) generalises: the price of capital "is always in part a social and political construct". This sits uneasily with using market-valued β as the core state variable of the book's laws (my observation).
- **Internal inconsistency in dates (my observation)**: the text gives German inflation of nearly 17 percent "between 1930 and 1950" with prices multiplied by 300 (p. 103), but note 2 gives the period as 1913–1950 (p. 444). Arithmetic favours the note: 17 percent a year for 37 years multiplies prices by about 330, while for 20 years it gives only about 23.
- **Tension on "America had little capital" (my observation)**: the headline claim of a low New World β (p. 108) holds only if slaves are excluded. With slaves, US wealth was "relatively stable" at about four and a half years (p. 115), and southern capital exceeded six years (p. 115). The egalitarian, low-capital America is the North. Synthesis should not cite the 1770–1810 US figure of "three years" without this qualification.
- **Tension with ch03 on Ricardian conditions**: note 8 (p. 445) says 1914–1945 deficits reduced national capital because private saving had already been shocked, unlike Ricardo's Britain. This confirms that ch03's "debt as internal claim" is neutral only conditionally.
- **Link to ch06 and Part Three**: the slave example uses α = 0.6 for labour, r = 5 percent and implies the r on slave capital was 7–8 percent (p. 117), so it presupposes a theory of returns not yet given. ch06 takes up how r is determined.
- **Link to ch13**: the US welfare state's "nondevelopment" is linked to racial inequality (p. 116), a claim not developed here.

## Open questions

- How was the 1913–1950 decomposition into volume and price effects done (p. 108)? The shares depend on which price index deflates asset prices; ch05's caveat that price comparisons over long periods are "approximate at best" (ch05, p. 135) applies.
- Is the German "paradox" (p. 104) about measurement, or does it show that market-valued β is not a measure of productive capital at all? If governance rules can move β by one to two years of income, cross-country β comparisons in ch05 inherit that noise.
- The land argument (pp. 108-109) assumes an inelastic demand for land. Is there evidence on American land prices beyond probate records? Was the low price also because land titles were insecure or land was taken from Native Americans at no market price? The chapter does not discuss dispossession.
- The slave-capital estimate rests on Jones, Goldsmith and Fogel (p. 114). Fogel and Engerman's work is contested on productivity; does the estimate depend on those contested parts?
- Piketty uses r = 5 percent and α = 40 percent in the slave thought experiment but gives 7–8 percent for actual slave returns (pp. 116-117). Is the higher return a risk premium (slaves could flee, die), consistent with his later account of returns rising with risk and fortune size (ch12)?
- The rejection of human capital (p. 117) is aimed at capitalised labour income as a stock. Does it touch the human-capital theory of earnings (education raises productivity), which ch09 discusses? The two uses should be kept apart in synthesis.
