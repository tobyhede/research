# ch16: The Question of the Public Debt (pp. 399–426)

*Boundary note: p. 399 opens with the last three paragraphs of ch15 (immigration, Africa's capital outflow); ch16 begins partway down p. 399. p. 426 carries the end of ch16 (transparency, Lonmin) and the opening of the Conclusion.*

## Question this chapter answers

How should rich countries, and Europe above all, deal with public debt at a level not seen since 1945, and what does the answer reveal about the role of central banks, the design of the euro, and the right level of public capital? Piketty's answer: public debt is a question of how wealth is *distributed* between public and private actors, not of how much wealth there is (p. 400). An exceptional progressive tax on private capital is the most just and efficient way to cut the debt; inflation is a cruder second best; prolonged austerity is the worst, and it is what Europe is doing (p. 400). Central banks redistribute but do not create wealth, can act fast but cannot target, and lack the democratic legitimacy to do more (pp. 408-411). The euro is a "currency without a state" (p. 414) whose contradictions can only be resolved by pooling debt under a Eurozone budgetary parliament (p. 416). No formula fixes the right level of public debt or capital; the golden rule sets only a theoretical ceiling (pp. 419-422), and the more pressing tasks are educational and natural capital (p. 425) and democratic control of capital through corporate transparency (pp. 425-426).

## Leading propositions

**Framing**

- Taxation is generally preferable to debt "in terms of justice and efficiency" because debt must be repaid to those with the means to lend; better to tax the wealthy than borrow from them (p. 399).
- Rich-country public debt averages about one year of national income (90% of GDP), the highest since 1945; emerging-economy debt is about 30% of GDP (p. 400).
- Hence debt is a question of distribution between public and private actors; "The rich world is rich, but the governments of the rich world are poor" (p. 400). Europe has the most private wealth and the hardest debt problem, "a strange paradox" (p. 400).

**Reducing the debt**

- Three methods, combinable: capital tax, inflation, austerity. Exceptional capital tax best; inflation useful and historically the usual route; prolonged austerity worst, and Europe's current course (p. 400).
- European national wealth is about six years of national income, mostly private; public assets roughly equal public debt; Europe's net foreign position is near balance, so European households own Europe's public debt (pp. 400-401).
- Selling all public assets would roughly pay off the debt, but should be "dismissed out of hand" because the social state must own its schools, hospitals and so on; still, interest on debt weighs on the exchequer much like rent (p. 401).
- A flat 15% tax on private wealth would yield nearly a year of national income and wipe out the debt (p. 401).
- The capital tax is equivalent to repudiation except that (1) its incidence is predictable while a haircut's is not and can trigger bank panic, and (2) it can be graduated by fortune size (pp. 401-402).
- Haircuts do not penalise those who took the risks, since assets are constantly traded and the largest holders can restructure in time (p. 402).
- A progressive exceptional tax generalises the logic of the EU's €100,000 deposit guarantee and must cover all assets, including unlisted shares, to reach the wealthiest (p. 402).
- Realistic target: cut debt by 20% of GDP (90% to 70%) with one-time rates of 0/10/20% (ten times the annual 0/1/2% schedule), or with the annual schedule for ten years; France's 1945 exceptional tax ran from 0 to 25% (p. 403).
- Inflation of 5% rather than 2% cuts the real debt/GDP ratio by more than 15% (p. 403). Historically most large public debts, particularly Europe's in the twentieth century, were reduced this way: France 13% and Germany 17% average inflation 1913–1950 (pp. 403-404).
- Other central banks (Fed, Bank of Japan, Bank of England) are trying to raise inflation targets; the ECB is "by far the most averse" (p. 404).
- Without a capital tax or inflation, with zero inflation, 2% growth and a 1% deficit, it takes 20 years to cut debt/GDP by 20 points; worse conditions mean 30–40 years (p. 404).
- Nineteenth-century Britain ran primary surpluses of 2–3% of GDP for a century (1815–1914) to pay down Napoleonic war debt and spent more on debt interest than on education; Piketty speculates this may explain later British decline (p. 404).
- Starting from about 100% rather than 200% of GDP, and with 2% target inflation, European austerity might last "only" ten or twenty years (p. 405).
- Inflation is "at best a very imperfect substitute" for a capital tax: hard to control (France over 50% a year 1945–1948; German prices multiplied by 100 million in 1923), and its effect disappears once expected (p. 405).
- Concession: idealised inflation taxes idle cash and spares real investors and debtors, a "tax on idle capital" with "some truth" to it (pp. 405-406); but it does not stop large diversified portfolios earning high returns (p. 406).
- The French view that inflation could soak "German rentiers" is "naïve and preposterous"; a great inflation would hurt people of modest means while sparing real estate and stock fortunes (p. 406). A little more inflation is preferable to a little more austerity (p. 406).

**Central banks**

- Under the gold standard the price level depended on the hazards of gold and silver discoveries; a return is "highly unlikely" (p. 406).
- With fiat money, central bank money creation is potentially unlimited and so must be strictly regulated (pp. 406-407).
- The Depression trauma produced a universal consensus that central banks must act as lenders of last resort (p. 407).
- Friedman and Schwartz's *Monetary History* (1963) blamed the Fed for the Depression and drew the political conclusion that a well-run Fed, not a welfare state, was all capitalism needed; this helped create the climate for the "conservative revolution of 1979–1980" (pp. 407-408).
- Piketty: a well-run central bank and a social state with progressive taxation are "complements rather than substitutes" (p. 408).
- Central banks "do not create wealth as such; they redistribute it": their operations are loans with matching assets and liabilities (p. 408). Their dynamic effect on wealth can be positive or negative (pp. 408-409).
- Central banks can prevent failures but cannot make firms invest, households consume, or set the inflation rate (p. 409).
- Their strength is speed; their weakness is limited ability to target, so balance sheets should be limited. Since 2008 they roughly doubled (Fed and BoE from 10 to over 20% of GDP; ECB from 15 to 30%), still modest against private wealth of 500–600% of GDP (p. 410).
- Central banks lack "democratic legitimacy" to buy firms, finance energy transition or run the economy; industrial and educational policy belong to states. "The problem is not one of technical impossibility but of democratic governance" (pp. 410-411).

**Cyprus**

- Bank regulation and capital taxation are complementary (p. 411). Neither the Troika nor member states have a financial cadaster (p. 412).
- Greece cannot tax its rich alone because they move money abroad, often within Europe, and has had to sell public assets at fire-sale prices (p. 412).
- Cyprus (March 2013): a banking sector swollen by foreign, notably Russian, deposits; no published statistics on depositors (p. 412).
- The Troika's proposed deposit levy (6.75% up to €100,000, 9.9% above) had illusory progressivity and an undefined base limited to deposits, so would likely have been "extremely regressive" (p. 413). It was rejected by Cypriots; the replacement exempted deposits under €100,000 on obscure bank-by-bank terms (p. 413).
- Lesson: a progressive capital tax can work as an exceptional levy, potentially at high rates, in banking crises; what is "deeply shocking" is that authorities did not equip themselves to share the burden progressively (p. 413). The excuse was missing data; the obstacles may be "purely ideological" (p. 414).

**The euro and European unification**

- Europe created "for the first time in human history on such a vast scale" a currency without a state (p. 414). The step-by-step story is partly true, but refusing to specify the endpoint may derail the process (p. 414).
- The design reflects the late-1980s belief, after 1970s stagflation, that central banks should be independent and target only inflation; 2008 shattered that view (p. 414).
- Tight constraints on central banks are legitimate, but their independence should have precise limits; the Fed, BoJ and BoE are public bodies working "hand in hand" with elected governments (pp. 414-415).
- The ECB's statutes put low inflation above growth and employment and bar it from buying newly issued government debt; it must juggle seventeen national debts (p. 415).
- Rates converged 2002–2007 and diverged after 2008; southern countries lost devaluation and gained no guarantee of low, predictable borrowing rates (pp. 415-416).
- "The only way" out: pool Eurozone debts under a budgetary parliament drawn from national parliaments (p. 416), with a European finance minister; secretive councils of heads of state "cannot do the work of this budgetary body" (p. 417). France has been "mostly absent" from the debate (p. 417).
- Other joint tools: the progressive capital tax and, "even more obvious", a corporate tax based on a single European profit declaration with revenue apportioned by sales or wages, ending profit-shifting to low-tax subsidiaries (pp. 417-418).
- Extend the "residence of the capital asset" principle to financial assets (p. 418).
- These proposals are no more utopian than a stateless currency; relinquished monetary sovereignty must be matched by restored fiscal sovereignty at the European level (p. 418), so as to construct "a continental political authority capable of reasserting control over patrimonial capitalism" (pp. 418-419).
- Without political union, tax competition continues and pushes toward consumption taxes, a nineteenth-century system with no progressivity; the financial transactions tax shows progress is possible (p. 419).

**Public capital, the golden rule, and climate**

- The desirable level of public debt cannot be fixed with certainty; "only democratic deliberation can decide", and only alongside the question of desirable public and total national capital (p. 419).
- The golden rule (r = g) gives a theoretical ceiling on accumulation. Taken literally it implies β far above historical levels: perhaps 10–15 years of national income for r to fall to 1–1.5%, 20–30 years for it to fall to 0.2% (p. 420).
- At r = g, α = s; as long as r > g, α > s (p. 420). "The inequality r > g is the basis of a society of rentiers" (p. 421).
- Taxing rentiers is a "much simpler and more effective" way to end their reign than accumulating capital worth dozens of years of income (p. 421).
- The optimal capital stock depends on the sources of growth and on how generations are weighed; one can reach "any desired conclusion", so the golden rule "is of limited practical utility" (pp. 421-422).
- Maastricht's 3%/60% rules have never been fully justified and follow from creating a currency without pooled debt; a budgetary parliament would make them unnecessary (p. 422).
- No a priori constitutional debt limits: crises need emergency budgets, judges interpret fiscal law conservatively, and EU law tends to rank free movement of capital above member states' right to tax (pp. 422-423). A supermajority rule would be acceptable as a confidence-building step (p. 422).
- Piketty has "no particular liking for public debt", which often redistributes from poor to rich (pp. 422-423).
- European national wealth "has never been so high"; the real shame is its unequal distribution and that interest on the debt exceeds investment in higher education (p. 423). Debt should be cut "as quickly as possible", ideally by a progressive one-time capital tax, failing that by inflation, decided by a sovereign parliament (p. 423).
- Climate change is the world's principal long-term worry (p. 424). Stern's low discount rate is "more reasonable" than Nordhaus's (p. 424), but the discount-rate debate sidesteps the practical issue of what to invest in, who finances it, and who owns the resulting patents (pp. 424-425).
- Public debt is "not our major worry"; education and natural capital are (pp. 424-425). No one knows yet what role governments will play (p. 425).

**Economic democracy**

- New forms of property and democratic control of capital will be among the most important issues ahead; the public/private line is less clear than assumed since 1989 (p. 425).
- Transparency of corporate (and government) accounts, not of individual incomes, is what democratic participation needs; Lonmin's accounts do not show the profit/wage split at Marikana (pp. 425-426).
- Transparency without a right to intervene, including worker board seats, is "of little use"; institutions of democracy and capitalism must be "reinvented again and again" (p. 426).

## Arguments

**A. The exceptional capital tax beats repudiation (pp. 401-402)**

- **Premises**: (p. 401) A capital tax and a repudiation both transfer the debt's burden to owners of wealth. (pp. 401-402) A repudiation or haircut has unpredictable final incidence, depending on banks' balance sheets and creditors, and at European scale risks bank panic. (p. 402) The largest holders can reallocate in time to escape a haircut. (p. 402) A tax falls on ultimate owners, not institutions, so bank failures are avoided, and it can be graduated by fortune.
- **Unstated premises**: that the wealthy cannot equally reallocate or relocate ahead of a capital tax (he notes the need for a financial cadaster, p. 402, but the comparison assumes it exists); that a one-time levy will be believed to be one-time and so not discourage saving.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 402) The tax "would arrange things in a more civilized manner".

**B. Inflation is a crude second best (pp. 403-406)**

- **Premises**: (p. 403) Government bonds are nominal assets, so modest extra inflation sharply cuts real debt. (pp. 403-404) It has historically worked, notably for France and Germany 1913–1950. (p. 405) It is hard to stop once started and loses force once expected. (p. 406) Large portfolios held in real assets escape it, while modest savers are hurt.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 406) "When it comes to decreasing inequalities of wealth for good or reducing unusually high levels of public debt, a progressive tax on capital is generally a better tool than inflation." But inflation beats austerity (p. 406).
- **Assessment**: the incidence claim (small savers lose, real-asset fortunes spared) is asserted with the 1945–1948 French case and a reference to ch12's return-by-size results (note 15, p. 502), not with an incidence study. The concession on p. 405 (inflation as a tax on idle capital) is left partly unresolved.

**C. Austerity is the worst option (pp. 400, 404-405)**

- **Premises**: (p. 404) Without tax or inflation, debt reduction takes decades. (p. 404) Britain's century of surpluses meant more spending on debt interest than education. Note 12: the capital tax puts most of the burden on the very wealthy, "whereas austerity policies generally aim to spare them" (p. 502).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 400) Austerity is worst on justice and efficiency.
- **Assessment**: the efficiency leg rests on the "obvious contractionary effect" of budgetary rigour, asserted only "at least in the short term" (p. 404), and on a speculative causal link from British debt service to British educational and economic decline ("It may be that", p. 404).

**D. Central banks cannot substitute for fiscal democracy (pp. 408-411)**

- **Premises**: (p. 408) Central bank operations are loans and redistribute rather than create wealth. (p. 410) They can act instantly and without limit, which tax authorities cannot. (p. 410) They are poorly placed to decide who gets what and lack democratic legitimacy for large reallocations.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 410-411) Their balance sheets should be limited and their mandate focused on financial stability; large-scale redistribution belongs to legislatures because "when significant shares of national wealth are shifted about, it is best not to make mistakes" (p. 411).

**E. A stateless currency needs a European fiscal sovereign (pp. 414-418)**

- **Premises**: (p. 414) The euro was built on the belief that central banks need only control inflation. (p. 415) The ECB faces seventeen debts and governments and restrictive statutes. (pp. 415-416) Members gave up devaluation and got no guarantee of low borrowing rates; rate divergence made democratic debate on adjustment nearly impossible. (p. 417) Intergovernmental councils are secret and unaccountable.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 416) Pool debts; (pp. 416-417) create a budgetary parliament from national parliaments; (p. 418) restore at European level the fiscal sovereignty lost nationally, including capital and corporate taxation.
- **Unstated premise**: that the democratic legitimacy of a parliament drawn from national parliaments would be accepted by creditor-country electorates. Piketty treats the political feasibility question as answered by analogy ("No more so than attempting to create a stateless currency", p. 418).

**F. The golden rule sets a ceiling, not a target (pp. 419-422)**

- **Premises**: (p. 420) At r = g, α = s, so all capital income must be reinvested to hold β steady; capital "no longer yields anything". (note 42, p. 507) Beyond that point (r < g) maintaining capital costs more than it returns, which is dynamically inefficient. (p. 421) Accumulating to r = g would demand generations of forgone consumption, and its justification depends on contestable assumptions about eternal population growth and intergenerational weights (pp. 421-422).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 421) Taxing rentiers is the simpler route to ending a rentier society; (p. 422) no formula settles what to leave for future generations.
- **Derivation check**: note 41 gives α = r × β and β = s/g, so α = s × r/g. The printed note reads "α = r if r = g" (p. 507); the algebra gives α = s, as the main text says (p. 420). The note appears to contain a typo.

**G. Constitutional debt limits are undemocratic (pp. 422-423)**

- **Premises**: (p. 422) The 3%/60% numbers lack a rational basis unless public assets and national capital are counted (note 48: they hold only at 5% nominal growth, p. 508). (p. 423) Crises need large emergency budgets. (p. 423) Courts read fiscal law conservatively (note 49: US Supreme Court on income tax and minimum wage; French Constitutional Council on top rates, p. 508).
- **Conclusion**: "there is no justification for engraving untouchable debt and deficit limits in stone in order to thwart future political majorities" (p. 422). Note 51 adds that a constitutionalised rule might make a progressive capital tax unconstitutional (p. 509).

**H. The debt panic misreads national wealth (p. 423)**

- **Premises**: (p. 423) Net private wealth is so high that public plus private wealth is as great as in a century. (p. 423) The burden is its unequal distribution and the crowding out of education by interest.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 423) The idea of bequeathing a shameful debt burden to our children "simply makes no sense", yet the debt should be cut quickly because slow growth since 1970 makes interest heavy (note 50: interest far above primary deficits 1970–2010; Italy 7% of GDP, pp. 508-509).

**Position versus argument.** The chapter's ranking (capital tax over inflation over austerity) is argued mainly on justice and predictability of incidence; the efficiency claims (that a one-time levy has no harmful incentive effects, that austerity is contractionary) are asserted more than shown. The political programme (budgetary parliament, European corporate tax) is defended by its logical necessity given a common currency, not by evidence that it can be enacted.

## Evidence offered

The chapter's evidence is mostly the book's own national-accounts series reused, plus recent policy episodes. Little new data appears; the policy analysis leans on hypotheticals and illustrative arithmetic.

- **Structure of European national wealth**: about six years of national income, mostly private; public assets ≈ public debt ≈ one year; private wealth half real estate, half financial; net foreign position near balance (p. 400). Source: Part Two series, note 1 points to Table 3.1 (p. 501). Note 2: counting tax-haven assets, Europe's foreign position is "significantly positive" (Figure 12.6) (p. 501).
- **Wealth concentration**: top 10% own 60% of European wealth (p. 401), from ch10.
- **Debt levels**: rich-world average about 90% of GDP; emerging economies about 30% (p. 400). No source given in text.
- **Privatisation equivalence**: sale of all public assets would roughly pay the debt, "according to the national accounts" (p. 401; note 3 cites chs. 3–5 and the online appendix).
- **Simulations**: flat 15% levy ≈ nearly a year of national income (p. 401); 0/1/2% annual schedule ≈ 2% of European GDP (from ch15, p. 389); one-time 0/10/20% ≈ 20% of GDP (p. 403); other schedules in online Supplemental Table S15.1 (note 7, p. 501). The tenfold scaling assumes the tax base does not shrink under much higher rates; behavioural response is not discussed.
- **Historical precedent**: France's 1945 exceptional capital tax, progressive 0–25% (p. 403; note 8 refers to ch10).
- **Inflation arithmetic and history**: 5% vs 2% inflation cuts real debt/GDP >15% (p. 403); France 13% and Germany 17% average inflation 1913–1950 (p. 404); France >50% a year 1945–1948 (p. 405); German prices ×100 million in 1923 (p. 405). Note 10: much of Germany's postwar debt was cancelled by the Allies (citing Albrecht Ritschl, LSE 2012) (p. 502). Note 13: French response to 1945–1948 was the 1956 old-age minimum and PAYGO pensions (p. 502).
- **Austerity arithmetic**: 2% growth, 1% deficit, zero inflation → about 1 point a year off debt/GDP (p. 404; note 11, p. 502). Britain 1815–1914 primary surpluses 2–3% of GDP; debt interest exceeded education spending (p. 404; Chapter 3 cross-reference). European university spending under 1% of GDP (p. 405).
- **Central banks**: Friedman and Schwartz, *A Monetary History of the United States, 1857–1960* (1963) (p. 407; note 19, p. 503); balance sheets Fed and BoE from 10 to >20% of GDP, ECB 15 to 30% (p. 410); private wealth 500–600% of GDP (p. 410); note 22: gross financial assets and liabilities ten to twenty years of GDP; Bank of Japan near 40% of GDP in summer 2013; Banque de France 90–100% in 1944–1945 (pp. 503-504); 2012–2013 government borrowing at just over 1% (pp. 409-410); note 21: 6–7% for Italy and Spain, 15% for Greece in 2011–2012 (p. 503). Note 25: Banque de France governed by its 200 largest shareholders 1803–1936, nationalised 1945 (p. 504). Note 26: ECB's December 2009 collateral announcement on Greek bonds (p. 504).
- **Cyprus**: one million inhabitants, EU 2004, euro 2008; Russian deposits drawn by low taxes; proposed rates 6.75% and 9.9%; replacement exempting deposits under €100,000 (pp. 412-413). Sourced to "statements by officials of the Troika" (p. 412) and conversations with officials (p. 413; note 24, p. 504). Piketty stresses that no depositor statistics were published (p. 412), so the "Russian millionaires" characterisation (p. 413) is itself unverified.
- **Euro**: Europe nearly one-quarter of world GDP in 2013 (p. 414); Maastricht 1992, notes 1 January 2002 (p. 414); rates near-identical 2002–2007 (p. 415).
- **Corporate tax**: Ireland and Eastern European countries' low-rate strategies (p. 417); Google managers' "positive externality" defence (note 33, p. 505); Belgium's 2006 allowance for corporate equity (note 36, p. 506); FTT revenue unlikely above 0.5% of GDP (note 38, p. 506).
- **Golden rule**: Phelps 1961, "A Fable for Growthmen" (p. 419; note 40, p. 506), also Allais 1947, von Neumann 1945, Malinvaud 1953; Keynes's *General Theory* chs. 16 and 24 (note 44, p. 507). The β estimates for r = 1–1.5% (10–15 years) and r = 0.2% (20–30 years) are explicit guesses ("Perhaps", "maybe even more", p. 420).
- **Climate**: Stern Report (2006); damage "dozens of points of global GDP per year" in some scenarios; Stern 1–1.5% discount and ≥5% of GDP mitigation vs Nordhaus 4–4.5% (p. 424). Note 52 cites Guesnerie and Sterner on limited substitutability of natural capital (p. 509). Note 54: net public investment in rich countries about 1–1.5% of GDP (p. 509).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch15**: the 0/1/2% annual schedule and its 2%-of-GDP yield (p. 389), the automatic exchange of bank data, and the Italy/Spain failed national wealth taxes (p. 412 refers back) all come from ch15. The financial cadaster here is ch15's transparency programme turned to crisis management.
- **Depends on Part Two (chs. 3–5)**: the public/private wealth decomposition, Britain's nineteenth-century debt, and the claim that national wealth is at a century high (pp. 400, 404, 423) reuse the U-curve of β. The private-wealth figures of 500–600% of GDP (p. 410) are ch5's.
- **Depends on ch10 and ch12**: unequal returns by size of fortune are the basis for saying inflation spares large portfolios (p. 406; note 15). Top-decile share of 60% (p. 401) is ch10's.
- **ch02 link**: ch02's account of inflation ending nineteenth-century monetary stability is the background for the inflation history here (pp. 403-405).
- **Restatement of r > g**: the golden-rule section gives the clearest one-line statement of why r > g matters for distribution, "the basis of a society of rentiers" (p. 421): with r > g an owner need reinvest only g and can consume r − g. This is the same mechanism as ch10 and is restated in ch17 (p. 427).
- **Internal tension: how hard is the debt?** Debt reduction may take "several decades" (p. 404) without a tax or inflation; public debt is "perhaps not really that difficult to eliminate" (p. 424) and could be erased "at the stroke of a pen (or with a tax on capital, which comes to the same thing)" (p. 425). Consistent only if the capital tax is politically available, which the Cyprus section says it currently is not (pp. 413-414).
- **Internal tension: debt is both a non-problem and urgent.** "The nations of Europe have never been so rich" (p. 423), so the debt-burden-on-our-children story "makes no sense" (p. 423); yet the debt "must be reduced as quickly as possible" (p. 423). The reconciling move is distributive: the cost is interest paid to wealthy creditors instead of investment in education.
- **Tension with ch13 on the social state**: ch16 insists the state must own education, health and security assets (p. 401) yet calls for forms of property between public and private (p. 425); the two are compatible but the chapter gives no model of the intermediate forms.
- **Climate qualifies the growth assumptions**: the book's baseline of 1–1.5% frontier growth (ch02, restated p. 427) does not price natural-capital degradation; ch16 raises it as "the world's principal long-term worry" (p. 424) but does not integrate it into β = s/g or r > g. Note 52's point that natural capital is not infinitely substitutable (p. 509) would, if taken seriously, undercut the standard growth accounting the book uses.
- **Political scale**: the euro section's call for a continental fiscal sovereign (pp. 418-419) is restated in ch17 as "we must bet everything on democracy—and in Europe, democracy on a European scale" (p. 428). Note 35 aligns it with Rodrik's trilemma (pp. 505-506).
- **Cross-book**: the chapter's argument that monetary authorities lack democratic legitimacy for redistribution (pp. 410-411), and its rejection of constitutionalised fiscal rules (pp. 422-423), engage the rule-of-law tradition directly: Piketty accepts rule-bound central banks but rejects rule-bound budgets, the opposite of what a Hayekian reading of "rules fixed in advance" would recommend for fiscal policy. Useful for a themes synthesis on rules versus discretion.

## Open questions

- Would a one-time 10–20% levy on large fortunes raise the projected 20% of GDP, given capital flight before enactment and the credibility problem of announcing it as one-time? The chapter scales the annual schedule by ten without discussing base erosion (p. 403).
- Is inflation's incidence as he describes? Modern holdings of nominal assets (pension funds, insurers) are spread across the middle class; the chapter gives no incidence estimate beyond the 1945–1948 French case (pp. 405-406).
- The causal claim that nineteenth-century debt service retarded British education and caused relative decline (p. 404) is offered as a possibility only. What evidence would support it?
- How would a Eurozone budgetary parliament drawn from national parliaments (p. 416; note 28, p. 504) resolve creditor/debtor conflicts that national electorates have so far refused to delegate?
- Note 41's "α = r if r = g" (p. 507) contradicts the main text's α = s (p. 420): is this a translation or typesetting error? Check against the French edition.
- How does the discount-rate debate connect to the book's r? Piketty lines Nordhaus up with the return on capital and Stern with growth (p. 424), which maps the climate dispute onto r versus g; the analogy is suggestive but not developed.
- What are the "intermediate forms" of property and governance (p. 425)? The chapter names the problem and the need for worker board seats (p. 426) but proposes no design. Later work (*Capital and Ideology*) may be where this is taken up.
