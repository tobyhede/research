# ch03: The Metamorphoses of Capital (pp. 82–101)

## Question this chapter answers

Has the "deep structure" of capital changed since the eighteenth century, or only its form? Using Britain and France, the two countries with the longest series, Piketty answers in two parts. The *size* of national capital relative to income is roughly the same today (five to six years) as in 1700–1914 (six to seven), after a twentieth-century collapse and recovery (the "U-shaped curve"). The *composition* has changed completely: farmland has been replaced by housing and business capital, and foreign assets rose and fell with empire. The second half of the chapter asks what the public/private split adds. Net public wealth has almost always been small compared with private wealth, but public debt and public ownership have pushed private wealth up or down at particular moments, and who holds public debt is a distributional question that representative-agent economics hides.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capital/income ratio (β) | p. 82 | Value of the capital stock divided by annual national income, read as "years of national income". Introduced in ch01; here the organising measure of Part Two (p. 82). Always national (not disposable) income unless stated; dividing by disposable income is called "a dubious methodological choice" (p. 85), justified in ch05 (p. 130). |
| National capital (national wealth) | p. 86 | Private capital plus public capital. Government debt nets out "if each country owns its own government debt" (p. 86). Decomposed as farmland + housing + other domestic capital + net foreign capital (p. 86). |
| Domestic capital | p. 86 | Value of the capital stock located on national territory: farmland, housing (including the land under it), and other domestic capital (p. 86). |
| Other domestic capital | p. 86 | Capital of firms and government organisations: business buildings and land, infrastructure, machinery, computers, patents (p. 86). In the eighteenth century nearly half of it was farm buildings and livestock (note 1, p. 443). |
| Net foreign capital (net foreign assets) | p. 86 | Assets residents own abroad minus assets the rest of the world owns at home, including government bonds (p. 86). |
| Market value | p. 86 | The valuation rule for every asset, e.g. share price for a listed corporation (p. 86). Consequence developed in ch04 (German firms) and ch05 (Tobin's Q). |
| Net wealth | p. 90 | Capital "is always defined as net wealth": market value of assets minus liabilities, for public and private sectors alike (p. 90). |
| Public capital (net public wealth) | p. 90 | Assets of the state and all public agencies minus their liabilities. Public assets are nonfinancial (buildings for administration, health, education) or financial (shareholdings, sovereign wealth funds); the boundary shifts with legal form (p. 90). |
| Private capital | p. 90 | Assets minus liabilities of private individuals (p. 90); ch05 adds nonprofit foundations (pp. 130-131). |
| Rent / rentier | p. 82 | Wealth that exists "to produce rents", i.e. dependable regular payments to owners, typically from land or government bonds (p. 82). The rentier is "a suspect figure in the modern era of democracy and meritocracy" (p. 82). |
| "U-shaped curve" | p. 85 | The twentieth-century path of β in Britain and France: fall of nearly two-thirds 1914–1945, more than doubling 1945–2012 (p. 85). |
| Redistribution via inflation | p. 96 | Reducing the real burden of public debt by inflation, expropriating bondholders; "extremely powerful" but crude in targeting and self-defeating once anticipated (p. 96). |
| Ricardian equivalence | p. 97 | Ricardo's 1817 hypothesis that "under certain conditions, public debt has no effect on the accumulation of national capital" (p. 97); Barro's representative-agent version extends neutrality to the distribution of the fiscal burden (p. 98). |
| Representative agent models | p. 97 | Models in which every agent has the same income and wealth, so owns the same quantity of government bonds; Piketty says they yield "extreme and unrealistic conclusions" about debt (pp. 97-98). |
| Capitalism without capitalists | p. 100 | France 1950–1980: a mixed economy or state capitalism "in which private owners no longer controlled the largest firms" (p. 100). |

## Leading propositions

- In classic nineteenth-century novels wealth takes one of two forms, land or government bonds, valued for steady rents (p. 82); but these two assets differ, since a government bond is a claim of taxpayers' creditors on taxpayers and belongs in private, not national, wealth (p. 83).
- Dynamic, entrepreneurial capital was already present in 1800 (Goriot, Birotteau, Sir Thomas's plantations) (pp. 83-84); capital is risk-taking at inception but tends to turn into rents as it accumulates (p. 84).
- In Britain and France national capital was six to seven years of national income throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries up to 1914, fell to two to three years in the 1950s, and is about five to six years in 2010 (a bit more than six in France) (p. 85).
- The wars of the twentieth century, not a structural change in capitalism, produced the mid-century trough and the "illusion" of a transformed capitalism (p. 85).
- Composition changed utterly: farmland fell from four to five years of national income (nearly two-thirds of national capital) around 1700 to under 10 percent of national income and under 2 percent of wealth today (p. 86); housing rose from about one year to more than three, and other domestic capital from about 1.5 to a little under 3 (pp. 86-87).
- Net foreign assets rose through the nineteenth century to nearly two years of national income in Britain and more than one in France by 1914, collapsed in 1914–1945, and have stayed near zero since 1950 (pp. 87-88).
- Foreign assets let Britain and France run structural trade deficits of 1–2 percent of national income in 1880–1914 while foreign income exceeded 5 percent, so their balance of payments stayed positive and holdings kept growing (p. 88).
- Comparing 1700 with today, net foreign assets are negligible at both ends, and the real long-run change is the replacement of farmland by real estate and working capital, with total capital roughly constant relative to income (pp. 88-89).
- Net public wealth today is about zero in Britain and slightly under 30 percent of national income in France; private wealth is more than 99 percent and about 95 percent of national wealth respectively, and "certainly greater than 90 percent" (pp. 90-91).
- Over three centuries net public wealth has ranged roughly between +100 and −100 percent of national income, small against private wealth of up to 700–800 percent, so the history of national β is largely the history of private β (p. 91).
- Britain's public debt reached about 200 percent of national income in the 1810s (and again in 1950), was never defaulted on, and took a century of primary surpluses to reduce (pp. 92-94); it was financed by extra private saving, so national capital stayed near seven years while private wealth rose above eight (p. 93).
- Nineteenth-century public debt served the lenders: zero inflation 1815–1914 and bond yields of 4–5 percent, "significantly higher than the growth rate" (p. 94). This is the first appearance in the historical narrative of r > g as a distributional mechanism.
- Twentieth-century debt was "drowned by inflation" instead (p. 95): French prices rose about a hundredfold 1913–1950 (p. 95), British about threefold (p. 96).
- Inflation as redistribution is crude (bondholders are not always the richest) and cannot work indefinitely because lenders adapt (p. 96).
- Representative-agent models, and Barro's reading of Ricardian equivalence, miss that public debt is owned by a minority and is therefore a vehicle of internal redistribution whether repaid or not (pp. 97-98).
- Public assets rose from about 50 percent of national income in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries to about 100 percent at the end of the twentieth (150 percent in France, 100 percent in Britain in 2010) (p. 98).
- In 1950 the French state owned 25–30 percent of national wealth, and more than half in the industrial and financial sectors 1950–1980 (pp. 99-100); after 1986 privatisation reversed this, and France "totally transformed its national capital structure at two different points in time without really understanding why" (p. 101).

## Arguments

**A. Size constant, composition transformed (pp. 84-89)**

- **Premises**: (p. 86) National capital = farmland + housing + other domestic capital + net foreign capital, all at market value. (pp. 86-87) Series for Britain and France (Figures 3.1-3.2) show farmland collapsing as agriculture's share of activity fell from nearly three-quarters to a few percent, offset by rises in housing and other domestic capital. (p. 85) The total stays between six and seven years before 1914 and returns to five to six by 2010.
- **Conclusion**: "The nature of capital has changed" (p. 87) but it "has lost none of its importance" (p. 87); the mid-century fall was a war effect, not a structural change (p. 85).
- **Unstated premise**: that market valuations of assets as different as eighteenth-century farmland and twenty-first-century shares are comparable in "years of income". The chapter treats market value as the only possible aggregator; ch04 (p. 107) makes this explicit and admits the "arbitrariness".

**B. Public debt is a claim within the population, not national wealth (pp. 83, 86, 97)**

- **Premises**: (p. 83) A bond is a claim of bondholders on taxpayers. (p. 86) In the national balance sheet it is an asset of the private sector and a liability of the public sector, and nets out if held domestically.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 83) Bonds belong in private wealth only; (p. 97) Ricardo correctly saw Britain's debt as "a claim of one portion of the population on another".
- **Consequence drawn**: national β can be stable while private β rises (Britain 1770–1810, p. 93), so the private/public split matters for distribution even when national totals do not move.

**C. Who gains from public debt depends on r, g and inflation (pp. 93-96)**

- **Premises**: (p. 94) Lending to the state and receiving interest for decades beats paying taxes without compensation. (p. 94) Deficits raise demand for private wealth and so its return. (p. 94) In 1815–1914 inflation was about zero and bond yields 4–5 percent, above growth. (p. 94) Worked example: deficits of 5 percent of GDP for twenty years add debt of 100 percent of GDP; at 5 percent interest the state pays 5 percent of GDP a year to bondholders indefinitely if it never repays principal.
- **Conclusion**: Britain's debt "enhanced the influence of private wealth" (p. 93) and served lenders "quite well" (p. 93); Marx and nineteenth-century socialists were right to see debt as a tool of private capital (p. 94).
- **Contrast case**: (p. 95) In the twentieth century inflation turned debt into a transfer *from* lenders, making deficit finance progressive in effect; Piketty says this view persists although inflation has fallen and the distributional effects are now "relatively obscure" (p. 95).

**D. Against representative-agent debt neutrality (pp. 97-98)**

- **Premises**: (p. 97) Representative-agent models give every agent the same bonds. (p. 98) In fact public debt is held by a minority, and wealth has always been highly concentrated.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 98) Barro's neutrality result ignores the internal redistribution debt effects "when it is repaid as well as when it is not"; to study debt without inequality "is in fact to say nothing" about what is at stake.
- **Note**: the argument is conceptual, not empirical. No data on the distribution of bondholding are given here (he defers to "literary sources", p. 93).

**E. Public ownership as political choice (pp. 98-101)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 98-99) The Depression discredited laissez-faire; the Soviet war role raised the prestige of statism (Schumpeter 1942, Samuelson 1970); in France collaboration charges against the business elite led to nationalisations (Renault, banks, coal). (p. 99) By 1950 French public assets exceeded a year of national income with debt inflated away.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 100) France had a "capitalism without capitalists" during the Trente Glorieuses; (p. 101) after 1986 it returned to private ownership "without really understanding why".

## Evidence offered

- **Literary sources** as an introduction to wealth composition: Balzac (*Père Goriot*, *César Birotteau*), Austen (*Sense and Sensibility*, *Mansfield Park*), Proust (pp. 82-84, 95-96). Piketty marks their limits: Austen's landlords and bondholders "were largely the same people, if literary sources count as reliable historical sources" (p. 93). Literature illustrates; it is not the evidence for the series.
- **National balance-sheet series** for Britain and France 1700–2010 (Figures 3.1-3.6). Every figure cites only "piketty.pse.ens.fr/capital21c" (pp. 84-92). The chapter text gives no underlying sources; the endnotes name the historical estimators: King and Petty for Britain, Vauban and Boisguillebert for France (note 1, p. 443), and for the twentieth century Campion (1939), Revell (1967), Giffen, Colson, and Divisia-Dupin-Roy (note 19, p. 444). Most numerical details are deferred to the online appendix (notes 4-6, 9-10, 12-14, pp. 443-444).
- **Current official estimates** of public assets and debt from national statistical offices and central banks (p. 90): Britain public assets 93 percent and debt 92 percent of national income (net +1 percent); France 145 and 114 percent (net +31 percent) (note 10, p. 443). Table 3.1 is referenced (p. 90) but did not survive extraction.
- **Measurement caveats stated by Piketty**: national accounting "is not, and never will be, an exact science"; public debt and financial assets are easy to measure, but public buildings and infrastructure rarely sell and are priced by comparison (p. 91). He argues the conclusion (private wealth above 90 percent of national wealth) survives even a large undervaluation of public assets (p. 91). The 1950 French figures may undervalue public relative to private assets because asset prices were at historic lows (p. 99).
- **Public debt history**: Britain and France about 50 percent of national income 1700–1720 and 100 percent 1760–1770 (p. 93); France below 20 percent in 1815 after the 1797 "two-thirds bankruptcy" (p. 93, citing Crouzet, note 11, p. 443); Britain nearly 200 percent in the 1810s (p. 93); British primary surplus of 2–3 percent of GDP 1815–1914, against an education budget under 2 percent (note 12, pp. 443-444); British growth nearly 2.5 percent a year 1815–1914 (p. 94); France 70–80 percent vs Britain under 50 percent in 1880–1914, French interest payments 2–3 percent of national income (p. 95).
- **Inflation**: France more than 13 percent a year 1913–1950 (prices ×100) and above 50 percent a year 1945–1948; Britain a little over 3 percent a year 1913–1950 (prices ×3), more than 4 percent in the 1950s and nearly 15 percent in the 1970s (pp. 95-96).
- **Foreign assets and trade**: British foreign assets "barely 10 percent" of national income in 1812, "as far as we can tell from the available sources" (p. 87); about 5 percent yield around 1900 (p. 87); trade deficits of 1–2 percent of national income 1880–1914 (p. 88), annual series deferred to the appendix (note 6, p. 443); since 1950 net foreign holdings between −10 and +10 percent (note 7, p. 443).
- **Per-capita illustration**: today 30,000 euros income, 180,000 euros capital per head, split roughly 90,000 housing / 90,000 other (p. 89). The text's 1700 and 1910 splits use six years; the endnotes revise both to seven years (210,000 euros) with 150,000 in land in 1700 and about 90,000 other domestic capital in 1910 (notes 8-9, p. 443). The rounded text figures are inconsistent with the seven-year totals stated earlier in the chapter.
- **Authorities**: Ricardo 1817 (p. 97; note 15 concedes Ricardo is "not without ambiguity" and points to Gregory Clark's work on crowding out, p. 444); Keynes 1936 (p. 97); Barro 1974 and 1987 (note 16, p. 444); Schumpeter 1942 and Samuelson's 8th edition (p. 99; note 17, p. 444); Marx's *Class Struggles in France* (p. 95).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch01**: definitions of national income, national capital, domestic vs foreign capital, and the first law α = r × β are recalled, not re-derived (pp. 86, 90).
- **Feeds ch05**: the stable six-to-seven-year level is the fact the second law β = s / g is built to explain; ch05 (p. 141) then argues farmland was mostly accumulated capital, not pure land, which is what makes the eighteenth-century level explicable by saving.
- **Feeds ch10 and ch11**: the observation that bondholding and landholding were concentrated in a small class (pp. 93, 98) is asserted here and evidenced only later from estate records.
- **Feeds ch16**: the inflation-versus-repayment history of public debt (pp. 93-96) is the historical basis for the Part Four discussion of taxing capital, inflation and austerity as ways out of debt. The judgement that inflation is crude and self-limiting (p. 96) sets up that chapter.
- **Proto r > g (my observation)**: the 1815–1914 bond yield of 4–5 percent "significantly higher than the growth rate" (p. 94) is the first concrete historical use of r > g. Note that this r is a nominal yield on a riskless asset in a zero-inflation regime, not the average return on all capital the book later uses.
- **Tension on "dynamic" capital (my observation)**: the chapter opens by saying modern capital only *seems* more dynamic (p. 84) and closes ch05 with the suggestion that faster depreciation of modern capital may contribute to the impression of dynamism (ch05, p. 141). Whether entrepreneurial capital differs in its distributional effect from rentier capital is not settled by showing β is constant.
- **Tension on public debt neutrality (my observation)**: the chapter accepts that British debt 1770–1810 left national capital unchanged, i.e. accepts Ricardo's empirical observation for that episode (pp. 93, 97), while attacking Barro's generalisation. ch04 note 8 (p. 445) says the 1914–1945 deficits, by contrast, did reduce national capital. So the claim is conditional neutrality, depending on whether there is a prosperous class able to save more.
- **Measurement link to ch04 and ch05**: the market-value rule (p. 86) is innocuous here but becomes decisive for Germany (ch04, p. 104) and for Tobin's Q (ch05, pp. 135-137).

## Open questions

- How reliable are eighteenth-century balance sheets built from King, Petty, Vauban and Boisguillebert (note 1, p. 443)? These are contemporary conjectural estimates, not records; the U-curve's left arm rests on them. Check the online appendix or later critiques before treating 1700–1800 levels as data.
- Is the composition change (land to housing and business capital) more than relabelling? Note 1 says half of eighteenth-century "other domestic capital" was farm buildings and livestock, and ch05 says farmland value was mostly accumulated improvements (p. 141). The categories are partly an accounting convention.
- The chapter's per-capita examples use six years for 1700 and 1910 while the series show seven (notes 8-9, p. 443). Minor, but any synthesis quoting the 120,000 euros of land in 1700 should use the note's 150,000.
- Who actually held public debt in nineteenth-century Britain and France? The distributional claim (pp. 93, 98) rests on literary evidence and on later wealth-concentration data; it needs the ch10 estate series to be tested.
- The rate on nineteenth-century bonds (4–5 percent) is quoted as exceeding growth (p. 94). Did British bondholders' heirs actually accumulate, or consume the interest? The answer depends on saving out of capital income, which ch10 and ch11 model.
- "Without really understanding why" (p. 101) is a strong interpretive claim about French politics with no evidence offered in the chapter.
