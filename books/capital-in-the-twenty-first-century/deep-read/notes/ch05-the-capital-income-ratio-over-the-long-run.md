# ch05: The Capital/Income Ratio over the Long Run (pp. 118–142)

## Question this chapter answers

What determines the level of the capital/income ratio in the long run? Why has it returned to historical highs in Europe and Japan since the 1970s, and why is it structurally higher in Europe than in the United States? Piketty's answer is the "second fundamental law of capitalism", β = s / g: an economy that saves a net fraction s of its income and grows at rate g tends toward a capital/income ratio of s / g. Slow growth, especially slow population growth, combined with steady saving produces high β. He then uses the law to account for 1970–2010 in the eight richest countries, adding two reinforcing factors (privatisation and an asset-price catch-up), and to project a world β of about 700 percent by 2100. The law is the core of Part Two and, with α = r × β (ch01) and r > g (Introduction, ch10), one of the three pieces of the book's formal apparatus.

## Key terms

Applies to national capital or to private capital depending on context; the 1970–2010 analysis uses private capital (p. 123). |
| s (savings rate) | p. 119 | Net new saving divided by national income: saving "net of capital depreciation, that is, truly new savings" (p. 128); see also note 2 (p. 446). Private saving includes household saving and firms' retained earnings (p. 127). |
| g (growth rate) | p. 120 | Growth rate of *total* national income, i.e. per capita growth plus population growth (p. 120). Note 3 (p. 446) gives the alternative notation β = s / (g + n). |
| Second fundamental law of capitalism (β = s / g) | p. 119 | The long-run equilibrium capital/income ratio toward which an economy tends if it saves s and grows at g permanently (pp. 119-120). Asymptotic, dynamic, "never perfectly realized in practice" (p. 121). |
| First fundamental law (α = r × β) | p. 121 | Recalled from ch01: capital's share of income α equals the average return r times β. "a pure accounting identity, valid at all times in all places" (p. 121), in contrast to the dynamic second law. |
| Accumulable capital vs pure natural resources | p. 121 | β = s / g covers only capital humans can accumulate. Pure natural resources, "whose value is independent of any human improvement and any past investment", can raise β without saving (p. 121). |
| Pure land | p. 141 | Land "prior to any human improvements" (p. 141); estimated at no more than half to one year of national income in both eighteenth-century farmland and twenty-first-century urban land (pp. 141-142). |
| Relative asset prices; volume vs price effects | pp. 121-122 | Asset prices relative to consumer prices. | Retained earnings | p. 127 | Profits reinvested by firms on behalf of their owners; up to half of private saving in some countries (p. 127). Counting them as saving turns most apparent long-run share-price gains into volume effects (p. 127). |

| Durable goods / valuables | pp. 128-129 | Household durables (30–50 percent of national income) are excluded from wealth, following national-accounting standards; valuables (art, jewellery, gold, 5–10 percent) are included (pp. 128-129). |
| Disposable income | p. 129 | National income minus taxes and obligatory payments plus monetary transfers; about 70–80 percent of national income today, 90 percent before 1900 (pp. 129-130). Rejected as β's denominator because it omits transfers in kind (p. 130). |

## Leading propositions

- In the long run β = s / g; with s = 12 percent and g = 2 percent, β = 600 percent (p. 119).
- "in a quasi-stagnant society, wealth accumulated in the past will inevitably acquire disproportionate importance" (p. 119).
- The return to a high β in the twenty-first century is explained by the return to slow growth; "Decreased growth—especially demographic growth—is thus responsible for capital’s comeback" (p. 119).
- Small changes in g have large effects on β: at s = 12 percent, β is 8 at g = 1.5 percent and 12 at g = 1 percent (p. 119); at g = 3 percent β = 4, and with s = 9 percent β = 3 (pp. 119-120).
- Because g includes population growth, countries with the same per capita growth can have very different β: Europe (near-zero population growth, g = 1.5–2 percent) six to eight years; the US (g = 2.5–3 percent) three to four years (p. 120).
- The law is valid only (i) asymptotically, (ii) for accumulable capital, and (iii) if relative asset prices are stable on average over the long run (pp. 120-122). It takes decades to become true (p. 121).
- The law is independent of the motives for saving (p. 122): it holds because s / g is "the only stable capital/income ratio" for an economy saving s and growing at g (p. 122).
- The law explains the equilibrium toward which β tends, not short-run shocks such as wars or 1929 (p. 123).
- Annual series for 1970–2010 show large short-run swings from volatile asset prices and bubbles (pp. 123-124), but a clear trend: private wealth rose from two to three and a half years of national income in 1970 to four to seven in 2010 in all eight rich countries (p. 124).
- Three factors explain the comeback: slower growth with high saving (dominant in the long run), privatisation of public wealth, and a long-run catch-up of real estate and stock prices (p. 125).
- Per capita growth 1970–2010 was 1.6–2.0 percent in all eight rich countries; differences in population growth were much larger, giving total growth of about 3 percent in the US, Canada and Australia against about 2 percent in Europe and Japan (p. 125).
- Private saving was generally 10–12 percent of national income, but 7–8 percent in the US and Britain and 14–15 percent in Japan and Italy (p. 126).
- Private wealth predicted by 1970–2010 savings flows plus 1970 wealth is close to actual 2010 wealth in most countries (p. 126), Britain excepted; volume effects dominate in the long run (p. 127). Japan's rise from three to six years of income is "predicted almost perfectly by the flow of savings" (p. 127).
- Retained earnings must be counted as private saving; otherwise one would misattribute share-price growth to relative price effects (p. 127).
- The relevant s is net of depreciation; gross saving of 25–30 percent of national income leaves net saving of 10–15 percent (p. 128).
- Expressed in disposable income, private capital in the 2000s would be five to nine years rather than four to seven (p. 130); Piketty prefers national income because the gap measures public services in kind (p. 130).
- Public dissaving and the fall in public wealth account for one-tenth to one-quarter of the increase in private wealth 1970–2010 (p. 133); in Italy private saving was about 15 percent but national saving under 10 percent (p. 133).
- Private enrichment in Russia and Eastern Europe after 1990 "obviously had nothing to do with saving or the dynamic law β = s / g"; it was a transfer of ownership (p. 134).
- Over 1870–2010 or 1910–2010 the evolution of β is "very well explained" by β = s / g, but the 1950s trough was lower than the law predicts because asset prices were depressed (p. 134); the post-1950 rebound in asset prices has broadly offset the 1910–1950 fall (p. 134).
- The price of capital "is always in part a social and political construct" (p. 135).
- Tobin's Q has risen in rich countries since 1970; the asset-price rebound explains one-quarter to one-third of the increase in national β from 1970 to 2010 (p. 137).
- β = s / g can produce large international imbalances: countries with higher s or lower g accumulate more capital and invest abroad (p. 138); Japan's net foreign assets are about 70 percent of national income and Germany's nearly 50 percent (p. 138).
- Gross cross-border positions are far larger than net ones and make small European countries vulnerable to valuation "errors" (p. 139).
- The world β followed a U-curve and is now close to 500 percent; with g falling from 3 percent to 1.5 percent and s about 10 percent it could approach 700 percent by 2100 (p. 140).
- β = s / g explains 80–100 percent of observed capital in 2010, so pure land must be a small part of national capital (p. 141); even eighteenth-century farmland was mostly accumulated improvements (p. 141).

## Arguments

**A. Reconstruction of β = s / g and its convergence (pp. 119-123; notes 2-5, p. 446)**

Piketty gives the law, a numerical example and a verbal stability argument; the equation is in the online appendix (note 5, p. 446). The reconstruction below fills that gap and is mine, but every step is licensed by the text.

1. Notation (pp. 119-120, 128): K = capital (market value), Y = national income, s = net saving / Y, g = growth rate of Y.
2. Accumulation with no relative price change (pp. 121-122): new net saving is the only source of new capital, so K grows by sY each year. Then the growth rate of capital is sY / K = s / β. Piketty states exactly this: "the capital stock grows at a rate equal to s / β" (p. 446), in note 4.
3. β is constant only if capital and income grow at the same rate: s / β = g, i.e. β = s / g (p. 122).
4. Stability (pp. 122-123): if β < s / g, capital grows faster than income (s / β > g) and β rises; if β > s / g, capital grows slower and β falls. So s / g is "the only stable capital/income ratio" (p. 122).
5. In discrete time β(t+1) = (β(t) + s) / (1 + g), whose fixed point satisfies β(1 + g) = β + s, i.e. β = s / g. The gap from equilibrium shrinks by the factor 1 / (1 + g) each year. *Consequence (mine)*: convergence speed is set by g itself. At g = 2 percent the half-life of a deviation is about 35 years; at g = 1 percent about 70 years. The slower the growth, the higher the equilibrium β *and* the longer it takes to get there. This is the formal content of "it will take several decades for the law β = s / g to become true" (p. 121).
6. Check on Piketty's example (pp. 120-121): starting from zero capital with s = 12 percent, g = 2 percent, the formula gives β after fifty years of 6 × (1 − 1.02^−50), about 3.8, not 6. This matches his statement that fifty years of saving do not produce a ratio of 6 "because national income will itself have increased considerably" (p. 121).

**Conditions under which Piketty says the law holds** (the load-bearing list):

- **Long run only, with permanent s and g** "this is an asymptotic law" (p. 120). It is "the result of a dynamic process" whose equilibrium is "never perfectly realized in practice" (p. 121).
- **Only accumulable capital** (p. 121): pure natural resources add to β independently of saving. The equilibrium may be "possibly augmented by pure natural resources" (p. 123).
- **Relative asset prices stable on average over the long run** (pp. 121-122): if real estate or share prices outpace consumer prices, β can be high without any new saving (pp. 121-122). If price variations balance out, "the law β = s / g is necessarily valid" (p. 122).
- **s is net of depreciation** (p. 128; note 2, p. 446). In gross terms the law becomes β = s / (g + δ) (note 12, p. 447).
- **g is total growth** of national income, per capita plus population (p. 120; note 3, p. 446).
- **Retained earnings are included in s** (pp. 127-128); otherwise an accounting convention reclassifies volume as price.
- **Durable goods excluded, valuables included** (pp. 128-129), as in the international national-accounting standards.
- **Independent of the motives for saving** (p. 122). The law says nothing about why s is what it is.
- **Not a theory of shocks** (p. 123).
- **Implicit, not stated in this chapter**: s and g are treated as exogenous parameters, and ch06 adds that they are "largely independent of each other" (ch06, p. 142). For national capital, s must be *national* saving; applied to private capital (as in the 1970–2010 analysis), private saving overstates accumulation when public saving is negative (Italy, p. 133). For an open economy, net foreign assets also change through returns on gross positions, not only through saving (p. 139; note 31, p. 450).

**Contrast with the first law** (p. 121): α = r × β is an identity, "a definition of the share of capital" (p. 121) or of r, and holds at every moment. β = s / g is a behavioural-accounting equilibrium. Combined, they give α = r × s / g, which is how Part Two links slow growth to a rising capital share (ch06), *provided r does not fall enough as β rises*. That proviso is not addressed here.

**B. Why Europe's β is higher than America's (pp. 120, 125-126, 134)**

- **Premises**: (p. 125) Per capita growth was 1.6–2.0 percent everywhere. (p. 125) Population growth was under 0.5 percent in Europe and Japan and 1.0–1.5 percent in the US, Canada and Australia. (p. 126) Saving was higher in Japan and Italy, lower in the US and Britain. (note 20, p. 448) 1870–2010 total growth was 2–2.2 percent in Europe and 3.4 percent in the US.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 126) Differences in growth rates "largely explain" why β is structurally higher in Europe and Japan; (p. 134) the long-run Europe/US gap is "perfectly consistent" with s / g.

**C. Volume effects dominate prices in the long run (pp. 126-127, 134, 137)**

- **Premises**: (p. 126) Cumulating 1970–2010 savings on 1970 wealth predicts 2010 wealth well in most countries. (note 10, p. 447) For Japan the series rose from 299 to 601 percent of national income against a predicted 616, so saving explains about 95 percent of the increase. (p. 127) Retained earnings explain most long-run share-price growth.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 127) Asset prices "always dominate in the short and even medium run but tend to balance out over the long run, where volume effects appear generally to be decisive".
- **Qualification by Piketty**: (p. 137) the asset-price rebound still explains one-quarter to one-third of the increase in national β 1970–2010, and (p. 134) the 1950s trough needs low asset prices to explain it. His reconciliation: the 1970–2010 price rise is a *catch-up* that offsets the 1910–1950 fall, so over the century relative prices balance out (p. 134).

**D. Privatisation transfers wealth without creating it (pp. 132-134)**

- **Premises**: (p. 132) Public capital fell and private capital rose in all eight countries 1970–2010. (p. 132) In Italy public wealth fell by nearly a year of income while private wealth rose by about four and a half years. (p. 133) Government deficits exceeded public investment by 2–3 percent of national income in France, Britain, Germany and the US, and by over 6 percent in Italy.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 133) Public dissaving explains one-tenth to one-quarter of the rise in private wealth; part of private wealth is "a growing debt that one portion of the Italian population owed to another" (p. 132). Russia after 1990 is the extreme case of pure transfer (pp. 133-134).

**E. The land "mystery" is small (pp. 141-142)**

- **Premises**: (p. 141) Eighteenth-century farmland was worth about four years of national income. (p. 141) Labour invested annually in clearing, draining and fencing was 3–4 percent of national income. (p. 141) Growth was under 1 percent a year.
- **Inference (s / g applied to land improvements)**: an investment rate of 3–4 percent with growth under 1 percent cumulates to roughly the whole value of the land, so pure land was "at most one year of national income, and probably less than half a year" (p. 141). Paine's "unimproved land" estimate of about one-tenth of national wealth agrees (p. 141).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 141) "even in a traditional society, the bulk of national capital already stemmed from accumulation and investment". Modern capital's faster depreciation may contribute to the impression that modern capital is more dynamic (p. 141).
- **Caveat by Piketty**: (p. 141) when growth is low, small differences in investment produce enormous differences in β = s / g, so these estimates are "inevitably highly approximate".

## Evidence offered

- **National balance sheets**, eight richest countries 1970–2010, annual (Figures 5.3, 5.5, 5.7) and Europe/US 1870–2010 decennial (Figures 5.1-5.2). Europe is the income-weighted average of Germany, France, Britain and Italy (note 1, p. 446). Sources are stated as national accounts following "international standards" (pp. 128-129) and the online appendix; all figures cite only "piketty.pse.ens.fr/capital21c".
- **Tables 5.1-5.4** (growth and saving rates; components of private saving; gross and net saving; public saving) are referred to (pp. 125-128, 133) but did not survive extraction. Their key values are restated in the text: per capita growth 1.6–2.0 percent, population growth under 0.5 vs 1.0–1.5 percent (p. 125); saving 7–15 percent (p. 126); depreciation 10–15 percent of national income (p. 128).
- **Savings-flow simulation**: predicted vs actual 2010 private wealth by country (p. 126; Supplemental Figure S5.1, note 9, p. 447); worked Japanese figures (note 10, p. 447). Britain is the stated misfit (p. 126), explained conjecturally by undervalued public assets in the 1970s and cheap privatisations (p. 133).
- **1970 and 2010 ranges**: 2.2 years (Germany) to 3.4 (US) in 1970; 4.1 (Germany, US) to 6.1 (Japan) and 6.8 (Italy) in 2010 (notes 6-7, p. 446).
- **Durables and valuables**: national-accounts estimates for 1970–2010 and Gregory King's estimates for Britain around 1700 (p. 129); gold's share of private wealth (note 14, p. 447).
- **Foundations and church property**: France 1 percent, Japan 3–4 percent, US 6–7 percent of private wealth; eighteenth-century French church property 7–8 percent of private wealth (p. 131), source in the online appendix (note 17, p. 448).
- **Tobin's Q**: Figure 5.6; CAC 40 firms ranged from 20 percent to over 340 percent in 2012, from an accounting firm report (p. 136; note 23, p. 448). National Q: US 100–120 percent, Britain 120–140 percent, France about 80 percent, Germany and Japan 50–70 percent (pp. 136-137).
- **Former Soviet bloc**: "highly imperfect estimates" of private wealth of about four years now and public capital of three to four years before 1990 (p. 133).
- **World β 1870–2100**: Europe and North America reliable from 1870; Japan from incomplete data including probate records from 1905 (p. 140); the rest of the world (just over a fifth of global output) estimated from 1990 at about four years and *assumed* constant back to 1870 (p. 140). The projection uses ch02's growth scenario and an assumed s of 10 percent (p. 140).
- **Pure land**: contemporary eighteenth-century estimates of improvements (p. 141), Paine's *Agrarian Justice* (1795) (p. 141), Goldsmith on ancient Rome (note 33, p. 450). Piketty's own estimate of urban pure land, half to one year of national income, comes with "substantial" uncertainty (p. 142).
- **Gross financial positions**: central bank and statistical agency balance sheets of primary financial assets, excluding derivatives (p. 139; note 28, p. 449).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch01 and ch02**: α = r × β (ch01) and the growth projections (ch02) are inputs; the 2100 forecast is only as good as ch02's assumption that global growth falls to 1.5 percent.
- **Feeds ch06**: α = r × β with β = s / g gives a rising capital share only if r falls less than proportionally as β rises, i.e. an elasticity of substitution between capital and labour above one. ch06 takes this up. The present chapter is silent on what happens to r as β rises toward 7.
- **Feeds ch10-ch11**: high β in a slow-growth society is the precondition for inheritance's return and for r > g to bite; "quasi-stagnant society" (p. 119) is the bridge.
- **Feeds ch12**: international imbalances from differing s / g (p. 138) and natural-resource wealth (p. 142) are deferred to the chapter on global wealth and oil exporters.
- **Tension on how much is "explained" (my observation)**: the chapter says the 1970–2010 rise in private wealth is explained "largely" by s / g (p. 132) and "without assuming a significant structural increase in the relative price of assets" (p. 127), yet also attributes one-tenth to one-quarter to privatisation (p. 133) and one-quarter to one-third to asset prices (p. 137). The figures are not on a common base: p. 133 concerns private wealth, p. 137 national capital. Read together, saving explains perhaps half to two-thirds of the rise in national β, not "nearly the entirety". Piketty reconciles by calling the price rise a catch-up (p. 134), which makes the long-run claim depend on the 1910–1950 price fall being of the same size, a claim he himself calls approximate (pp. 134-135).
- **Tension on price neutrality (my observation)**: the law needs relative asset prices to be neutral in the long run (pp. 121-122), but the chapter argues that the price of capital is "a social and political construct" (p. 135), set by rent control, governance law and regulation, and ch04 shows German β would rise by one to two years at book value (ch04, p. 104). If institutions durably set prices, the "price effect" is not noise around an s / g equilibrium but a second determinant of β.
- **Tension on classification (my observation)**: whether a rise in share prices is "price" or "volume" depends on counting retained earnings as saving (p. 127). This is defensible, but it means the headline finding (volume dominates) is partly produced by an accounting choice. Piketty is explicit that household saving alone would point to price effects (p. 127).
- **Net vs gross saving (my observation, external debate)**: the law holds net s fixed as g changes. As g goes to zero with fixed net s, β grows without limit, which would require gross saving to absorb nearly all income through depreciation. If instead gross saving is the stable behavioural parameter, β = s / (g + δ) (note 12, p. 447) stays bounded by s / δ. Piketty states both forms but defends neither as the behavioural one. This choice matters most for his slow-growth projection (p. 140), which assumes net s stays at 10 percent while g halves.
- **Link to ch03 on public debt**: the Italian case (pp. 132-133) is the 1970–2010 analogue of Britain 1770–1810 (ch03, p. 93): private wealth rising faster than national wealth because private saving funds public deficits.
- **Link to ch04**: the 1950s trough "lower than the simple logic of accumulation" predicts (p. 134) relies on ch04's decomposition (one-quarter to one-third price effects, ch04 p. 108).

## Open questions

- Is s independent of g and r? The chapter says the law holds "regardless of the exact reasons" for saving (p. 122), but the projections need s to stay near 10 percent as growth halves (p. 140). The evidence offered is that rich-country private saving clusters at 10–12 percent (p. 126); is that evidence of stability across growth regimes or only across countries at similar growth?
- Should the behavioural parameter be net or gross saving (note 12, p. 447)? Any synthesis that leans on the 700 percent projection should state which.
- What happens to r as β rises? If r falls proportionally, α stays constant and much of the distributional argument weakens. Check ch06's elasticity-of-substitution argument and its evidence.
- How robust is the world β series given that a fifth of world output is assumed at four years for 1870–1990 (p. 140)? And how robust is the US series given that it is market-valued and excludes slaves after 1865?
- Does the British misfit (p. 126) undermine the savings-flow test, or is the undervalued-public-assets explanation (p. 133) supported by anything beyond conjecture? Note 19 (p. 448) cites only the small number of public-asset transactions.
- Pure urban land is estimated at half to one year of national income with "substantial" uncertainty (p. 142). Given that housing is now about half of national capital (ch03, p. 89), is the claim that land price rises explain little of the rebound sensitive to how land and structures are separated in housing values?
- The "only stable ratio" argument (p. 122) assumes s and g fixed. Under the book's own account of shocks (ch04), how often has any country been close enough to equilibrium for β = s / g to be tested directly, rather than through the savings-flow simulation?
