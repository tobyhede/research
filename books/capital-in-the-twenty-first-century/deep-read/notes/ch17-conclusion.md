# ch17: Conclusion (pp. 426–432)

*Boundary note: the Conclusion begins partway down p. 426, after the end of ch16's section on economic transparency; its last sentence ends at the top of p. 432, where the Notes begin. Its two endnotes are on p. 510.*

## Question this chapter answers

What, in the end, has the book shown, how certain is it, and what kind of economics produced it? Piketty restates the whole argument in a few pages: a market economy left to itself has forces of convergence (diffusion of knowledge and skills) and forces of divergence, the principal one being r > g, which he now calls "The Central Contradiction of Capitalism" (p. 426). With frontier growth of 1–1.5% and returns of 4–5%, r > g is likely to become the norm again (p. 427); the remedy is a progressive annual tax on capital, which requires international cooperation and, in Europe, democracy on a European scale (pp. 427-428). He then defends "political and historical economics": economics as a subdiscipline of the social sciences whose distinguishing feature is its normative purpose, sceptical of mathematical modelling and of a "scientistic" use of controlled experiments, and resting on historical experience as "our principal source of knowledge" (pp. 428-430). He ends with the claim that everyone, especially citizens, should take money and its measurement seriously, because "Refusing to deal with numbers rarely serves the interests of the least well-off" (pp. 431-432).

## Leading propositions

**Status of the conclusions**

- The book presents the current state of historical knowledge on the distribution of wealth and income since the eighteenth century and draws lessons for the century ahead (p. 426).
- Its sources are "more extensive than any previous author has assembled" but "imperfect and incomplete"; all conclusions are "by nature tenuous and deserve to be questioned and debated" (p. 426).
- Social science should not try to produce "mathematical certainties" that substitute for open democratic debate (p. 426).

**The restated argument**

- A market economy based on private property, left to itself, contains powerful forces of convergence and powerful forces of divergence; the latter threaten democratic societies and social justice (p. 426).
- The principal destabilising force is that r can exceed g "significantly" and "for long periods of time" (pp. 426-427).
- r > g means past wealth grows faster than output and wages; the entrepreneur tends to become a rentier; "The past devours the future" (p. 427).
- The consequences are "potentially terrifying", especially since returns rise with the size of the initial stake and the divergence is occurring globally (p. 427).
- There is no simple solution: education, knowledge and clean technology can encourage growth, but will not raise it to 4–5%; only catching-up economies grow that fast, and frontier growth will likely not exceed 1–1.5% "no matter what economic policies are adopted" (p. 427).
- With a 4–5% return, r > g is likely to become the norm again, as it was until the eve of World War I; the two world wars created "the illusion" that it had been overcome (p. 427).
- Taxing capital income heavily and indiscriminately enough to push r below g would risk "killing the motor of accumulation" and further lowering growth (p. 427).
- The right solution is a progressive annual tax on capital (p. 427).
- Immense wealth inequalities have "little to do with the entrepreneurial spirit" and do not promote growth, nor are they of "common utility" (p. 428).
- The capital tax needs international cooperation and regional political integration, beyond the reach of the nation-states where earlier social compromises were made (p. 428).
- Pure and perfect competition cannot alter r > g, which is "not the consequence of any market “imperfection.”" (p. 428).
- Despite the risk that European integration erodes existing social states, there is no genuine alternative: "we must bet everything on democracy—and in Europe, democracy on a European scale" (p. 428). The US and China have more options; small European countries withdrawing nationally would face worse frustration (p. 428).

**Political and historical economics**

- Economics is a subdiscipline of the social sciences; the term "economic science" is arrogant; "political economy" conveys its normative purpose (p. 428).
- Political economy asks what policies and institutions bring us closer to an ideal society, and this aspiration to study good and evil is "necessary, indeed indispensable" even if often unfulfilled (pp. 428-429).
- Social scientists, like all citizens, should take stands on specific institutions and policies, not invoke abstract principles; the idea that scholars deal in means and citizens in ends is "dangerous" (p. 429).
- Economists' "immoderate use of mathematical models" is often an excuse "for occupying the terrain and masking the vacuity of the content" (p. 429).
- Controlled experiments are useful in moderation and have turned economists toward concrete questions, but they succumb to "a certain scientistic illusion" and neglect history (p. 429).
- Historical causality is hard to prove, but the lessons of history, especially the twentieth century, are of "inestimable, irreplaceable value" no controlled experiment can equal (pp. 429-430).
- Economists should be pragmatic in method and work with other disciplines; other social scientists should not flee numbers or rest on "every statistic is a social construct", which is true but insufficient (p. 430).
- Furet's career illustrates both why serial history died (aridity, data-processing limits, the anti-Marxist turn of the 1970s) and the loss involved (pp. 430-431).
- Politics and ideas exist independently of economic change, yet prices, wages, incomes and fortunes shape political attitudes, which in turn shape institutions and economic change; an approach that is economic, political, social and cultural at once is "indispensable" (pp. 430-431).
- The 1917–1989 clash of communism and capitalism "sterilized rather than stimulated" research on capital and inequality (p. 431).
- Serial history petered out before reaching the twentieth century, where politics is visibly "ubiquitous" in inequality and capital/income curves; studying that century forces one to study the state, taxes and debt concretely and to abandon infrastructure/superstructure notions (p. 431).
- Not all social science needs statistical series, and data collection is not always imaginative (p. 431); but all citizens should take a serious interest in money, its measurement and history, because those who have a lot of it always defend their interests (pp. 431-432).

## Arguments

**A. The restated core: r > g drives divergence (pp. 426-427)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 426-427) r can exceed g for long periods. (p. 427) When r > g, past wealth grows faster than output and wages. (p. 427) Returns rise with the size of the fortune. (p. 427) Frontier growth will not exceed 1–1.5%; returns are 4–5%.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 427) r > g will "again become the norm in the twenty-first century", with potentially terrifying consequences for wealth concentration.
- **Unstated premises**: that r is roughly insensitive to the accumulation of capital at high β (ch06's elasticity of substitution above one, which the Conclusion does not restate); that the savings out of capital income are high enough for r > g to translate into rising concentration (ch10's model). The Conclusion's note 1 concedes a further one: a higher g might raise r, so the gap need not shrink (p. 510).
- **Force of "contradiction"**: the Conclusion calls r > g "a fundamental logical contradiction" (p. 427), but ch10 explicitly said it was "a historical fact, not a logical necessity" (p. 255). What is logical is the implication from r > g to faster growth of past wealth; whether r > g holds is empirical. The rhetoric of "contradiction" (Marxian in echo) outruns the more careful earlier formulation.

**B. Why a progressive annual capital tax rather than heavy capital-income taxation (p. 427)**

- **Premises**: (p. 427) Indiscriminate heavy taxation of capital income could push r below g but would risk killing accumulation and lowering g. (p. 427) A progressive tax on the stock targets the largest fortunes and spares new accumulators. (p. 428) Very large fortunes have little to do with entrepreneurship and do not promote growth.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 427) The progressive annual capital tax avoids "an endless inegalitarian spiral" while preserving incentives.
- **Assessment**: the claim that huge fortunes are useless for growth (p. 428) is stated as what "Historical experience shows"; the supporting evidence is in ch11 and ch14 (e.g., the absence of a growth penalty from high top rates), not in the Conclusion.

**C. The capital tax requires European democracy (p. 428)**

- **Premises**: (p. 428) The tax needs international cooperation beyond the nation-state. (p. 428) Competition, however perfect, cannot remove r > g. (p. 428) Small European nations retreating to national sovereignty will be powerless against the global economy.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 428) Bet everything on democracy at European scale.
- **Concession**: integration might erode existing social states and build only "a vast market predicated on ever purer and more perfect competition"; "the risk is real" (p. 428).

**D. Economics as political economy (pp. 428-430)**

- **Premises**: (p. 428) What distinguishes economics among the social sciences is its normative purpose. (p. 429) Social scientists cannot avoid taking stands and should not hide behind abstract principles or demolition of others' data. (p. 429) Mathematical models often mask empty content; experimental methods prove causal relations of limited interest and neglect history. (pp. 429-430) History, especially the twentieth century, cannot be replayed and is the principal source of knowledge.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 430) Economists should be methodologically pragmatic, use whatever tools are available, and work with other disciplines.
- **Assessment**: the attack on formal modelling sits awkwardly with the book's own reliance on β = s/g, α = r × β and r > g as organising devices; Piketty's position is better read as a demand that models be subordinate to specified facts and problems ("without a clear specification of the economic facts one is trying to explain", p. 429) than as a rejection of models.

**E. Why serial history died and why it should return (pp. 430-431)**

- **Premises**: (p. 431) Data processing was laborious, leaving little room for interpretation. (p. 430) Furet's turn against Marxist historians led him away from economic and social history. (p. 431) The programme stopped before the twentieth century, where politics visibly shapes the series.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 431) A unified economic, political, social and cultural approach is now possible and indispensable; the old infrastructure/superstructure dichotomy must go.

**Position versus argument.** The Conclusion argues for nothing new; it re-ranks the book's claims. It hardens the descriptive claim (r > g as a "contradiction") while softening the epistemic status of all conclusions ("tenuous", p. 426). The policy claim (progressive capital tax) is stated as the one right solution, while the means (European democracy) are presented as a wager.

## Evidence offered

The Conclusion introduces almost no new evidence; it refers back to the book.

- **Growth and return figures**: frontier growth 1–1.5%, return 4–5%, catching-up economies (postwar Europe, China) able to grow at 4–5% (p. 427). These restate ch02 and ch10.
- **Historical claim**: r > g held "throughout history until the eve of World War I", and two world wars reduced the return (p. 427), restating chs. 3–5 and 10.
- **Tax schedule**: the illustrative rates (p. 427) are ch15's (pp. 380, 390).
- **Normative source**: Article 1 of the 1789 Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen, "common utility" (p. 428), returning to the book's epigraph.
- **Historiography**: Jean Bouvier, François Furet and Marcel Gillet, *Le mouvement du profit en France au 19e siècle* (1965), opening sentence quoted (p. 430); Furet and Jacques Ozouf, *Lire et écrire* (1977) (p. 430); Furet's work on the French Revolution (p. 430). Conclusion note 2: Sartre, Althusser and Badiou treat capital and class inequality as "of only moderate interest" (p. 510).
- **Conclusion note 1** (p. 510): an increase in g could raise r and so not reduce r − g (pointing back to ch10). This is a significant qualification of the claim that growth-promoting policy cannot close the gap: it suggests that even if growth could be raised, it might not help.
- **Methodological claims** about mathematical models and controlled experiments (p. 429) are unsupported by examples; no model or experiment is named.

## Concessions and qualifications

- Sources are "imperfect and incomplete" and conclusions "tenuous" (p. 426).
- Growth can be encouraged through education, knowledge and nonpolluting technologies, though not to 4–5% (p. 427).
- Heavy indiscriminate capital taxation could kill accumulation (p. 427).
- European integration risks undermining existing social states; "the risk is real" (p. 428).
- The nation-state remains the right level for modernising many social and fiscal policies and for new forms of shared ownership (p. 428).
- The aspiration of political economy "often goes unfulfilled" (p. 429).
- Controlled experiments, used with moderation, "can be useful" (p. 429).
- Historical causality "is always difficult to prove beyond a shadow of a doubt" (p. 429).
- Every statistic is a social construct, "which of course is true but insufficient" (p. 430).
- Specialisation is legitimate, and accumulating data is not always indispensable or "especially imaginative" (p. 431).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **"Logical contradiction" vs "historical fact"**: ch10 states r > g is "a historical fact, not a logical necessity" (p. 255) and "a historical reality dependent on a variety of mechanisms" (p. 261). The Conclusion's "fundamental logical contradiction" (p. 427) and "fundamental structural contradiction of capitalism" (p. 427) upgrade it. The Introduction and ch10 labelled it the fundamental force for divergence (pp. 22, 254). A cross-book synthesis should quote ch10's formulation as Piketty's considered view and treat the Conclusion's as rhetoric with a Marxian echo.
- **Certainty and policy confidence**: the Conclusion's epistemic modesty (p. 426) sits beside unqualified claims that r > g "will again become the norm" as "likely" (p. 427) and that the progressive capital tax is "The right solution" (p. 427).
- **Market imperfection**: the claim that r > g is not a market imperfection (p. 428) restates ch11 (p. 309). It is the core of Piketty's disagreement with readings in which better-functioning markets would equalise; it also means the case for the capital tax does not depend on market failure.
- **Note 1's concession** (p. 510): if a higher g raises r, the book's policy space narrows to taxation; this links to ch06's discussion of the elasticity of substitution and the response of r to β.
- **Growth ceiling**: "no matter what economic policies are adopted" (p. 427) is the strongest form of ch02's claim; ch16 notes that climate change may push the other way (p. 424).
- **Method vs practice**: the critique of "immoderate" mathematical modelling (p. 429) applies only partly to the book itself, which uses a small set of accounting identities and a steady-state law (β = s/g) heavily. The link is to the Introduction's statement that the book is "as much a work of history as of economics" (p. 28).
- **Scale of democracy**: the European-democracy wager (p. 428) is the end point of ch15 (global tax as a "utopia") and ch16 (budgetary parliament, pp. 416-417). The nation-state's remaining role (p. 428) echoes ch13's modernised social state.
- **Marikana and Furet**: the Conclusion closes the frame opened in the Introduction (Marikana, p. 31; the 1789 Declaration) and closed in ch16 (Lonmin accounts, p. 426): numbers and transparency as weapons for the least well-off.
- **Cross-book**: the claim that scholars cannot confine themselves to means while citizens choose ends (p. 429) is a direct rejection of the value-free, means-only economics that a Weberian or Hayekian position would defend. Useful for a themes synthesis on economics as science vs political economy.

## Open questions

- If r > g is "a historical fact, not a logical necessity" (p. 255), what would falsify the forecast that it "will again become the norm" (p. 427)? The Conclusion gives no conditions.
- Does note 1 (higher g may raise r, p. 510) cut against or support the claim that growth policies are no solution? It seems to close off growth as a remedy even if growth could be raised, which strengthens the case for taxation but rests on a mechanism the Conclusion does not explain.
- How does Piketty square the critique of mathematical models (p. 429) with the work done by β = s/g and α = r × β? A reader of the critiques should check whether objections target the identities (which are definitional) or the behavioural assumptions behind them (elasticity of substitution, savings behaviour).
- The claim that extreme wealth inequality is of no use to growth (p. 428) is made without data in this chapter; which chapters carry it, and how strong is that evidence?
- Is "bet everything on democracy" (p. 428) a policy claim, a value commitment, or both? The Conclusion presents it as the only alternative without assessing the probability of European political union.
