# ch10: Inequality of Capital Ownership (pp. 244–273)

## Question this chapter answers

Why was wealth so extremely, and increasingly, concentrated in Europe before 1914; why did the shocks of 1914–1945 compress it; why has it not (yet) returned to Belle Époque levels even though the capital/income ratio has; and is the present lower concentration permanent? Piketty's answer: in low-growth societies the return on capital r durably exceeds growth g, and with multiplicative shocks this produces an equilibrium distribution whose inequality rises steeply with r − g; the twentieth century was the only period in which the net return (after taxes and war losses) fell below g; and lower concentration today rests on "accidental events" plus capital taxation, which could be undone (pp. 253-254, 257, 273).

The chapter matters for the reading purpose because it is where the book's central inequality r > g is turned from a slogan (Introduction) into a mechanism with a stated logical status, a calibration, and a list of counterforces.

## Key terms

| Inequality of wealth (capital ownership) | p. 244 | Distribution of net private wealth among living adults, measured by shares of top decile, top centile, middle 40 percent and bottom 50 percent of total private wealth (shares of private rather than national wealth; "little difference", note 6, p. 465). |
| Patrimonial middle class | p. 244 | The middle 40 percent of the wealth hierarchy, "distinctly wealthier" than the bottom half, owning between a quarter and a third of national wealth; its emergence is "no doubt the most important structural transformation" in the long-run wealth distribution (p. 244; defined again as "the middle 40 percent", p. 251). |

| Inheritance society | p. 254 | A society with "both a very high concentration of wealth and a significant persistence of large fortunes from generation to generation" (p. 254). |
| Pure rate of return (pretax) | p. 256 | Return on capital net of any remuneration for the owner's management labour; set at 4.5 percent as a "minimum value" for early periods in Figure 10.9 (p. 256). Distinguished from after-tax-and-loss return (Figure 10.10, p. 257). |
| Net (after-tax, after-loss) return | p. 257 | Pure return minus taxes on capital and estimated war destruction; fell to 1–1.5 percent in 1913–1950, below g "for the first time in history" (p. 257). |
| Rate of time preference θ | p. 259 | Standard-model parameter of impatience; in a zero-growth economy r = θ (note 17, p. 466). Piketty calls the theory "somewhat tautological" and too "simplistic and systematic" (pp. 259-260). |
| Infinite horizon model | p. 260 | Model in which agents plan for all descendants at their own θ; implies infinite long-run elasticity of saving (note 18, p. 466) and r = θ + γ × g (note 19, pp. 466-467). Rejected as unrealistic but used to show r > g holds in a perfect capital market (p. 260). |

## Leading propositions

- The fall in wealth inequality, and of income from it, was "the only reason" total income inequality fell in the first half of the twentieth century; labour-income inequality did not decline structurally in France or the US between 1900–1910 and 1950–1960 (p. 244).
- In all known societies the bottom half owns almost nothing (about 5 percent), the top decile more than 60 and up to 90 percent, the middle 40 percent from 5 to 35 percent (p. 244).
- France is the one country with a homogeneous wealth source from the late eighteenth century, thanks to the universal 1791 estate and gift tax (p. 245).
- Before 1914 there was no trend toward lower wealth inequality in France; concentration rose slowly through the nineteenth century and accelerated in 1880–1913 (p. 246). Top decile 80–85 percent early in the century, nearly 90 percent by 1900–1910; top centile 45–50 percent in 1800–1810, 60 percent in 1900–1910 (p. 247).
- Paris was more extreme still: top centile 55 percent at the start of the century, 70 percent on the eve of 1914, with two-thirds of Parisians dying with nothing to leave (p. 247).
- The Revolution had only small effects: wealth inequality probably fell slightly in 1780–1810, then rose; France was a patrimonial society before and after 1789 (p. 248).
- The twentieth-century fall in the top decile's share went to the middle 40 percent "exclusively"; the bottom half stayed under 5 percent (pp. 248-249).
- Britain and Sweden followed the French path; Britain was slightly more unequal; "the formal nature of the political regime clearly had very little influence on the distribution of wealth" (p. 249). Sweden in 1900–1910 was as concentrated as France and Britain (p. 250).
- Concentration "has never fully recovered" from 1914–1945: in France the top decile went from 90 percent (1910–1920) to 60–70 percent (1950–1970); the top centile from 60 percent to 20–30 percent (pp. 250-251).
- The US began much less unequal (around 1800 not much above Sweden 1970–1980), grew more unequal in the nineteenth century, fell less than Europe after 1910, and is now more unequal: top decile 80 to 70 percent, against Europe's 90 to 60 percent (pp. 252-253).
- The primary reason for pre-1914 hyperconcentration was low growth with r "markedly and durably higher" than g (p. 254).
- With g = 1 percent and r = 5 percent, saving a fifth of capital income keeps an inherited fortune growing with the economy; saving more makes it grow faster even with no labour income (p. 254).
- In France 1820–1913, r was about 5 percent, g about 1 percent; capital income was nearly 40 percent of national income, and saving a quarter of it gave a savings rate of about 10 percent (pp. 254-255).
- r > g is a historical fact, not a logical necessity (p. 255); growth before the seventeenth century never exceeded 0.1–0.2 percent for long, while returns were 4–5 percent, so r was "at least 10 to 20 times" g (p. 255).
- Taxes and shocks are decisive: after taxes and war losses the net return fell to 1–1.5 percent in 1913–1950, below g, and high postwar growth kept r below g in 1950–2012: an unprecedented century "about to end" (p. 257).
- r > g "will probably be true again in the twenty-first century", depending on shocks and policies (p. 258).
- No society seems ever to have had a return that fell "naturally and persistently" below 2–3 percent (p. 259).
- Time preference theory is tautological and its infinite-horizon form predicts infinite saving elasticity, which history contradicts; but the perfect-capital-market logic shows r > g holds in standard models and more so as markets become efficient (pp. 259-260).
- r > g is "the result of a confluence of forces, each largely independent of the others": structurally low g at the frontier (around 1 percent) and r of 4–5 percent from many factors (p. 261).
- Forces that can stop an indefinite spiral: a rising capital/income ratio lowering r (slow, especially with foreign assets), individual demographic and economic shocks, fertility choices, and inheritance law (pp. 261-262).
- Equal rights and the Civil Code did not stop concentration: "equality of rights and opportunities is not enough" (p. 263). With r significantly above g, accumulation and transmission "automatically" concentrate wealth, and equipartition "does not make much of a difference" (p. 263).
- A simple model gives an equilibrium distribution whose inequality rises with r − g; multiplicative shocks give a Pareto distribution whose coefficient rises steeply with r − g (pp. 263-264). With r = 5 and g = 1 the model predicts about 90 percent for the top decile and more than 50 percent for the top centile (p. 264).
- This explains "in a sense the failure of the French Revolution": a 1–2 percent estate tax could not affect r − g (p. 264).
- Primogeniture raised British top-centile concentration (70 against 60 percent), partly offset by France's slow population growth (pp. 264-265).
- According to the model, concentration will not fall much unless g exceeds 1.5–2 percent or taxes bring the net return below 3–3.5 percent (p. 265).
- Pareto's "law" of stable inequality had no evidential basis; Pareto hid a slight upward trend in his own data; saying a distribution is Pareto says nothing without the coefficient (pp. 266-267).
- The fall after 1914 is easy to explain: top fortunes were old and slow to rebuild (pp. 267-268); rentiers kept spending and ate capital (Paris top centile capital income fell from 80–100 to 20 times the average wage), "probably the most important reason" for deconcentration (p. 268); foreign assets and Russian debt (p. 268); postwar nationalisations and the 1945 national solidarity tax (p. 269).
- Belle Époque Parisian portfolios were diversified and "modern"; freer markets or more secure property could not have reduced inequality there (pp. 268-269).
- Non-recovery is the puzzle. "Not enough time" is part of the answer but not enough (pp. 269-270). The main structural cause is twentieth-century taxation of capital: a 30 percent average rate cuts a 5 percent return to 3.5 percent, enough in the model to account for roughly the observed fall in the top centile's share (pp. 270-271).
- Capital-income taxation changes the structure of wealth, not its total: the top centile's loss is offset by middle-class gains (p. 271).
- Progressive estate taxes of 20–30 percent and more after 1914–1945, unlike the 1–2 percent nineteenth-century fee, made it harder to keep one's rank and easier to rise (pp. 271-272). Germany's lower top estate rate (15–20 against 30–40 percent) may explain its higher top capital incomes (p. 272).
- Taxes "suffice to explain most of the observed evolutions" (p. 272), but a lower long-run return and higher growth than before the eighteenth century mean that abolishing capital taxes would not necessarily restore 1900–1910 concentration (p. 272).
- Forces pointing the other way: negative population growth, more "perfect" capital markets that cut returns off from owners' characteristics, and inequality of returns by portfolio size (pp. 272-273).
- Lower concentration today is "largely a consequence of accidental events" and capital taxation; modern growth and markets do not guarantee falling wealth inequality (p. 273).

## Arguments

**A. Wealth deconcentration, not labour income, drove the twentieth-century fall in inequality (p. 244)**

- **Premises**: (p. 244) Labour-income inequality did not fall structurally between 1900–1910 and 1950–1960 in France or the US (from ch08-ch09). (p. 244) Total income inequality fell sharply. (p. 244) The fall came from "the collapse of high incomes from capital".
- **Conclusion**: (p. 244) The fall in capital inequality is "the only reason" total inequality fell; the same holds for other developed countries (note 1, p. 464, cites income-composition data and later series for Germany, Japan and Nordic countries).
- **Comment**: the decomposition rests on Part Two's income-composition series; here it is restated, not demonstrated.

**B. The core mechanism: r > g in a low-growth world (pp. 254-255)**

- **Premises**: (p. 254) In low-growth societies (g about 0.5–1 percent) the return is about 4–5 percent. (p. 254) With g = 1 percent and r = 5 percent, a rentier who saves one-fifth of capital income keeps pace with the economy; saving more makes the fortune grow faster than income, without any labour income. (pp. 254-255) In France 1820–1913, r about 5 percent, g about 1 percent, capital share nearly 40 percent, saving a quarter of capital income giving about 10 percent of national income.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 254) "For strictly mathematical reasons" conditions are "ideal" for an inheritance society; (p. 255) in France wealth grew slightly faster than income, so concentration trended up.
- **Unstated premises**: that large fortunes earn at least the average return (later relaxed upward in ch12); that the wealthy save a large share of capital income (asserted from Parisian behaviour, p. 265); that r is roughly invariant to accumulation over the relevant horizon. The arithmetic is exact; the empirical inputs (r, savings behaviour of the rich) are the load-bearing part.

**C. Why r > g: historical fact, not logic (pp. 255-261)**

- **Premises**: (p. 255) Growth from antiquity to the seventeenth century never exceeded 0.1–0.2 percent for long. (p. 255) Returns were 4–5 percent, and even a low estimate of 2–3 percent after paying the owner's management labour is far above g. (p. 259) No society has had returns fall "naturally and persistently" below 2–3 percent; land and today's real estate earn about 4–5 percent, perhaps trending to 3–4 percent. (p. 260) In a perfect capital market, r < g would make agents "feel infinitely wealthy" and borrow without limit until r rose above g. (p. 261) g is structurally low (about 1 percent) at the frontier once the demographic transition is done; r depends on many technological, psychological, social and cultural factors that together give about 4–5 percent.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 261) r > g is "a historical reality dependent on a variety of mechanisms" and "not as an absolute logical necessity".
- **Comment**: Piketty uses two kinds of support at once. One is purely historical (the long-run record). The other is theoretical (the standard model's transversality-type argument, p. 260). He rejects the theory as a description (infinite elasticity, r rising too fast with g, note 19) while accepting its sign prediction. That is consistent but means the "contingent" label applies to the *size* of r − g more than to its sign in market economies.

**D. Twentieth-century exception: net r < g (pp. 256-258)**

- **Premises**: (p. 256) Before 1914 capital taxes were negligible, so pretax return ≈ net return. (pp. 256-257) After 1914 top-income, profit and wealth taxes rose sharply; since the 1980s they have fallen under fiscal competition. (p. 257) Assumed average capital tax: 30 percent for 1913–2012, 10 percent for 2012–2050, 0 for 2050–2100, plus estimated war destruction in 1913–1950. (p. 256) Global growth 3.5–4 percent in the second half of the twentieth century; about 1.5 percent in 2050–2100 in the central scenario.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 257) Net return was 1–1.5 percent in 1913–1950, below g; high growth kept it below in 1950–2012; "for the first time in history" net r < g, and this "is about to end".
- **Status**: the post-2012 part of Figures 10.10–10.11 is an explicit assumption made "For the sake of argument" (p. 257), and Piketty himself calls the no-political-reaction premise "dubious and to my mind not very plausible" (p. 258). These figures illustrate a scenario; they are not forecasts.

**E. From r − g to the shape of the distribution (pp. 261-265)**

- **Premises**: (p. 261) If r > g persistently, only a falling r (from a rising capital/income ratio), individual shocks, fertility and inheritance law can stop an indefinite spiral. (pp. 261-262) Demographic and economic shocks always reshuffle family fortunes. (pp. 263-264) With a given structure of shocks the distribution converges to an equilibrium; with multiplicative shocks it is Pareto, with coefficient steeply increasing in r − g (formula only in the online appendix, note 25, p. 467).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 264) With r = 5 and g = 1 the model predicts about 90 percent top decile and more than 50 percent top centile, matching nineteenth-century France; so r > g "can explain" the observed concentration and the "failure of the French Revolution".
- **Qualification in the note**: note 26 concedes the model and calculations "do not claim to identify the precise role played by each mechanism" (p. 467) and claims only that r > g "is by itself sufficient to explain the observed level of concentration" (p. 467). This is a sufficiency claim, much weaker than the text's "automatically lead to a very highly concentrated distribution" (p. 263).
- **Unstated premise**: that the variance and form of the shocks are stable across countries and periods, so that differences in concentration can be read as differences in r − g. Primogeniture (p. 264) and population growth (p. 265) are allowed to shift the result, which shows the shock structure is not in fact fixed.

**F. Institutions and inheritance law matter less than r − g (pp. 262-265)**

- **Premises**: (p. 262) The Civil Code abolished entails and imposed equipartition; (p. 263) revolutionaries such as Condorcet expected fortunes to equalise naturally. (pp. 246-247) Concentration rose anyway through the nineteenth century. (p. 249) Republican France was barely less unequal than monarchical Britain. (p. 265) Paris under the Civil Code reached a 70 percent top-centile share by 1913, above Britain.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 263) Equal rights and opportunities are not enough; (p. 264) "The formal nature of the regime was of little moment compared with the inequality r > g."
- **Comment**: this is a comparative-history argument with one main contrast (France v Britain), plus Sweden. It shows that legal equality was not *sufficient*; it does not show that r − g is the only cause.

**G. Why concentration fell in 1914–1945 (pp. 267-269)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 267-268) The largest fortunes were accumulated long ago and take longest to rebuild. (p. 268) Parisian probate records show rentiers did not cut spending: the top Parisian centile's bequeathed capital financed 80–100 times the average wage in 1872–1912, 30–40 times in the interwar years, 20 times by the late 1930s. (p. 268) Foreign assets, up to a quarter of the largest fortunes and nearly half of that foreign sovereign debt, mostly vanished. (p. 269) Nationalisations and the 1945 solidarity tax hit large industrial fortunes.
- **Conclusion**: Dissaving by rentiers was "probably the most important reason" (p. 268) for European deconcentration; the descending part of the curve "is not the most difficult part to explain" (p. 269).

**H. Why concentration has not returned (pp. 269-272)**

- **Premises**: (p. 269) Capital accumulation takes generations; private wealth regained its 1914 level only in 2000–2010. (p. 270) Concentration has risen since 1970–1980. (p. 270) But the top centile fell from 60–70 to 20–30 percent, which looks structural. (pp. 270-271) Capital income went from near-zero tax before 1914 to about 30 percent in 1950–1980; 5 percent becomes 3.5 percent net. (p. 271) The model shows a 30 percent effective rate on all capital can by itself produce a fall roughly equal to the observed fall in the top centile's share (note 36, p. 469: technical appendix only). (pp. 271-272) Progressive estate taxes of 20–30 percent or more made keeping rank harder.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 272) Taxes "suffice to explain most of the observed evolutions"; (p. 272) but other factors (slightly lower return, higher growth) mean even zero capital tax need not restore 1900–1910 levels.

**Status of claims: logic, evidence, conjecture (Piketty's own labels)**

- *Logic/arithmetic*: the one-fifth savings rule (p. 254); that a higher r − g produces a more unequal equilibrium in the model (pp. 263-264); that a tax cutting r from 5 to 3.5 percent compounds over generations (p. 271).
- *Historical evidence*: the French, British, Swedish and US series (pp. 246-253); r > g before 1914 (pp. 254-255); Parisian rentier dissaving and portfolios (pp. 268-269).
- *Model calibration*: the 90/50 prediction (p. 264), the 1.5–2 percent and 3–3.5 percent thresholds (p. 265), the tax counterfactuals (pp. 271-272). All rest on a model whose equations are in the online appendix only (notes 25, 36).
- *Conjecture/scenario*: twenty-first-century r and g (pp. 256-258, 272-273); Germany's estate tax as cause ("Perhaps", p. 272); how high Paris would have gone without the war (pp. 247, 266).
- *Admitted ignorance*: no evidence to set the precise share of each mechanism (p. 254); "no definitive and totally satisfactory answer" on non-recovery (p. 267).

## Evidence offered

**Data and sources (provenance)**

- **French estate and gift tax records, 1791–present** (pp. 245-246): proportional low-rate tax 1791–1901; government statistics on annual flows from the 1820s; tabulations by estate size from 1901, increasingly cross-tabulated until the 1950s; digital representative samples after 1970 (p. 246).
- **Individual declarations** collected with Postel-Vinay and Rosenthal from national and departmental archives, samples for each decade 1800–1810 to 2000–2010 (p. 246); note 4 (p. 465) adds the Enquête TRA project (Bourdieu, Kesztenbaum, Suwa-Eisenmann) and Piketty's 2010/2011 inheritance paper. Figure 10.1 updates Piketty, Postel-Vinay and Rosenthal, *American Economic Review* 2006 (note 5, p. 465), averaged by decade.
- **Mortality-multiplier reweighting** of wealth at death to the living (p. 247), with checks against Nordic wealth taxes and the French wealth tax of 1990–2010 (note 7, p. 465).
- **Britain**: probate data from 1910–1920 (Atkinson and Harrison); Peter Linder's estate-inventory estimates for 1810–1870, "more robust but less homogeneous" (p. 249). Homogeneous probate statistics only from 1919–1920 (p. 246).
- **Sweden**: data from 1910 (Ohlsson, Roine, Waldenström); Lee Soltow for 1810–1870 (p. 250).
- **United States**: federal estate tax from 1916, covering a small share of the population (pp. 246, 251); Lampman, Kopczuk and Saez; Federal Reserve wealth surveys since the 1960s (Kennickell, Wolff); Alice Hanson Jones and Lee Soltow for 1810–1870, "less robust" (pp. 251-252). Note 12 (p. 466): the 1800 estimates include unfree people; focusing on free people only gives a top centile under 15 percent. Note 14 (p. 466): Fed-survey levels "may be somewhat low"; top centile may be 40 not 35 percent.
- **Pre-modern societies**: orders of magnitude only, "not sufficiently robust" for comparisons; note 10 (pp. 465-466) cites Bagnall on landholding in late Roman Egypt (p. 250).
- **Aristocratic names** in the top Parisian centile: about 15 percent (1800–1810), nearly 30 percent (1840–1850), under 10 percent (1890–1900) (p. 248); probably over 50 percent before 1789 (note 8, p. 465).
- **Return and growth series**: France 1820–1913 (Figures 10.7–10.8, pp. 254-255); world antiquity–2100 and –2200 (Figures 10.9–10.11, pp. 256-258), built from Part One growth estimates and Part Two British and French returns; 4.5 percent assumed as a minimum early return, with historical data suggesting 5–6 percent (p. 256; note 16, p. 466, citing Homer and Sylla on interest rates of 6–8 percent).
- **Parisian portfolios** (Table 10.1, pp. 268-269): about a third real estate, almost two-thirds financial assets on the eve of 1914; foreign assets up to a quarter of the largest fortunes (p. 268); more detail in online Table S10.4 (note 34, p. 468).
- **Rentier lifestyle ratios**: top Parisian centile capital income 80–100 times the average wage (p. 268), equal to 2–2.5 million euros a year against a 24,000-euro average wage (note 33, p. 468).
- **1945 solidarity tax**: up to 20 percent on wealth and up to 100 percent on nominal gains 1940–1945 (note 35, pp. 468-469).
- **Estate-tax rates**: 1–2 percent in the nineteenth century; at most 5 percent on direct-line bequests after 1901, affecting a few dozen estates a year (p. 271); Germany 15–20 against France 30–40 percent top rate (p. 272; Fabien Dell’s simulations, note 38, p. 469).

**Models**

- The equilibrium-distribution/Pareto model (pp. 263-264) and the tax simulations (pp. 271-272): equations and simulations are only in the online technical appendix and Piketty's *Les hauts revenus en France* (notes 25-26, p. 467; notes 36-37, p. 469). The book itself gives no formula, so the 90/50 prediction and the thresholds on p. 265 cannot be checked from the text.

**Literary and illustrative material**

- Balzac (*Père Goriot*: Goriot's pauper's death, pp. 248-249), Austen (*Sense and Sensibility*, *Persuasion* on entails, p. 262), Condorcet's 1794 prediction (p. 263), Jefferson (pp. 262-263), Disney's *The Aristocats* (p. 265; note 29, pp. 467-468). These illustrate; they do not carry evidential weight.

**Weak points in the evidence**

- Only four countries have long series (pp. 244-245), and only France has a homogeneous source before 1910 (p. 246). The European-wide nineteenth-century claim rests on France plus imperfect British (Linder) and Swedish (Soltow) samples (pp. 249-250). The "European average" in Figure 10.6 is France, Britain and Sweden (note 15, p. 466).
- The return series is "approximate and uncertain" (p. 256); for early periods the 4.5 percent is an assumed value, not a measured one.
- The Pareto attack (p. 266) rests on Piketty's own earlier reanalysis (note 30, p. 468), so the charge that Pareto "intentionally sought to hide" a trend is sourced to Piketty himself.
- Text slip: p. 256 says the pretax returns "in Figure 10.8" exclude war losses; context (Figures 10.9–10.10) suggests Figure 10.9 is meant.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch05-ch06**: the counterforce that a rising capital/income ratio "should lead to a decrease in the rate of return" (p. 261) is the elasticity-of-substitution question of ch06; if the elasticity exceeds one, as ch06 argues, the counterforce is weak and slow, which is what this chapter needs. The β recovery to the 1914 level only in 2000–2010 (p. 269) is ch05's result.
- **Depends on ch07-ch09**: the claim that labour-income inequality did not fall structurally (p. 244) and the 6–7 percent top-centile wage share come from ch08-ch09; the patrimonial middle class and the standard distribution shares come from ch07 (Table 7.2, note 2, p. 464).
- **Feeds ch11**: the inheritance society defined here (p. 254) is measured in ch11 (inheritance flow, share of inherited wealth). Ch10 promises that ch11 will show "most wealth in this period did come from inheritance" (p. 255).
- **Feeds ch12**: inequality of returns by portfolio size is flagged as an extra divergence force (p. 273); it undercuts the equal-return assumption in Argument B.
- **Feeds ch14-ch15**: the tax counterfactuals (pp. 270-272) are the empirical basis for the global capital tax. The claim that a capital-income tax changes structure but not the total capital stock (p. 271) is a key premise for the policy argument and is asserted from the model plus a reading of twentieth-century history.
- **Internal inconsistency (my observation)**: on p. 254 saving one-fifth of capital income makes an inherited fortune grow "at the same rate as the economy"; on p. 261 the same one-fifth makes wealthy fortunes "grow faster than average income". With r = 5 and g = 1, one-fifth gives exactly g; "faster" needs more than a fifth.
- **Tension on causal weight (my observation)**: for the 1914–1945 fall, dissaving by rentiers is "probably the most important reason" (p. 268); for non-recovery, taxation is "the most natural and important explanation" (p. 270) and "suffice to explain most" (p. 272); yet the chapter opens by saying the shares of mechanisms cannot be determined (p. 254) and closes by saying it would be "going too far" to rule out other factors (p. 272). The hedges and the confident statements sit side by side.
- **Tension on the status of r > g (my observation)**: labelled contingent and historical (pp. 255, 258), yet also derived from the standard perfect-market model and said to become more likely as markets become efficient (p. 260). The contingency is really about *net* r after taxes and shocks; the pretax inequality is treated as near-universal (pp. 255, 259).
- **"Accidental" versus policy (my observation)**: p. 273 calls the compression "largely a consequence of accidental events" and "specific institutions". But p. 257 says the progressive taxes were "made possible by the shocks of 1914–1945". So the institutions themselves depend on the accidents, which bears on how far ch15's tax can be had without a comparable shock.
- **Political-regime claim**: "formal nature of the political regime clearly had very little influence" (p. 249) is about the nineteenth century; in the twentieth century political choices (taxes) are said to be decisive (pp. 270-272). Not a contradiction, but the point is that institutions matter only when they bite on r − g.

## Open questions

- The Pareto/r − g model is never written down in the book (note 25, p. 467). Does the online appendix specify the shock process, savings rule and death process? Without it, the 90/50 prediction (p. 264) and the thresholds (p. 265) cannot be assessed; synthesis should treat them as reported model outputs.
- Is the sufficiency claim of note 26 (p. 467) also enough to rule out rivals (e.g. returns rising with wealth, closed elite marriage markets, political rents)? The chapter does not test alternatives.
- How reliable is the US 1800 baseline, given that including enslaved people changes the top centile share so much (note 12, p. 466)? This affects the narrative that the New World was less unequal (p. 252).
- The pretax return series for early periods is assumed (4.5 percent, p. 256). How sensitive is the claim that r was 10 to 20 times g (p. 255) to measurement of returns on land net of management labour and risk?
- Does the claim that capital taxes leave the total capital stock unchanged (p. 271) survive a check against ch05's β series after 1950? Ch05 attributes β's low mid-century level partly to policy; here policy is said not to reduce the total.
- Germany: is the estate-tax explanation (p. 272) more than a conjecture? Dell's thesis (note 38) should be checked if this is used in synthesis.
- The 1945 solidarity tax is called "extremely high" (p. 269); note 35 gives up to 20 percent on wealth and 100 percent on nominal gains. How much did it actually contribute relative to the war shocks?
- How do Piketty's 21st-century r and g assumptions (pp. 256-258, 272) square with ch02's growth projections and ch06's return trends? Check against those notes in synthesis.
