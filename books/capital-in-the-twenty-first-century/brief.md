## 30-second brief

- **Problem:** does capitalism, left to itself, make wealth steadily more concentrated (Marx), or does growth gradually reduce inequality (Kuznets)?
- **Thesis:** when the return on capital (r) stays above the growth rate (g), inherited wealth grows faster than output and wages, and wealth concentrates. That was the normal state of affairs before 1914. The equal mid-twentieth century was an exception, produced by war and policy. Slow growth is now bringing the old pattern back. The remedy Piketty proposes is a progressive global tax on capital.
- **Who benefits:** anyone arguing about inequality, taxation or wealth, who needs the data and the accounting behind the slogans.
- **Worth reading?** Selectively. About half the book carries the argument. The rest is supporting data series, literary illustration and policy detail.

## 3-minute mental model

### Core ideas, ranked

1. **r > g is the main force for divergence.** When the return on capital exceeds growth, past wealth outgrows the economy. Piketty treats this as a historical fact, not a law of logic. [Introduction, line 1006](./source/source.txt#L1006) [Ch 10, line 10644](./source/source.txt#L10644) [Ch 10, line 10697](./source/source.txt#L10697)
2. **Slow growth makes capital large.** The second fundamental law, β = s / g, says the long-run capital/income ratio equals the saving rate divided by the growth rate. As growth falls, especially population growth, β rises back toward nineteenth-century levels. [Ch 5, line 5110](./source/source.txt#L5110) [Ch 5, line 5129](./source/source.txt#L5129)
3. **Larger capital means a larger capital share.** α = r × β is an identity. The return on capital falls only slowly as capital grows, because capital and labor substitute easily (elasticity above one). So the capital share rises with β. [Ch 1, line 1796](./source/source.txt#L1796) [Ch 6, line 6614](./source/source.txt#L6614) [Ch 6, line 6769](./source/source.txt#L6769)
4. **Twentieth-century equality came from shocks and policy, not markets.** Wars, depression, inflation, rent control, nationalization and progressive taxes destroyed the rentiers. Kuznets read a temporary episode as a law. [Introduction, line 838](./source/source.txt#L838) [Ch 4, line 4602](./source/source.txt#L4602) [Ch 8, line 8298](./source/source.txt#L8298)
5. **Wealth is always far more unequal than labor income.** The top tenth of earners take 25–35% of labor income. The top tenth of owners hold 50–90% of wealth. The rise of a “patrimonial middle class” was the great structural change of the twentieth century, and it is fragile. [Ch 7, line 7428](./source/source.txt#L7428) [Ch 7, line 7939](./source/source.txt#L7939)
6. **Recent US inequality is driven by top pay, set by institutions rather than productivity.** “Supermanagers” largely set their own pay, and lower top tax rates made it worth bargaining for. [Ch 8, line 8882](./source/source.txt#L8882) [Ch 8, line 9198](./source/source.txt#L9198) [Ch 9, line 10035](./source/source.txt#L10035) [Ch 14, line 15375](./source/source.txt#L15375)
7. **Inheritance is coming back.** In France the yearly flow of bequests and gifts fell from 20–25% of national income to under 5% after 1945, and has climbed back to about 15%. [Ch 11, line 11498](./source/source.txt#L11498) [Ch 11, line 12144](./source/source.txt#L12144)
8. **Bigger fortunes earn higher returns.** Economies of scale in portfolio management amplify r > g at the top. The evidence is billionaire rankings and US university endowments. [Ch 12, line 13019](./source/source.txt#L13019) [Ch 12, line 13537](./source/source.txt#L13537)
9. **The remedy is a progressive global tax on capital, plus transparency.** An annual tax on net wealth, backed by automatic exchange of banking information. Its purpose is to regulate capitalism more than to raise revenue. Top income-tax rates above 80% and a one-off capital levy on public debt complete the package. [Ch 15, line 15599](./source/source.txt#L15599) [Ch 15, line 15645](./source/source.txt#L15645) [Ch 14, line 15471](./source/source.txt#L15471) [Ch 16, line 16400](./source/source.txt#L16400)

### Three things to remember

- The famous claim is conditional. r > g is “a contingent historical proposition,” and Piketty does not predict the future of capital. He says divergence is one possible path among several. [Ch 10, line 10834](./source/source.txt#L10834) [Introduction, line 1049](./source/source.txt#L1049)
- Piketty insists that r > g has nothing to do with market imperfection. A more perfect capital market makes it more likely, so more competition is not the cure. [Introduction, line 1052](./source/source.txt#L1052) [Ch 11, line 12812](./source/source.txt#L12812)
- The two “laws” differ in kind. α = r × β is an identity that holds at every moment. β = s / g holds only in the long run and only on assumptions. The book's forecasts rest on the second. [Ch 5, line 5202](./source/source.txt#L5202)

### What it changes in the reader's thinking

**[Analysis]** Before this book, most economists treated the split of income between capital and labor as roughly fixed and inequality as a matter of skills and wages. Piketty moves attention to the stock of wealth: how large it is relative to income, who owns it, and whether it was inherited. The reader learns to see the egalitarian postwar decades as a product of destruction and policy rather than the natural endpoint of growth. A small gap between two percentages, sustained for decades, then looks like the central fact of distribution. The book also shifts the question from how much inequality there is to whether it can be justified, and by what institutions.

## 10-minute deep brief

### 1. r > g: the fundamental force for divergence

- **Author's claim:** with a return on capital of about 4–5% against growth of 0.1–1%, a rentier needs to reinvest only a fifth of capital income for inherited wealth to keep pace with the economy. Saving more concentrates wealth further. This is why wealth was so concentrated before 1914. [Ch 10, line 10644](./source/source.txt#L10644) [Ch 10, line 10651](./source/source.txt#L10651)
- **Reasoning:** for most of history g was close to zero, while r was rarely below 2–3% before tax. Under random shocks to fortunes, the equilibrium wealth distribution has a Pareto tail whose inequality rises steeply with r − g. [Ch 10, line 10697](./source/source.txt#L10697) [Ch 10, line 11031](./source/source.txt#L11031)
- **Evidence:** French probate records from 1791 onward show no fall in concentration before 1914. Paris's top centile rose to over 70% of wealth by 1913. [Ch 10, line 10322](./source/source.txt#L10322) [Ch 10, line 11080](./source/source.txt#L11080) In the twentieth century, for the first time, the return net of taxes and losses fell below growth. [Ch 10, line 10776](./source/source.txt#L10776)
- **Boundary:** Piketty concedes no evidence assigns precise weights to each mechanism. His world r and g series are “approximate and uncertain,” and the model linking r − g to wealth shares sits in the notes. [Ch 10, line 10638](./source/source.txt#L10638) [Ch 10, line 10734](./source/source.txt#L10734) **[Analysis]** The argument uses a single economy-wide r, although the same chapter shows rentiers consuming their capital and returns differing by fortune size.
- **Application [Application]:** when judging whether wealth will concentrate, compare the after-tax return the wealthy actually get with the growth rate of incomes, not the headline return.

> “I take this to be a historical fact, not a logical necessity.” [Ch 10, line 10698](./source/source.txt#L10698)

> “The inequality r > g implies that wealth accumulated in the past grows more rapidly than output and wages.” [Conclusion, line 17414](./source/source.txt#L17414)

### 2. The second law: slow growth makes capital large

- **Author's claim:** in the long run β = s / g. Saving 12% with 2% growth gives capital worth six years of income. At 1.5% growth it is eight years. [Ch 5, line 5110](./source/source.txt#L5110) [Ch 5, line 5137](./source/source.txt#L5137)
- **Reasoning:** g counts population growth as well as growth per head. Europe and the US had similar growth per head, but Europe's slower population growth gives it the higher ratio. [Ch 5, line 5153](./source/source.txt#L5153)
- **Evidence:** in Britain and France capital stood at 6–7 years of income through the nineteenth century, fell to 2–3 years by 1950 and was back at 5–6 by 2010. [Ch 3, line 3637](./source/source.txt#L3637) From 1970 to 2010 private wealth in the eight richest economies rose from 2–3.5 to 4–7 years of income. Saving flows predict most of that rise, and Japan's almost exactly. [Ch 5, line 5332](./source/source.txt#L5332) [Ch 5, line 5402](./source/source.txt#L5402) [Ch 5, line 5418](./source/source.txt#L5418)
- **Boundary:** the law takes decades, applies only to capital that can be accumulated, and assumes asset prices track consumer prices on average. [Ch 5, line 5181](./source/source.txt#L5181) [Ch 5, line 5214](./source/source.txt#L5214) Rising asset prices explain a quarter to a third of the 1970–2010 rise, and privatization a further tenth to a quarter. [Ch 5, line 5852](./source/source.txt#L5852) [Ch 5, line 5640](./source/source.txt#L5640) **[Analysis]** The projection of a world ratio near 700% by 2100 assumes saving holds at 10% while growth halves. If saving falls with growth, β rises less. [Ch 5, line 5981](./source/source.txt#L5981)
- **Application [Application]:** treat β = s / g as a sanity check on claims about wealth. Low growth and steady saving mean a large stock of wealth relative to income.

> “in a quasi-stagnant society, wealth accumulated in the past will inevitably acquire disproportionate importance.” [Ch 5, line 5129](./source/source.txt#L5129)

### 3. α = r × β and the elasticity of substitution

- **Author's claim:** capital's share of income equals the return times the capital/income ratio. This is “a pure accounting identity.” [Ch 1, line 1796](./source/source.txt#L1796) Whether a larger β raises α depends on how fast r falls as capital accumulates. [Ch 6, line 6614](./source/source.txt#L6614)
- **Reasoning:** if capital substitutes easily for labor (elasticity above one), r falls less than β rises, and α grows. Piketty puts the historical elasticity at 1.3–1.6 and concludes that no self-correcting mechanism stops β and α rising together. [Ch 6, line 6769](./source/source.txt#L6769) [Ch 6, line 6804](./source/source.txt#L6804)
- **Evidence:** in Britain and France the capital share was 35–40% in the nineteenth century, 20–25% in the mid-twentieth and 25–30% now. The “pure” return oscillated around 4–5% with no long-run trend. [Ch 6, line 6112](./source/source.txt#L6112) [Ch 6, line 6280](./source/source.txt#L6280) Across rich countries the capital share rose from 15–25% in 1970 to 25–30% in 2000–2010. [Ch 6, line 6807](./source/source.txt#L6807)
- **Boundary:** Piketty admits the elasticity estimate is uncertain. The same data fit a rise in capital's bargaining power. [Ch 6, line 6786](./source/source.txt#L6786) [Ch 6, line 6798](./source/source.txt#L6798) **[Analysis]** β is measured at market value, so asset-price booms raise β and lower measured r without any change in physical capital. The elasticity argument treats β as a volume.
- **Application [Application]:** when someone says labor's share is naturally stable, ask what is happening to the capital stock and how quickly returns are falling.

### 4. Equality in the twentieth century was produced by shocks and policy

- **Author's claim:** the fall in inequality from 1910 to 1950 came from war and wartime policy. The rise after 1980 came largely from political changes in taxation and finance. [Introduction, line 838](./source/source.txt#L838) The Kuznets curve was formulated “in large part for the wrong reasons,” and was a product of the Cold War. [Introduction, line 654](./source/source.txt#L654) [Introduction, line 641](./source/source.txt#L641)
- **Reasoning:** physical destruction explains only a fifth to a third of Europe's capital loss in 1913–1950. The rest came from lost foreign assets, low saving, and asset prices held down by rent control and regulation. [Ch 4, line 4531](./source/source.txt#L4531) [Ch 4, line 4559](./source/source.txt#L4559)
- **Evidence:** in France the top centile's income share fell from over 20% to 8–9%. The whole compression came from lower capital incomes at the top, while the wage hierarchy stayed stable. [Ch 8, line 8319](./source/source.txt#L8319) [Ch 8, line 8298](./source/source.txt#L8298)
- **Boundary:** French data before 1914 come from estates, not income. In the twentieth century, managers did not overtake rentiers; rentiers fell behind them. [Ch 8, line 8381](./source/source.txt#L8381) [Ch 8, line 8449](./source/source.txt#L8449)
- **Application [Application]:** read claims that growth naturally reduces inequality against what was destroyed or taxed in the same period.

> “managers did not race ahead of rentiers” [Ch 8, line 8451](./source/source.txt#L8451)

### 5. Capital is always more unequal than labor

- **Author's claim:** in every country and period with data, the top 10% of earners take 25–30% of labor income, while the top 10% of wealth holders own over 50%, sometimes 90%. The bottom half owns under 10% of wealth, usually under 5%. [Ch 7, line 7428](./source/source.txt#L7428)
- **Reasoning:** neither precautionary nor life-cycle saving can explain this, because wealth is almost as concentrated within each age group as across the whole population. Inheritance and the compounding of returns explain it. [Ch 7, line 7467](./source/source.txt#L7467) [Ch 7, line 7494](./source/source.txt#L7494)
- **Evidence:** in France in 2010–2011 the top 10% held 62% of wealth and the bottom half 4%. In 1900–1910 the top 10% held about 90%, and the middle 40% only 5–10%. [Ch 7, line 7793](./source/source.txt#L7793) [Ch 7, line 7915](./source/source.txt#L7915)
- **Boundary:** the figures are rounded on purpose, and the class labels are explicitly arbitrary. Piketty also argues that Gini coefficients and P90/P10 ratios hide the top of the distribution. [Ch 7, line 7515](./source/source.txt#L7515) [Ch 7, line 8139](./source/source.txt#L8139) **[Analysis]** The claim about within-age concentration is supported only by an endnote.
- **Application [Application]:** ask any inequality statistic whether it covers wealth or income, and whether it shows the top 1%.

> “intergenerational warfare has not replaced class warfare.” [Ch 7, line 7496](./source/source.txt#L7496)

### 6. Supermanagers: US inequality since 1980

- **Author's claim:** of the 15-point rise in the US top decile's income share, about 11 points went to the top 1%. Between 1977 and 2007 the top 10% took three-quarters of growth. [Ch 8, line 9019](./source/source.txt#L9019) [Ch 8, line 9058](./source/source.txt#L9058) Wages explain two-thirds of the rise, and 60–70% of the top 0.1% are top managers. [Ch 8, line 9110](./source/source.txt#L9110) [Ch 8, line 9198](./source/source.txt#L9198)
- **Reasoning:** the race between education and technology explains wages in the long run but not this surge. The surge starts sharply at the top 1%, and countries with the same technology saw much smaller rises. For unique top jobs “individual marginal productivity” can barely be defined, so pay is set by committees and social norms. [Ch 9, line 9249](./source/source.txt#L9249) [Ch 9, line 9598](./source/source.txt#L9598) [Ch 9, line 10044](./source/source.txt#L10044)
- **Evidence:** the top 0.1% share rose from 2% to nearly 10% in the US, against roughly 1.5% to 2.5% in France and Japan. [Ch 9, line 9697](./source/source.txt#L9697) Executive pay responds more to luck than to performance, especially where top tax rates are low. [Ch 14, line 15442](./source/source.txt#L15442) Countries that cut top rates most saw the largest rise in top shares, with no extra productivity growth. [Ch 14, line 15358](./source/source.txt#L15358) [Ch 14, line 15391](./source/source.txt#L15391)
- **Boundary:** the case for norms and bargaining is argued mainly by ruling out alternatives. Piketty concedes that norms “only shifts the difficulty” elsewhere. [Ch 9, line 10119](./source/source.txt#L10119) **[Analysis]** Counting stock options and founder-owners' pay as wages moves some capital-like returns into labor income.
- **Application [Application]:** when judging an executive pay package, look at who sets it and at the top marginal tax rate, as well as at company performance.

### 7. The return of inheritance

- **Author's claim:** when r is well above g for a long time, inheritance comes to dominate saving. [Ch 11, line 11442](./source/source.txt#L11442) In France the yearly inheritance flow went from 20–25% of national income in the nineteenth century to 4–5% in the 1950s and about 15% in 2010. [Ch 11, line 11498](./source/source.txt#L11498)
- **Reasoning:** the flow equals μ × m × β: the wealth of the dying relative to the living, times the death rate, times the capital/income ratio. Longer lives do not end inheritance, because wealth ages with its owners. [Ch 11, line 11606](./source/source.txt#L11606) [Ch 11, line 11779](./source/source.txt#L11779)
- **Evidence:** inherited wealth was 80–90% of private wealth in the nineteenth century, about 40% at its low in the 1970s and about two-thirds in 2010. [Ch 11, line 12144](./source/source.txt#L12144) For cohorts born from the 1970s on, top inheritors and top earners are roughly level again, a reversal of the twentieth-century pattern (Rastignac's dilemma). [Ch 11, line 12365](./source/source.txt#L12365)
- **Boundary:** the evidence is strongest for France. The data for Britain, Germany and the US are weaker, and the projections are “highly uncertain”. [Ch 11, line 12856](./source/source.txt#L12856) [Ch 11, line 12059](./source/source.txt#L12059)
- **Application [Application]:** in debates about inheritance tax, separate the size of the yearly flow from the share of wealth that was inherited.

> “Rent is not an imperfection in the market” [Ch 11, line 12820](./source/source.txt#L12820)

### 8. Returns rise with the size of a fortune

- **Author's claim:** large fortunes earn higher average returns, through economies of scale and a greater capacity to bear risk. That amplifies r > g at the top even when it is modest overall. [Ch 12, line 13019](./source/source.txt#L13019) [Ch 12, line 13038](./source/source.txt#L13038)
- **Evidence:** from 1987 to 2013 the largest fortunes grew 6.4–6.8% a year in real terms, against 2.1% for average wealth. [Ch 12, line 13117](./source/source.txt#L13117) Returns on US university endowments for 1980–2010 rise with size: 6.2% for the smallest, 8.8% for those over $1 billion, 10.2% for Harvard, Yale and Princeton. [Ch 12, line 13537](./source/source.txt#L13537)
- **Boundary:** Piketty says the Forbes data have serious biases. The endowment results may partly reflect a rebound in asset prices, and extending them to families is an analogy. [Ch 12, line 13076](./source/source.txt#L13076) [Ch 12, line 13613](./source/source.txt#L13613)
- **Application [Application]:** treat average-return figures as a floor for the very wealthy and a ceiling for small savers.

> “Every fortune is partially justified yet potentially excessive.” [Ch 12, line 13417](./source/source.txt#L13417)

### 9. The remedy: a progressive global tax on capital

- **Author's claim:** Piketty proposes a progressive annual tax on individual net wealth, covering all assets with no exceptions. An illustrative schedule is 0% below €1 million, 1% between €1 and 5 million, and 2% above €5 million. [Ch 15, line 15599](./source/source.txt#L15599) [Ch 15, line 15607](./source/source.txt#L15607) Its main justification is ability to pay: for the very rich, declared income can be a tiny fraction of economic income. [Ch 15, line 15870](./source/source.txt#L15870)
- **Reasoning:** even a low rate would force reporting and so create transparency about wealth, which makes financial regulation possible. That requires automatic exchange of banking information, enforced by sanctions on countries that refuse. [Ch 15, line 15654](./source/source.txt#L15654) [Ch 15, line 15751](./source/source.txt#L15751) Rates of about 5% on the largest fortunes would be needed just to stop further divergence. [Ch 15, line 16028](./source/source.txt#L16028)
- **Companions:** top income-tax rates “probably above 80 percent” on incomes over $500,000–1 million, aimed at curbing top pay rather than raising revenue. [Ch 14, line 15474](./source/source.txt#L15474) For public debt, a one-off progressive capital levy is better than inflation, and austerity is worst. [Ch 16, line 16400](./source/source.txt#L16400) The euro needs a Eurozone budgetary parliament. [Ch 16, line 17003](./source/source.txt#L17003)
- **Boundary:** Piketty calls the tax “a utopian idea” that needs unrealistic cooperation, and says Europe is the place to start. [Ch 15, line 15555](./source/source.txt#L15555) [Ch 15, line 15561](./source/source.txt#L15561) **[Analysis]** The revenue estimate (about 2% of European GDP) assumes market valuation of unlisted firms and does not quantify avoidance.
- **Application [Application]:** judge any wealth-tax proposal first on whether it has a financial cadaster, meaning complete and automatic reporting of who owns what.

> “The primary purpose of the capital tax is not to finance the social state but to regulate capitalism.” [Ch 15, line 15647](./source/source.txt#L15647)

> “The rich world is rich, but the governments of the rich world are poor.” [Ch 16, line 16387](./source/source.txt#L16387)

## Critical assessment

Everything in this section is **[Analysis]** unless it carries a source link.

- **Strongest contribution:** the data. Two centuries of income-tax, estate and national-wealth series, assembled with many collaborators ([Introduction, line 744](./source/source.txt#L744)), changed what the inequality debate is about. The French inheritance flow, rebuilt by two independent methods that agree, is the best single series in the book ([Ch 11, line 11566](./source/source.txt#L11566)).
- **Weakest claim:** the forecast. It needs three things to hold together: frontier growth stays at 1–1.5% “no matter what economic policies are adopted” ([Conclusion, line 17432](./source/source.txt#L17432)), r stays near 4–5% while β rises, and saving does not fall as growth falls. Each is argued separately. Their joint persistence over a century is assumed. The elasticity above one that keeps r up is the thinnest link, and Piketty concedes the bargaining-power alternative ([Ch 6, line 6798](./source/source.txt#L6798)).
- **Hidden assumptions:**
  - Market value is the right measure of capital, so asset-price swings count as changes in wealth.
  - Tax records capture the top well enough once corrected for evasion. Piketty says his top shares are lower bounds ([Ch 8, line 8564](./source/source.txt#L8564)).
  - France is the more representative future, because its demography looks more like the world's to come ([Introduction, line 1125](./source/source.txt#L1125)).
- **Meaningful counterargument:** the rise in β since 1970 is partly a price effect, not accumulation. Piketty's own decomposition puts asset prices at a quarter to a third of the rise in national capital ([Ch 5, line 5852](./source/source.txt#L5852)). And the argument that rising US inequality comes mostly from wages ([Ch 8, line 9110](./source/source.txt#L9110)) sits awkwardly with a book whose title and engine are capital. The two stories meet only in the forecast that today's supermanagers become tomorrow's rentiers ([Ch 11, line 11965](./source/source.txt#L11965)).
- **Question left unanswered:** political feasibility. Piketty grants that the global tax needs “a very high and no doubt unrealistic level of international cooperation” ([Ch 15, line 15561](./source/source.txt#L15561)). His own history says the last great equalization took two world wars ([Ch 14, line 15517](./source/source.txt#L15517)). The book does not show how a peaceful route gets built.

## Reading recommendation

**Read selected chapters.** Read the Introduction, Chapters 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11 and 15, and the Conclusion, roughly half the main text. These carry the two laws, the elasticity argument, the inequality data, the r > g theory, the return of inheritance and the remedy. Add Chapter 14 to understand the case for very high top income-tax rates. What this report cannot compress is the evidence itself. The book's force comes from long series country by country, which live in figures and tables missing from this extraction, so read a print or PDF edition with the charts. Chapters 1–4, 13 and the second halves of 12 and 16 can be skimmed.
