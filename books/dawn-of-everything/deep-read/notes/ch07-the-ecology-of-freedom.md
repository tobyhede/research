# ch07: The Ecology of Freedom (pp. 189–207)

Scope note: the chapter heading falls on p. 189 and the text ends on p. 207, where chapter 8 begins. One figure (a map, p. 192) is not in the text. Endnotes are not in this workspace's `source.txt`.

## Question this chapter answers

Once farming existed, did it spread as an unstoppable advance that carried private property and hierarchy with it, and what decided whether early farming societies succeeded or failed?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reluctant farmers | p. 189 | The first cultivators of the Fertile Crescent, who understood what agriculture demanded and avoided a major commitment to it. |
| Communal tenure | p. 190 | Open-field systems, periodic redistribution of plots and co-operative pasture (the Russian mir, run-rig or rundale, German mark associations, Palestinian mash'a, Balinese subak). Evidence that farming does not require private land. |
| Centres of domestication | p. 191 | Now fifteen to twenty independent ones, most of which did not lead to states. |
| Ecological imperialism | p. 194 | Crosby's thesis that Old World crops, animals and diseases, turned loose on the Americas and Oceania after 1492, explain European ascendancy. Accepted for the modern period, rejected as a model for the Neolithic. |
| Holocene | p. 195 | The warm period beginning about 12,000 years ago; a golden age for foragers and the setting in which farming started. |
| Ecology of freedom | p. 196 | Adapted from Bookchin: the tendency of societies to move freely in and out of farming, to raise crops and animals without becoming existentially dependent on them, keeping a food web broad enough that cultivation is not a matter of life and death. The opposite of being a peasant. |
| Play farming | p. 196 | Shorthand for the ecology of freedom; also "low-level food production" (p. 204). |
| Serious farming | p. 201 | Reliance on fully domesticated species around whose needs life is organised (Linear Pottery Europe, the Neolithic Nile valley, Lapita). |
| Linear Pottery | p. 197 | The culture of central Europe's first farmers, from about 5500 BC. |
| Lapita horizon | p. 200 | The farming dispersal from Taiwan and the Philippines to Polynesia, from about 1600 BC. |
| Terra preta | p. 204 | Amazonian dark earths, anthropogenic soils enriched slowly by village waste and burning. |
| Teleological reasoning | p. 206 | Jumping from the beginning of the story (first farming) to its end (states and billions of people) and assuming the middle. |

## Leading propositions

- It no longer makes sense to ask what "the" social implications of the transition to farming were, as if there were one transition (p. 189).
- People who want to expand agriculture without privatising land or submitting to overseers have always found ways to do so; communal tenure and periodic redistribution were common and long-lived (p. 190).
- The old picture of a few nuclear zones of domestication leading to states has been replaced by fifteen to twenty centres, none of which followed a linear path from food production to state formation (p. 191).
- Farming did not "fail to reach" California; Californians rejected it. In the American Southwest the trend for some 500 years before Europeans arrived was abandonment of maize and beans (p. 192).
- Language about crops "spreading" reflects the last few centuries of imperial expansion; Neolithic crops could not extend their range without sustained human effort (pp. 192-193).
- The geographical point made by Diamond and Morris about Eurasia's east-west axis is sound as far as it goes (p. 193).
- Agriculture did not develop earlier because only two sustained warm periods have occurred in our species' existence, and in the first humans had a restricted range (pp. 194-195).
- Early farmers entered the Holocene as cultural underdogs, filling gaps left by foragers (p. 196).
- Biodiversity, not bio-power, was the initial key to the growth of Neolithic food production (p. 197).
- Central Europe's first farmers suffered a boom and bust between 5000 and 4500 BC, with massacres, and extensive cereal farming resumed only about a thousand years later (pp. 197-198).
- In the Nile valley (5000 to 4000 BC) the Neolithic package was remade around herding and bodily display, with cereals relegated (pp. 199-200).
- Lapita colonists dropped rice and millet and diversified continuously, avoiding established populations (p. 200).
- Serious farmers tended to form societies with hard boundaries and to settle land others did not use (p. 201).
- In Amazonia people had the skills to farm and held back from it for thousands of years; manioc became a staple only after European contact (pp. 202-204).
- That flexible mode of production nonetheless supported towns, earthworks and roads across Amazonia by the beginning of the Christian era (p. 203).
- The same slow pattern holds in Mexico, the Eastern Woodlands and China: domesticates existed thousands of years before they became staples (pp. 204-205).
- The Linear Pottery economy was a narrowed subset of the Middle Eastern original on uniform terrain, which made it fragile (pp. 205-206).
- Farming often began as an economy of deprivation, in areas where wild resources were thinnest (p. 207).

## Arguments

- **Farming does not entail private property.**
  **Premises**: (p. 190) redistributive tenure systems are attested from Scotland to India, often for centuries in one place; (p. 190) Maine described them as modes of occupation, not property; (ch06) flood-retreat farming resisted enclosure.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 190) the adoption of agriculture cannot be assumed to mean private land ownership, territoriality or an irreversible break with forager egalitarianism. "It may have happened that way sometimes".

- **Why the European case failed and others did not.**
  **Premises**: (p. 205) Linear Pottery sites grew only glume wheats and one or two pulses, against the eight or more crops of the original package; (pp. 205-206) loess plains offered little variety and coastal foragers blocked expansion; (p. 206) everything depended on one food web; (pp. 199-200, 202-204) the Nile, Lapita and Amazonian cases kept or added diversity.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 197, 206) narrow commitment made farming an experiment that could fail; breadth of resources was what made early food production viable. My observation: the comparison is across cases with very unequal evidence, as the authors themselves warn (p. 205), and the Nile and Lapita cases are classed as "serious" farming too, so the contrast is diversity versus uniformity, not play versus serious.

- **Against teleology.**
  **Premises**: (p. 206) a sceptic would say that agriculture's long-run effects (cities by 2000 BC, population growth from about 5 million to 900 million by 1800) are what matter, and that large populations need chains of command; (p. 207) the first claim says nothing about what happened in between; the second is an assumption.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 206-207) one cannot jump from the beginning to the end and assume the middle; the middle is the subject of the next chapters. My observation: the authors state the sceptic's case fairly and do not answer the demographic point here. They defer the question of scale and hierarchy to ch08.

- **Against "spread" as a natural process.**
  **Premises**: (p. 193) domestic species cannot extend beyond their ecological limits without weeding, manuring and terracing; (p. 193) most people in the early Holocene were not farmers and crops faced wild predators and parasites; (p. 192) fast spread in recent centuries was driven by armies and profit.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 192-194) expansion before the sixteenth century was full of false starts and reversals.

## Evidence offered

- Historical land-tenure systems; Maine's 1875 Lectures (p. 190). Textual and historical.
- Current counts of domestication centres with their crops (p. 191). Archaeological and genetic, summarised.
- Linear Pottery massacre sites: Kilianstädten, Talheim, Schletz (Asparn), Herxheim; enclosure ditches; demographic modelling showing boom and bust (pp. 197-198). Archaeological, well dated according to the authors.
- Isotope evidence from Mesolithic Brittany of women with inland diets among coastal foragers; the capture interpretation is labelled speculative (pp. 198-199).
- Mesolithic cemeteries and wood-carving from the Baltic to Brittany (p. 198). Archaeological.
- Neolithic cemeteries along the Nile with ornaments and cosmetics (p. 200). Archaeological.
- Lapita pottery, obsidian from the Bismarck Archipelago, settlement of Fiji, Tonga, Samoa (pp. 200-201). Archaeological.
- Amazonia: Nambikwara ethnography, pet-keeping, masters of the animals; Arawak language distribution; geometric earthworks exposed by deforestation; dark earths; early manioc (7000 BC) and maize (pp. 201-204). Ethnographic, linguistic and archaeological.
- Dates for domesticates and staples in Mexico, the Eastern Woodlands and China (pp. 204-205). Archaeobotanical, summarised.
- Crosby, Diamond and Morris as interlocutors (pp. 192-194).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Generalises ch06's Fertile Crescent findings to the rest of the world and repeats ch05's point about California's rejection of farming (p. 192).
- The Nambikwara (ch03 pp. 79-81) return as the model of moving in and out of farming (pp. 201-202).
- Amazonian towns and earthworks (p. 203) were promised in ch03 (p. 90) as evidence that Amazonians knew large polities.
- Evading the state by growing tubers and moving (p. 204) echoes James C. Scott, who is thanked in the acknowledgements (fm01, p. 6); the text does not name him here.
- The sceptic's objection about scale (p. 206) sets up ch08's attack on the assumption that large populations need rulers.
- The chapter concedes more to geography (p. 193) and to agriculture's "explosive growth potential" (p. 207) than the book's rhetoric elsewhere suggests. The long-run demographic victory of farming is acknowledged and not explained away.
- The statement that farming often began as an economy of deprivation (p. 207) sits oddly beside ch06's picture of leisurely, playful cultivation in settlements occupied for other reasons. The two can be reconciled (cultivation as a supplement in poorer lowland niches), but the text does not do so explicitly.

## Open questions

- Why did the sharp boundaries of "serious" farming societies form, and do they connect to ch04's claim that bounded cultures made domination easier?
- Did other early farming regions also see collapses not yet detected? The authors allow it (p. 205).
- If play farming was so durable, what tipped particular societies into full dependence? The chapter gives climate (a cooling around 5000 BC in China, p. 205) as one push and otherwise leaves it open.
