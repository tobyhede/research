# ch01: Farewell to Humanity's Childhood (pp. 6–25)

Scope note: the chapter heading falls at the foot of p. 6 and the text ends two-thirds of the way down p. 25, where chapter 2 begins. Endnote numbers appear in the text but the endnotes themselves are not in this workspace's `source.txt`.

## Question this chapter answers

Why is "what is the origin of social inequality?" the wrong question to ask of human history, and what should replace it?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rousseauian story | p. 7 | The popular account derived from Rousseau's 1754 Discourse: humans once lived in small egalitarian forager bands, and agriculture, then cities, brought civilization, the state and nearly everything bad with them. |
| Hobbesian story | p. 8 | The rival account: the original condition was a war of all against all, and such progress as there has been is owed to governments, courts, bureaucracies and police repressing our baser instincts. |
| State of Nature | p. 15 | A rhetorical device of natural law theory, a thought experiment and not a historical claim; both Rousseau and Hobbes used it that way. |
| Social evolution | p. 9 | The idea that societies can be arranged in stages of development (hunter-gatherers, farmers, urban-industrial), each with its own technology and organisation. The authors trace it to a conservative backlash against critiques of European civilization. |
| Indigenous critique | p. 10 | The critique of European society made by indigenous observers such as the Wendat statesman Kandiaronk, which the authors say inspired the Enlightenment critique. Developed in ch02. |
| Inequality | pp. 10-11 | A framing word suited to technocratic reform: it invites tinkering with numbers and makes its object seem the unavoidable price of scale. The authors prefer to ask about wealth turning into power and about people being told their needs do not matter. |
| Contemporary ancestors | p. 17 | Living peoples (Hadza, Pygmies, !Kung, Yanomami) cherry-picked as stand-ins for humans in a State of Nature. A practice the authors reject. |
| Primitive trade | p. 23 | The label put on long-distance movement of precious objects in prehistory, used to argue that markets are natural. The authors call the inference circular. |
| Self-creation | p. 12 | The human capacity to experiment with different forms of social organisation; what the authors take to be the real subject of history. |

## Leading propositions

- Arguing about whether humans are innately good or evil makes as little sense as arguing about whether humans are fundamentally fat or thin; the terms are human inventions for comparing ourselves with one another (p. 7).
- The two available big pictures of history, Rousseau's and Hobbes's, are objectionable on three counts: they are not true, they have dire political implications, and they make the past needlessly dull (p. 8).
- Evidence from archaeology and anthropology accumulated over recent decades points to a different account of the last 30,000 years or so (pp. 8-9).
- Pre-agricultural societies were not confined to small egalitarian bands; the forager world was one of bold social experiments (p. 9).
- Agriculture did not mean the start of private property or an irreversible step to inequality, and many early farming communities were relatively free of ranks (p. 9).
- A surprising number of the earliest cities were organised on egalitarian lines, without authoritarian rulers or administrators (p. 9).
- The idea of social evolution in stages arose as a conservative backlash against critiques of European civilization, and those critiques originated with indigenous observers, not Enlightenment philosophers (pp. 9-10).
- There are tendencies in history but no laws except those we make up ourselves (p. 10).
- Framing social problems as "inequality" suits an age of technocratic reformers and implies that no real transformation is possible (pp. 10-11).
- The real question of history is not equal access to material resources but the equal capacity to contribute to decisions about how to live together (p. 12).
- The authors set out to write about the origins of inequality and gave it up, because the framing assumes an idyllic starting state and a single point where things went wrong (p. 13).
- Generalist accounts by Fukuyama and Diamond repeat Rousseau: bands, then tribes, chiefdoms and states, with hierarchy inevitable at scale. These are prejudices presented as facts (pp. 13-14).
- Rousseau never claimed his State of Nature had existed; it was a thought experiment, and Hobbes's state of war was likewise not a historical claim (pp. 14-15).
- Pinker is the model modern Hobbesian, and he argues from anecdote (Ötzi) and not from a balanced appraisal of the evidence (p. 16).
- The ideals of freedom, equality and democracy are not products of a "Western tradition"; before the nineteenth century almost no European author thought democracy anything but a terrible form of government (pp. 18-19).
- The only dependable test of which way of life people prefer is to let them experience both and watch what they choose; where people had that choice between indigenous American and European society they mostly chose the former (pp. 20-21).
- The conventional narrative reduces people to stereotypes, and in doing so shrinks our sense of what is possible (p. 22).
- Social theory must simplify to discover anything, but the simplifications of Hobbes and Rousseau have outlived their use (p. 22).
- The book offers both the foundations of a new world history and a search for the right questions; Rousseau was not entirely mistaken, since something has been lost, but he misidentified it (p. 25).

## Arguments

- **Against "inequality" as the organising problem.**
  **Premises**: (p. 11) it is unclear what eliminating inequality would even mean, whereas overthrowing capitalism or breaking state power is at least imaginable; (p. 11) debating it lets one adjust Gini coefficients and tax regimes without addressing what people object to, that wealth becomes power over others and that some are told their lives have no worth; (p. 11) inequality is then presented as the inevitable result of any large, complex society; (p. 12) so real equality is thought possible only for tiny forager bands.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 11-12) the framing makes "wistful pessimism" look like common sense and leaves only modest tinkering.

- **Against a single original form of society.**
  **Premises**: (p. 12) people painted at Altamira over at least 10,000 years; (p. 12) it is bizarre to suppose nobody anywhere experimented with other social arrangements in that time; (p. 12) the capacity for such experiment is part of what makes us human.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 12) abandon the Garden of Eden, the idea that for hundreds of thousands of years everyone shared one idyllic form of organisation. My observation: this is an argument from plausibility; the evidence is deferred to chs. 3 and 4.

- **The cherry-picking argument against Pinker.**
  **Premises**: (p. 16) Pinker uses Ötzi as an emblem of early violence; (pp. 16-17) one could as easily pick Romito 2, a man with severe dwarfism supported to adulthood by his hunter-gatherer community about 10,000 years ago; (p. 17) balanced appraisals of Palaeolithic burials show both disability and high levels of care.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 17) picking emblematic corpses proves nothing either way; the authors explicitly decline to draw the opposite conclusion, noting that only unusual individuals seem to have been buried at all in the Palaeolithic.

- **The revealed-preference argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 20) if Pinker is right, anyone offered the choice between tribal life and Western civilization would choose the latter; (p. 20) Helena Valero, abducted by Yanomami in 1932, returned to Western society in 1956 and then chose to go back; (pp. 20-21) colonial American records show captured or adopted settlers almost invariably staying with indigenous societies, while Amerindians brought into European society mostly left; (p. 21) Franklin's letter says the same.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 20-21) something is wrong with Pinker's conclusion that Western civilization has made life better by any measure. My observation: the samples are of people who had already been socialised into one society for years, and the claim "almost invariably" rests on endnotes that are not in this text.

- **Against "primitive trade" as proof of universal markets.**
  **Premises**: (p. 23) objects moved long distances, so there was trade, so there were markets: a circle; (pp. 23-24) ethnography shows other ways valuables travel: the kula chain, dream-quests among Iroquoians, travelling curers such as Cabeza de Vaca's troupe, women's gambling.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 24) guesses about what past people were doing are usually less interesting and less human than what was likely going on.

## Evidence offered

- Published generalist accounts quoted as the target: Fukuyama, The Origins of Political Order (2011), and Diamond, The World Until Yesterday (2012) (pp. 13-14); Pinker, The Better Angels of Our Nature and Enlightenment Now (p. 16). Textual.
- Rousseau's own statement that his research is hypothetical reasoning, not historical truth (p. 14). Textual.
- Romito 2, a 10,000-year-old burial from Calabria (pp. 16-17). Archaeological, used only to show the game can be played both ways.
- Yanomami homicide rates reported as average-to-low among Amerindian groups; Chagnon's 1968 book and the controversy over it (pp. 17-18). Ethnographic, second-hand.
- Helena Valero's own account of her life (p. 20). Testimony.
- Benjamin Franklin's private letter on captives and adoptees (p. 21). Textual, a contemporary observer's generalisation.
- Malinowski on the kula chain of the Massim Islands; Iroquoian dream-fulfilment; Cabeza de Vaca in 1528; Warren DeBoer's estimate on gambling and the spread of shells (pp. 23-24). Ethnographic and historical.
- The chapter's central positive claims about foragers, farmers and cities (p. 9) are announced, not evidenced, here.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The claim that the stage theory of social evolution began as a backlash against the indigenous critique (pp. 9-10) is the thesis of ch02 and is picked up again in ch11.
- The three positive claims on p. 9 map onto the rest of the book: foragers (chs. 3-5), farming (chs. 6-7), cities (chs. 8-9), the state (ch10).
- "Something has been lost" (p. 25) is answered in ch12 with the three basic freedoms.
- The chapter rejects the search for an origin of inequality but keeps asking how we "came to be trapped" (pp. 12-13), which is itself a question about a turning point. The book lives with this tension to the end.
- The authors say social theory must simplify (p. 22) while attacking others for simplifying; their defence is that old simplifications have stopped revealing anything.

## Open questions

- How representative are the captive narratives, and who recorded them?
- If there was no single original form of society, what is the baseline from which the authors measure that something "has been lost"?
- The treatment of Pinker, Diamond and Fukuyama is polemical and brief; whether it is fair to them cannot be judged from this text alone.
