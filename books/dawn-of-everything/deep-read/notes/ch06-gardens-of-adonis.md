# ch06: Gardens of Adonis (pp. 160–189)

Scope note: the chapter heading falls on p. 160 and the text ends on p. 189, where chapter 7 begins. One figure (a map of the Fertile Crescent, p. 174) is not in the text. Endnotes are not in this workspace's `source.txt`.

## Question this chapter answers

Was the beginning of farming in the Middle East an "Agricultural Revolution" that brought property and inequality, and if not, what was it?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gardens of Adonis | pp. 160-161 | Plato's image of festive, quick-sprouting rooftop gardens made by Athenian women, which produced no food; the authors' emblem for farming begun playfully. Contrasted with Demeter and the Thesmophoria, emblem of serious farming. |
| Agricultural Revolution | p. 162 | Childe's term for the invention of farming as a rapid, world-changing switch. The authors say the evidence no longer supports the phrase (p. 188). |
| Matriarchy | p. 167 | Not rule by women (that is gynarchy) but a situation where the role of mothers in the household is the model and economic basis for female authority elsewhere, giving women a preponderance of day-to-day power. On this definition the Wendat, Hopi, Zuñi and Minangkabau qualify. |
| Old Europe | p. 165 | Gimbutas's name for the Neolithic village world of the Balkans and eastern Mediterranean, about 7000 to 3500 BC, which she held to be peaceful and without sharp differences of wealth or between the sexes. |
| Kurgan folk | p. 165 | Gimbutas's cattle-keeping, warlike, patriarchal steppe peoples whose migration she held ended Old Europe. |
| Upland and lowland crescents | p. 172 | The authors' division of the Fertile Crescent after about 10,000 BC: the foothills of the Taurus and Zagros (forest and game-rich prairie), and the lowlands of the Jordan and Euphrates valleys (fertile pockets tied to rivers and springs). |
| Levantine Corridor | p. 173 | The trade route from the Syrian Euphrates through the Damascus basin into the Jordan valley, home to craft-specialist lowlanders. |
| Domestication | p. 174 | In crops, the loss of traits that allow reproduction in the wild, chiefly spontaneous seed dispersal (the brittle rachis). |
| Pre-domestication cultivation | pp. 177-178 | Tilling and tending wild cereals without their becoming domestic, detected through grain size and arable weeds. |
| Flood-retreat farming | p. 179 | Also décrue farming: sowing on soil left by receding seasonal floods. Light labour, no central management, and resistant to enclosure because fertile ground shifts yearly. |
| Play farming | p. 188 | The authors' term for the 3,000 years in which people moved in and out of cultivation without committing to it. |
| Science of the concrete | pp. 181-182 | Lévi-Strauss's term for discovery through close engagement with sensible qualities; the authors attribute much of it to women. |
| Skull portraits | p. 187 | Lowland Neolithic practice of modelling clay and plaster over skulls removed from burials; read by the authors as a negation of upland trophy-taking. |

## Leading propositions

- Çatalhöyük (founded about 7400 BC, occupied about 1,500 years, some 5,000 people) has been reinterpreted: its cattle were wild aurochs, its "shrines" were houses, and its figurines were not obviously goddesses (pp. 162-163).
- The idea of Neolithic matriarchy became an academic taboo, more than mere doubt about an old theory would warrant (pp. 163-164).
- Gimbutas was vilified for doing what male scholars do routinely, building a grand narrative; ancient DNA now supports her claim of a steppe migration in the third millennium BC (pp. 166-167).
- Matriarchy in the household sense is real and documented, so its presence in the Neolithic is a question of evidence, not a fantasy (pp. 167-168).
- At Çatalhöyük the elaborate figurines depict elder women; there is no equivalent for elder men; diet and burial treatment show parity between the sexes (pp. 168-169).
- Çatalhöyük had no central authority; households were autonomous, and prestigious houses were scattered among the others (pp. 169-170).
- Its art and ritual ignore agriculture and centre on hunting, though people lived mainly on domestic cereals, sheep and goats (p. 170).
- What survives at Çatalhöyük probably reflects winter social arrangements; seasonal variation of social structure continued there (p. 171).
- Its people adopted domestic cereals, sheep and goats but refused domestic cattle and pigs for about a thousand years, a cultural refusal (p. 172).
- Founder crops were domesticated at several places along the Levantine Corridor and beyond, not in one core area (p. 174).
- Experiments show wheat could be domesticated in 20 to 200 years of simple harvesting; in the Fertile Crescent it took about 3,000 (pp. 176-178).
- People settled in villages before cereals were a large part of their diet, and may have harvested wild grasses first for straw (p. 177).
- That 3,000-year span is a phase of history in its own right, in which foragers moved in and out of cultivation without becoming bound to crops (p. 178).
- Flood-retreat farming did not lend itself to private property (p. 179).
- Narratives in which wheat domesticates humans replace questions about who did the intellectual work with a version of the Eden story, and write women out (pp. 175, 180).
- Where evidence exists, plant knowledge and fibre crafts are associated with women, and these crafts carry mathematical and geometrical knowledge (pp. 180-181).
- Early farming looks less like an economic transition than a media revolution and a social revolution, leisurely, unforced by catastrophe, and unlikely to produce radical inequality (p. 183).
- Upland sites (Göbekli Tepe, Nevalı Çori, Çayönü) show stone monuments and imagery of male predation and severed heads; lowland sites show clay, female figurines and skull portraits (pp. 184-187).
- The two patterns were in contact through trade in obsidian and shell, and their opposition is plausibly schismogenesis (pp. 173, 186-187).
- In the Fertile Crescent it was the groups least dependent on farming that show entrenched stratification and violence; the early farmers look more egalitarian, linked to the visibility of women (p. 189).

## Arguments

- **Against the "revolution".**
  **Premises**: (p. 176) experiments since the 1980s show domestication achievable in 20 to 30 years, at most 200; (p. 178) cultivation of wild cereals in northern Syria goes back to at least 10,000 BC; (p. 178) the switch to the tough rachis was complete only around 7000 BC; (unstated) had humans been drawn along by their crops, the genetic change would have followed cultivation at the experimental rate.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 178) a 3,000-year process is too long to be a revolution or a transition; people were keeping farming at arm's length. The authors add that cultivators "may even have strategically chosen" practices that worked against domestication (p. 178), which is offered as a possibility.

- **Against property as farming's immediate consequence.**
  **Premises**: (p. 179) early lowland cultivation was flood-retreat farming on lake and river margins; (p. 179) the fertile ground moves from year to year, so fixed plots and boundary stones make little sense; (p. 179) the labour is light and needs no central management.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 179) these systems leaned towards collective holding or flexible reallocation, contrary to Rousseau. The authors qualify: no ecology is innately egalitarian.

- **Refusal at Çatalhöyük.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 171-172) cattle and boar had been domesticated to the east a thousand years before the town was founded; (p. 172) its founders took cereals, pulses, sheep and goats from that direction but not cattle or pigs; (p. 172) there was no environmental obstacle; (pp. 169, 172) wild cattle and boar were the prestigious prey of its art.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 172) the refusal was cultural, protecting a certain kind of society, perhaps especially men's prestige.

- **Schismogenesis in the Fertile Crescent.**
  **Premises**: (p. 173) upland and lowland societies exchanged obsidian and shells and so knew each other; (pp. 184-186) uplanders built in stone with male, predatory imagery and took and displayed heads; (p. 187) lowlanders also gave heads ritual importance but modelled them into lifelike portraits kept as heirlooms; (p. 187) skull portraits peaked in the eighth millennium BC as Göbekli Tepe declined.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 186-188) it is "equally possible, and perhaps more plausible" to read the contrast as mutual, self-conscious differentiation. My observation: the authors grant coincidence and environment as alternatives (p. 186) and concede the lowland skull tradition predates the Neolithic (p. 187), which weakens the reading that it arose as a response. The claim is put forward as a plausible interpretation and should be carried as such.

- **Women as the scientists of the Neolithic.**
  **Premises**: (p. 180) harvesting and processing wild plants is nearly everywhere women's work; (pp. 180-181) fibre crafts developed with plant cultivation and embody geometry; (p. 181) Ice Age figurines show woven headgear and string skirts; (p. 182) lowland art gives women prominence.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 183) women's work and knowledge were central to the creation of Neolithic life. My observation: the first premise projects recent ethnographic regularity back ten thousand years; the authors acknowledge direct archaeological evidence is hard to come by (p. 180).

## Evidence offered

- Çatalhöyük excavations since the 1990s: faunal identification of aurochs, figurine find-spots, dental and skeletal studies, house rebuilding sequences, obsidian from Cappadocia 125 miles away; only about 5 per cent of the site excavated (pp. 162-163, 168-170). Archaeological.
- Ancient DNA studies of steppe migration (p. 167). Genetic; reported as contested in degree.
- Ethnographic matriarchies: Wendat longhouse councils, Hopi, Zuñi, Minangkabau (pp. 167-168).
- Archaeobotany: samples in the tens of thousands tracking grain size and weed flora; experimental harvesting from the 1980s (pp. 176-178). Scientific, the chapter's strongest evidence.
- Lowland sites: Jericho and Basta approaching ten hectares, Tell Aswad, Abu Hureyra, Jerf el-Ahmar with grain storage in subterranean lodges (pp. 173-174, 179). Archaeological.
- Upland sites: Göbekli Tepe imagery and skull fragments with incisions and drill-holes; Nevalı Çori; Karahan Tepe; Çayönü's House of Skulls with remains of over 450 people and blood residues on a stone table (pp. 184-186). Archaeological. The authors note that archaeologists are rightly cautious about inferring warfare and that evidence of interpersonal violence is limited (p. 184).
- Skull portraits from the Levantine Corridor and Çatalhöyük; mutilated skulls at Tell Qarassa (p. 187). Archaeological.
- Gimbutas's own books, and the history of their reception (pp. 165-166). Textual.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Delivers the evidence for ch01's claim that farming did not mean private property or an irreversible step to inequality (ch01 p. 9), and returns to Göbekli Tepe as promised in ch03 (ch03 p. 73).
- Uses ch05's schismogenesis and "refusal" directly, with the Pacific Coast as the explicit analogy (pp. 173, 183).
- Seasonal alternation of social structure (ch03) is carried into a farming town (p. 171).
- Harari is again the foil (p. 175), as in ch03 (p. 75); Diamond's "worst mistake" (p. 180) recalls ch01.
- The chapter ends by asking what went wrong if farming set people on a course away from domination (p. 189); ch07 broadens the question and chs. 8 to 10 pursue it.
- The authors attack others' myth-making while defending Gimbutas's, on the ground that hers was partly right and unfairly singled out. They concede "Some, maybe most" of the criticisms of her had truth in them (p. 166).
- The upland groups are described as hunter-foragers with "a striking turn towards hierarchy" (p. 173), but ch03 argued that monuments and rich burials need not mean hierarchy. Here the authors read Göbekli Tepe as stratified and violent to make the contrast with the lowlands.

## Open questions

- If the skull-curating tradition is Natufian, what exactly was new in the lowlands in response to the uplands?
- How representative is 5 per cent of Çatalhöyük?
- Was the long delay in domestication a choice, or a by-product of harvesting methods and gene flow from wild stands? The text argues for choice and mentions the alternative only in passing (p. 179).
