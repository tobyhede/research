# ch02: Wicked Liberty (pp. 25–64)

Scope note: the chapter heading falls on p. 25 and the text ends near the top of p. 64, where chapter 3 begins. Endnotes are not in this workspace's `source.txt`, so the sources behind many attributions cannot be checked here.

## Question this chapter answers

Where did the question "what is the origin of inequality?" come from, and why did eighteenth-century Europeans come to assume that humans without civilization would be free and equal?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indigenous critique | p. 29 | A consistent moral and intellectual assault on European society voiced by Native American observers from the seventeenth century on (defined again p. 42). It focused first on the Europeans' lack of freedom and mutual aid, and only later on equality (p. 33). |
| Sock-puppet | p. 33 | The historians' assumption that indigenous speakers in European texts are only mouthpieces for European authors' own subversive ideas. The authors reject it. |
| American intellectuals | p. 32 | "American" as used at the time, for indigenous inhabitants of the Western Hemisphere; "intellectual" for anyone in the habit of arguing about abstract ideas. |
| Noble savage | p. 34 | Originally not nobility of character: Indian men hunted and fought, which in Europe was the business of noblemen. Later (p. 58) a term of ridicule popularised around 1859. |
| Wicked liberty | p. 39 | A Jesuit's phrase for the freedom of indigenous Americans, seen as the main obstacle to their accepting Christian law. |
| Equality (American sense) | p. 39 | An extension of freedom: people are equal in being equally free to obey or disobey orders. Contrasted with Eurasian "equality before the law", which is equality in common subjection to a sovereign. |
| Baseline communism | pp. 41-42 | Not a property regime but "from each according to their abilities, to each according to their needs" in the minimal form found in every society: if the need is great and the cost modest, one helps. Societies differ in how far it extends. |
| Schismogenesis | p. 48 | Bateson's term for people's tendency to define themselves against one another; extended by the authors from processes within a society to relations between societies. |
| Stages of social evolution | p. 51 | Turgot's theory that societies pass from hunting to pastoralism to farming to commercial civilization. The authors say it was devised in direct response to the indigenous critique. |
| Myth of the myth of the noble savage | p. 58 | Ellingson's point that the naive stereotype was attributed to others by its critics; no such myth existed in the travel literature. |
| Myth of the stupid savage | p. 61 | The authors' name for what they call the toxic element of Rousseau's legacy: savages happy because they cannot imagine things otherwise. |
| Egalitarian society | pp. 61-62 | A default term with no analytical substance: societies lacking princes, judges, overseers or hereditary priests, equal only in that the obvious tokens of inequality are missing. |

## Leading propositions

- Ideas are produced in conversations, not by solitary great men; Rousseau's Discourse was an entry in an essay contest set by the Académie de Dijon in 1754 (p. 26).
- The contest question assumed that inequality had an origin, a startling assumption in an absolutist monarchy where no one had experienced a society of equals (pp. 26-27).
- The Enlightenment was the result of Europe's sudden exposure, after the late fifteenth century, to a flood of unfamiliar social, scientific and political ideas (p. 27).
- Historians of ideas wrongly assume European thinkers were only responding to one another, even when those thinkers said they were borrowing from abroad; Leibniz and Chinese statecraft is the example (pp. 27-28).
- Medieval thought had no concept of social equality; the terms equality and inequality entered common use in the early seventeenth century through natural law theory, which arose from debates over the conquest of the Americas (pp. 29-30).
- The State of Nature let philosophers imagine people without civilization as humanity in the raw and not as degenerate remnants (p. 31).
- Indigenous Americans did develop a strong critical view of French institutions, and Enlightenment thinkers said their ideals of liberty and equality drew on American sources because it was true (pp. 33-34).
- In the Jesuit Relations neither side says much about equality; the argument is about freedom, and both sides agreed that Americans were free and Europeans were not. They disagreed about whether freedom was desirable (p. 36).
- Wendat office-holders could not compel anyone; wealth could not be converted into power over others (p. 38).
- The Jesuits recognised a link between refusal of arbitrary power, open political debate and skill in reasoned argument (p. 40).
- The style of debate later identified with the Enlightenment (rational, sceptical, empirical, conversational) matches the style Iroquoian speakers prized (pp. 40-41).
- Baseline communism among the Northeast Woodlands peoples supported individual freedom; it was not opposed to it (p. 42).
- The arguments in Lahontan's Dialogues (1703) are, in their basics, Kandiaronk's own (pp. 44-45).
- Americans who saw France identified the key difference: in France power over possessions could be translated directly into power over people (p. 45).
- Kandiaronk exaggerated, as a skilled debater would; that is no reason to treat the Dialogues as European fantasy (p. 48).
- Lahontan's books were widely read for a century and set off a genre of outsider critiques by Montesquieu, Diderot, Voltaire, Graffigny and others (pp. 49-50).
- Turgot, replying to Graffigny in 1751, argued that savage freedom and equality are signs of poverty, and went on to formulate the four-stage theory; social evolutionism was first articulated as a response to the indigenous critique (pp. 51-52).
- Rousseau's Discourse succeeded because it fused the indigenous critique, the biblical Fall and the new stages of material development (pp. 55-56).
- What was lost in Rousseau's translation was the sense of possibility: unlike Kandiaronk he could not imagine society founded on anything but property (p. 56).
- European freedom was tied to private property and to the Roman household head's power; freedom in that tradition is exercised at others' cost (p. 56).
- By folding the indigenous critique into the doctrine of progress, Rousseau wrote the founding document of the left as an intellectual project (p. 58).
- "Egalitarian" has no clear meaning, and any history of the origins of inequality is really a Turgot-style history of civilization (p. 62).
- Self-conscious ideals of equality are latecomers in history and rarely apply to everyone (p. 63).
- The book will ask what first made possible kings, priests, overseers and judges, without assuming the answer (p. 64).

## Arguments

- **That the dialogue was real.**
  **Premises**: (p. 32) nobody denies that Europeans and Americans learned each other's languages and that missionaries debated as part of their work; (p. 33) missionary and travel literature was widely read in Europe; (pp. 34-39) Biard, Sagard, Le Jeune and Lallemant, hostile witnesses, independently report the same indigenous complaints; (unstated) hostile witnesses reporting criticism of themselves have little reason to invent it.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 33) the default assumption that such reports are projection should be reversed.

- **That the Dialogues reflect Kandiaronk.**
  **Premises**: (p. 44) Charlevoix and others describe Kandiaronk as a brilliant debater who was invited to Frontenac's table to argue; (p. 44) the Wendat sent an ambassador to Louis XIV's court in 1691 and Kandiaronk held the office that made him the logical choice; (p. 44) the criticisms attributed to Adario match those documented from other Iroquoian speakers; (p. 44) Lahontan said he worked from notes.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 44-45) the text was embellished, but "the basic arguments were Kandiaronk’s own". My observation: each premise shows the dialogues could be genuine. The step to "every reason to believe" is the authors' judgment, and the visit to France is inferred from an office, not attested. The authors themselves list the grounds for doubt on pp. 43-44.

- **How freedom became equality.**
  **Premises**: (p. 36) early exchanges concern freedom and mutual aid; (p. 45) Americans who had seen France added a critique of property; (p. 48) Kandiaronk invokes "a levelling equality" as a provocation; (p. 51) Turgot answers on the ground of equality, calling it the mark of poverty.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 51-52) the debate was shifted from freedom to equality, and equality was assigned to the bottom of an evolutionary ladder.

- **Social evolution as backlash.**
  **Premises**: (p. 51) Turgot's letter to Graffigny answers a novel in the Kandiaronk tradition; (p. 51) his lectures a few years later set out the four stages; (pp. 51-52) Smith, Kames, Ferguson and Millar took them up.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 51) theories of social evolution arose "as a direct response to the power of indigenous critique". My observation: the sequence is shown; that the critique was the cause, and not one occasion among several for an idea Turgot was already developing (the text says he had been arguing for the primacy of technological progress "for some years", p. 51), is asserted.

- **Against "egalitarian".**
  **Premises**: (pp. 61-62) the word could mean ideology or fact, access to land, dignity, voice, or an observer's scale; many such societies are equal only among adult men; (p. 62) no answer is agreed.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 62) the term is a default label for what is left when civilization is subtracted.

## Evidence offered

- Pierre Biard on the Mi'kmaq, 1608 onward (p. 34); Gabriel Sagard on the Wendat (p. 35); Le Jeune on the Montagnais-Naskapi, 1642 (pp. 36-37); Lallemant on the Wendat, 1644 and 1648 (pp. 37-39). Textual, missionary reports, hostile to what they describe.
- The Jesuit Relations, seventy-one volumes, 1633-1673: the authors report that égal and égalité barely appear (p. 36). An argument partly from absence.
- A survey of medieval literature finding no use of aequalitas for social relations before Columbus (p. 29). Reported second-hand.
- Lahontan's Memoirs and Curious Dialogues (1703); Charlevoix's description of Kandiaronk; Barbara Alice Mann's reassessment (pp. 43-45). Textual, with the authenticity disputed, as the authors say.
- Publishing history: translations, a century in print, the play L'Arlequin sauvage (1721), Graffigny's Letters of a Peruvian Woman (1747) (pp. 49-50).
- Turgot's letter to Graffigny, 1751, and his lectures on world history (p. 51).
- Rousseau's two Discourses, quoted (pp. 54-55).
- Ter Ellingson on the history of the phrase "noble savage" and on Gilbert Chinard's 1913 book (pp. 58-59).
- The claim that Rousseau "was clearly familiar" with Lahontan and the Relations (p. 55) is asserted; the authors note he does not cite them.

- The chapter relies on Jesuit and other European texts to recover indigenous views while criticising historians who distrust those texts. The defence offered is convergence across independent hostile sources.

## Open questions

- How much of the Dialogues is Kandiaronk and how much Lahontan cannot be settled from the internal evidence given; the authors' case is circumstantial.
- Was the indigenous critique a necessary cause of Enlightenment ideas of liberty, or one stimulus among several (classical republicanism, the Reformation, the English revolutions)? The chapter mentions classical and Christian precedents (p. 32) only to set them aside.
- The claim about the left's origins (p. 58) is made in a paragraph and not argued.
