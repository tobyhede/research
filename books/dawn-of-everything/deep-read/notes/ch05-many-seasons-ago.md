# ch05: Many Seasons Ago (pp. 127–160)

## Question this chapter answers

Why did the foraging societies of the Northwest Coast keep slaves and those of California not, and what does the answer show about how cultures come to differ and how domination begins?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anti-agricultural | p. 129 | Indigenous Californians knew of maize and of planting techniques and systematically refused to grow staple foods; not pre-agricultural. |
| Culture area | p. 131 | Early twentieth-century ethnology's regional cluster of societies sharing a mesh of traits; revived here as a political phenomenon. |
| Structures of refusal | p. 134 | Mauss's view of cultures: since foreign techniques are generally known, what defines a culture is what it declines to borrow. |
| Schismogenesis | p. 139 | Societies in contact become joined in a common system of differences while defining themselves against one another; Athens and Sparta are the classic case. |
| Protestant foragers | pp. 137-139 | Goldschmidt's comparison of the Yurok ethos (work, self-denial, individual moral responsibility, private property, money) to Weber's Puritans. The authors call it in many ways absurd but informative. |
| Fisher kings | pp. 139-141 | Northwest Coast aristocrats: hereditary title-holders in households of nobles, commoners and slaves, competing through potlatch. |
| Potlatch | p. 140 | Competitive feasts by nobles taking titles, with displays of excess and sometimes destruction of treasures and killing of slaves. |
| Slavery | p. 144 | Defined by the lack of social ties: no kin, no community, no capacity to make commitments; Patterson's "social death". |
| Capturing societies | p. 144 | Santos-Granero's term for Amerindian societies that fed off others by organised violence, taking slaves for their "vitality". |
| Caring labour | pp. 146-147 | The work of raising, feeding and tending people. Slave-raiding steals another society's investment of it and uses captives to add to the captors' own. |
| Optimal foraging theory | p. 149 | Behavioural ecology's cost-benefit modelling of foraging; used by others to explain the contrast as fish (front-loaded, raidable) against acorns (back-loaded). The authors reject it. |
| Shatter zone | p. 153 | Kroeber's term for northwest California, a compressed area of many small societies between the two culture areas. |
| Self-determination | p. 158 | The authors' emphasis: societies build and reproduce themselves partly by reflection and with reference to each other, not only under environmental or cultural compulsion. |
| Inequality from below | p. 160 | Domination appears first in the household; self-consciously egalitarian politics then arise to keep it from spreading into public life. |

## Leading propositions

- The world just before agriculture was one of villages, towns, sanctuaries and stored wealth, not roving bands (pp. 127-128).
- Pacific Coast foragers numbered in the hundreds of thousands or more and are routinely misdescribed as incipient farmers (p. 128).
- Californians grew tobacco and ritual plants, so they knew cultivation and rejected it for staples (p. 129).
- Cultural difference is not explained by language drift: northern Californian groups speaking unrelated languages resembled each other far more than they resembled distant speakers of related languages (pp. 130-131).
- For Mauss the question is not why traits spread but why some do not; cultures define themselves by refusal, and consciously (pp. 134-135).
- The formation of culture areas is therefore political: choices such as adopting agriculture reflect values about how humans should relate (p. 135).
- North of the Klamath River there were warrior aristocracies with chattel slaves; south of it there were not, and scholars have barely asked why (p. 136).
- Northwest California had private, alienable property and money used for purchases, with an ethos of thrift and work; the Northwest Coast had hereditary rank, unique heirloom treasures and competitive excess (pp. 137-140).
- Lumping both together as "complex hunter-gatherers" is as empty as calling a Texas oil executive and a medieval Egyptian poet "complex agriculturalists" (p. 141).
- Perhaps a quarter of the Northwest Coast population was enslaved, and the status was hereditary (pp. 142-143).
- Elements of the slavery complex (bulk fish harvesting, warfare, fortification, extreme disparities in burial) appear on the Northwest Coast from about 1850 BC; they are absent in California in the same periods (p. 143).
- In capturing societies, slavery works as a relation between societies and produces certain kinds of people (nobles, warriors) within the captor society (pp. 146-147).
- Violence turned into caring relations tends to endure (p. 147).
- The Chetco story of the Wogies and Yurok legend show that the rejection of slavery in the border zone had ethical and political dimensions (pp. 147-149).
- The ecological explanation fails because raids aimed at people, never food (pp. 151-152).
- Slavery arose on the Northwest Coast because nobles could not compel their own free commoners to do menial work at key seasons (p. 152).
- Californian shatter-zone societies inverted Northwest Coast values point by point: wood-carrying as sacred duty, sweating against grease feasts, interior discipline against masks and spectacle (pp. 154-155).
- Yurok customs such as compensation for every life taken in war removed the profit from raiding (p. 153).
- Most of central and southern California rejected slavery entirely, helped by burning a person's wealth at death (p. 156).
- Both environmental determinism and cultural determinism assume we are already stuck (pp. 157-158).
- How much human agency matters cannot be known; where to set the dial between freedom and determinism is largely a matter of taste, and the authors set it towards freedom (p. 158).
- Domination begins at home: slavery starts in war but is first a domestic institution (p. 159).
- Hierarchy and equality tend to emerge together, as complements (p. 160).

## Arguments

- **Culture as refusal.**
  **Premises**: (p. 134) people in the past travelled widely and knew their neighbours' techniques; (p. 134) Athabascans refused Inuit kayaks and Inuit refused Athabascan snowshoes despite the practical advantage; (p. 135) Mauss cites explicit court debate in China over foreign dress.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 135) societies define themselves by what they refuse to borrow, and they reflect on what borrowing would say about them. Mauss, the authors say, overstated his case (p. 134).

- **Against the fish-and-acorns explanation.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 150-151) the theory: stored fish invite raiders, acorns do not, so fishers become warlike; (p. 151) but capturing food was never an aim of Northwest Coast raids, since little can be carried in a canoe or overland; (pp. 151-152) raids aimed at people, in one of the most densely populated parts of North America; (p. 150) Californians had access to fish and chose acorns anyway, which the theory treats as irrational.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 152) ecology does not explain slavery on the Northwest Coast.

- **Freedom as the cause of slavery.**
  **Premises**: (p. 152) there was no shortage of hands, but title-holders would not do menial work; (p. 152) commoners could defect to another house if pressed and sometimes killed overbearing chiefs; (p. 152) fish runs need large amounts of processing labour in a short season.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 152) nobles lacked controllable labour at key times and went abroad for it. My observation: this explains demand for slaves given an aristocracy that disdains work. It does not explain where the aristocracy and its disdain came from; the authors point to political jockeying over the proper ordering of society (p. 152) and leave it there.

- **Schismogenesis on the Pacific Coast.**
  **Premises**: (p. 143) archaeology shows continuous contact and movement along the coast, including captives; (p. 153) linguistic and other evidence suggests groups moved south from the Northwest Coast into the shatter zone; (pp. 153-154) Yurok custom and Chetco story show awareness and disapproval of northern practice; (pp. 154-155) Californian ritual and aesthetics invert northern ones.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 159) the two culture areas defined themselves against each other, and California's rejection of slavery was part of that. My observation: the inversions are documented ethnographically, mostly from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries; that the inversion was deliberate and caused by reaction to the north is an inference from pattern, plus two stories. The authors say the "when" is a matter for future research (p. 153) and that the Yurok are unusual even for California (p. 156).

- **Domination begins at home.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 145-147) capture is assimilated to hunting and captives to pets being domesticated; slaves are held at the midpoint of a process that normally ends in adoption; (pp. 159-160) Northwest Coast societies had no overarching polity, only great houses with their own nobles, commoners and slaves.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 159) the most brutal exploitation originates as a perversion of nurture and care, in the household, not in government.

## Evidence offered

- Goldschmidt on the Yurok and neighbours (pp. 136-139, 154); Kroeber on the shatter zone (p. 153); an ethnographer on the Atsugewi (p. 155); a contemporary ethnographer on Yurok spiritual training (pp. 155-156). Ethnographic, twentieth century.
- Boas and others on Kwakiutl and Northwest Coast rank, potlatch and slavery; late eighteenth-century European accounts estimating a quarter of the population enslaved (pp. 140-143). Ethnographic and textual.
- Middle Pacific period archaeology (1850 BC to AD 200): burials, fortifications, Salish Sea defences by 1500 BC (pp. 143-144). Archaeological. The authors say slavery is very hard to find archaeologically.
- Santos-Granero on capturing societies: Northwest Amazonia, the Guaicurú of the Paraguay basin, the Calusa; a German observer of 1548 (pp. 144-146). Ethno-historical.
- The Chetco "Wogies" story, recorded by A. W. Chase in 1873 from survivors on the Siletz Reservation (pp. 147-148). Oral tradition at one remove.
- The Yurok legend of Le'mekwelolmei (p. 149). Oral tradition.
- Accounts of a Nootka chief murdered for taking his fishermen's whole catch (p. 152). Ethnographic anecdote.
- Mauss's essays on civilization, 1910 to 1930 (pp. 133-135). Theoretical source.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Answers the question left open in ch04 (p. 98) about why people differentiate themselves, using schismogenesis from ch02 (pp. 48-49).
- Applies ch04's rejection of subsistence categories (ch04 p. 120) to a worked case.
- "Domination begins at home" and the link between care and violence return in ch12's discussion of patriarchal households and sovereignty.
- The refusal of agriculture here prepares ch06's argument that Neolithic peoples also held farming at arm's length.
- The chapter grants that environment and technology often make "a huge difference" (p. 157) and that agency cannot be measured (p. 158), then declares its preference for freedom a matter of taste. This is the book's most explicit statement of its own method, and it concedes that the choice is not forced by the evidence.
- The Yurok are both the model of the Californian inversion and, by the authors' account, atypical of California: they held some slaves, inherited wealth, fought more, and had debt peons (p. 156). The clean opposition is strongest exactly where the Californian case is least pure.
- The chapter treats private property and money in California as part of an anti-slavery, egalitarian ethos (p. 160), which sits in interesting contrast with ch02, where money and property are what Kandiaronk blames for European unfreedom.

## Open questions

- When did shatter-zone societies give up slavery, if their ancestors had it? Unknown (p. 153).
- Were Mesolithic societies elsewhere organised like the Northwest Coast? The authors answer "Nothing for certain" (p. 159).
- Is the opposition between the two areas older than European contact in the form described, given that the ethnography postdates the fur trade and the Gold Rush (p. 135)?
