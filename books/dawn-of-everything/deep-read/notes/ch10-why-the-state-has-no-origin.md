# ch10: Why the State Has No Origin (pp. 267–330)

Scope note: the chapter heading falls on p. 267 and the text ends on p. 330, where chapter 11 begins. This is the longest chapter. Endnotes are not in this workspace's `source.txt`.

## Question this chapter answers

What is "the state", does it have an origin, and what did the kingdoms and empires usually called early states actually have in common?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The state (received definitions) | pp. 268-269 | Von Ihering's (later Weber's) monopoly of legitimate coercive force in a territory; the Marxist apparatus protecting a ruling class; the functionalist apparatus required by social complexity. The authors find each either too narrow or circular. |
| Three primary freedoms | p. 270 | The freedom to move, the freedom to disobey orders, the freedom to reorganise social relations (restated p. 320 as to move, to disobey, to create or transform social relationships). |
| Three elementary forms of domination | pp. 270-272 | Control of violence, control of information, and individual charisma: the three possible bases of social power, derived from a thought experiment about what secures property. |
| Sovereignty | p. 273 | The institutional form of control over violence: power above the law, the right to use violence with impunity. Intensely personal and hard to delegate (p. 294). |
| Bureaucracy / administration | p. 273 | The institutional form of control over information, including official secrets. |
| Competitive political field / heroic politics | p. 274 | The institutional form of charisma: contests among larger-than-life individuals. Modern elections descend from aristocratic contests, not from assembly democracy. |
| First-order regime | p. 292 | A polity organised around one of the three forms to the relative neglect of the others: Olmec (charismatic competition), Chavín (esoteric knowledge), Natchez and Shilluk (sovereignty). |
| Second-order regime | p. 310 | A polity that fuses two of the three, with the third displaced on to the cosmos: Old Kingdom Egypt and the Inca (sovereignty and administration), Early Dynastic Mesopotamia (administration and heroic politics), Classic Maya (heroic politics and sovereignty). |
| Theatre state | p. 288 | Geertz's term; applied seasonally to the Olmec, where organised power existed only in periodic spectacle. |
| Divine kingship | pp. 294-296 | Sovereignty as a break with the moral order; the king's arbitrary violence proves his transcendence, and his subjects respond by hedging him with ritual restrictions. |
| Patrimonial system | p. 301 | Weber's term: all subjects imagined as members of the royal household, working to care for the king. |
| Retainer burials | pp. 299-300 | Mass killing around royal tombs in the first generations of new kingdoms (Egypt's First Dynasty, Ur, Kerma, Shang China, Cahokia). |
| Caring labour | p. 306 | Work that attends to the particular needs of the one cared for; the state diverts it on to abstractions (the king, the dead, the nation). |
| Intermediate / dark ages | pp. 283-284 | Periods without a single ruler, written off by a chronology modelled on nineteenth-century nation states. |
| Village bureaucracy | p. 315 | Administrative devices (tokens, seals, archived stoppers) in small egalitarian settlements such as Tell Sabi Abyad, about 6200 BC. |
| Ayllu | pp. 278, 317 | Andean village associations with their own khipu accounting, redistributing land and labour on a principle of equality. |
| Ama(r)-gi | p. 320 | Sumerian for freedom, literally "return to mother", from decrees cancelling debts. |
| Civilization | p. 324 | From civilis: the political wisdom and mutual aid that let societies organise by voluntary coalition; not cities plus states. |

## Leading propositions

- There is no agreed definition of the state that covers the usual cases without being meaningless (p. 268).
- Monarchs, aristocracies, slavery and patriarchy existed without states, and irrigation, science and philosophy existed without them too, so classifying a polity as a state teaches little (p. 270).
- Landed property is a legal understanding kept up by morality and the threat of violence, not a material thing (p. 271).
- Social power has three possible bases: control of violence, control of information, and charisma (p. 272).
- The modern state is a combination of sovereignty, bureaucracy and a competitive political field, three things with separate origins and no necessary connection (p. 274).
- Modern states are an amalgam that came together at a certain point and may be coming apart again (pp. 275, 324).
- Different sequences are possible (administration then charismatic politics then sovereignty in Mesopotamia; charismatic vision then sovereignty then administration elsewhere); the error is the teleology that assumes one end point (pp. 275-276).
- The Aztec empire was a confederation of noble families under a first speaker; the Inca concentrated all authority in the body of the Sapa Inca, which made delegation hard (pp. 278-279).
- Both empires were easy to conquer because they had capitals and kings to seize and subjects schooled in obedience; the decentralised Maya resisted for centuries (pp. 280-281).
- Terms such as "Post-Classic" and "Intermediate" build a political bias into chronology, treating periods of relative freedom as mere gaps (p. 283).
- Egypt's Old, Middle and New Kingdoms are a late nineteenth-century scheme modelled by Prussian scholars on the nation state (p. 284).
- For most of the last 5,000 years, kingdoms and empires were islands surrounded by larger areas whose people avoided fixed overarching authority (p. 285).
- The Olmec built influence on spectacle and competitive ball games, Chavín on images tied to esoteric knowledge and visions; neither had military or administrative control of a hinterland (pp. 288-291).
- The Natchez Great Sun had absolute power in his presence and almost none beyond it; his subjects moved away from him (pp. 293-294).
- Sovereign power extends only as far as the king can walk, reach or see, unless an administration carries it further (p. 294).
- First-order regimes had clear elites but probably did little to restrict the three freedoms (p. 296).
- The Shilluk preferred an occasional arbitrary king to any systematic rule and resisted every attempt at administration (pp. 297-298).
- Mass killing at royal funerals marks the first generations of new kingdoms and then fades (p. 299).
- When sovereignty becomes a society's organising principle, it does so by turning violence into kinship (p. 301).
- In Egypt, the demand to feed dead kings bread and beer, from about 3500 BC, drove the intensification of wheat farming and created what may be the world's first peasantry, with debt and class dependence (p. 304).
- The pyramids were built by seasonal work gangs organised like boat crews, well fed, in something like the first production line (pp. 305-306).
- The so-called early states differ fundamentally from one another; what they share (spectacular violence at the top, mimicry of the patriarchal household, class division) also existed without central government (pp. 307-308).
- When Egypt's central authority broke down, heroic politics returned in a milder form, with local leaders claiming authority through care of the people (pp. 312-313).
- Bureaucracy did not begin as a response to scale; the first administrative techniques appear in tiny villages and in ritual contexts (pp. 314-315).
- Administrative tools may first have been designed to prevent accumulation and keep people equal (p. 317).
- Sovereignty joined to administration is a potentially lethal combination: impersonal equivalence becomes a means of tyranny (p. 318).
- Most early regimes were not staffed by full-time specialists and had no standing armies; government was largely seasonal (p. 321).
- "The state", if it means anything, is the totalitarian impulse behind rulers' cosmic claims, the wish to make the ritual last for ever (pp. 322-323).
- What has passed for "civilization" may be a male appropriation of knowledge systems that had women at their centre (p. 325).
- Minoan Crete shows palaces, writing and cities with no clear evidence of monarchy and with women as the figures of authority (pp. 326-328).

## Arguments

- **The three bases of power (thought experiment).**
  **Premises**: (pp. 271-272) a diamond necklace is secured first by the threat of force; (p. 272) if no one could be harmed, it could still be kept by secrecy; (p. 272) if no one could keep a secret either, it could be kept only by convincing everyone its owner deserves it.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 272) violence, information and charisma are the three possible bases of social power. My observation: the experiment shows these three are sufficient to secure exclusive possession in the imagined case; that they are exhaustive is asserted. Economic dependence or control of subsistence, the usual fourth candidate, is treated as reducible to the threat of violence (p. 271).

- **The state as amalgam, not stage.**
  **Premises**: (p. 274) the three institutional forms have separate origins, as Mesopotamia shows; (pp. 274-275) sovereignty existed without administration (Natchez, Shilluk); (pp. 288-291) spectacle and esoteric knowledge organised large populations without sovereignty (Olmec, Chavín); (pp. 314-317) administration existed in egalitarian villages without sovereignty; (pp. 307-310) the canonical early states combine different pairs.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 323) what we call states are a confluence of three forms with different origins, and nothing made their modern combination inevitable.

- **Why Egypt looks like the model state.**
  **Premises**: (p. 303) before the First Dynasty the Nile had many petty kings making cosmic claims without administrative control; (p. 300) First Dynasty kings were buried with their own courtiers; (p. 301) the killing fused servants and relatives into one household; (pp. 304-305) estates for feeding dead kings extended royal reach and created dependent cultivators; (pp. 305-306) corvée gangs built the pyramids.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 302, 312) Egypt is where a Natchez-like sovereignty broke out of its ritual cage through the cult of the dead king and armed itself with administration; with the later Inca it is almost the only early case that fits what theory says should happen (p. 314). The authors mark parts of the reconstruction as guesswork: "we can really only guess at the details" (p. 305).

- **Bureaucracy before scale.**
  **Premises**: (p. 315) Tell Sabi Abyad, a village of perhaps 150 people around 6200 BC, had central storage, tokens, seals and an archive of stoppers; (pp. 315-316) its houses are uniform, with no rich residence or burials; (pp. 316-317) in the 'Ubaid period that followed, villages over a wide region became standardised and outward signs of rank disappeared; (p. 317) Andean ayllu used khipu to redistribute land and cancel debts each year.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 317) administrative tools were perhaps first devised to maintain equality, and bureaucracy did not arise to manage large populations. The authors write "it is possible that we are witnessing the birth of an overt ideology of equality".

- **How equality becomes domination.**
  **Premises**: (p. 318) accounting creates precise equivalences; (p. 318) under the Inca, khipu records of labour owed to the centre were fixed and never retied, and households were grouped in decimal units regardless of local ties; (p. 319) uniform quotas guarantee that some will fail and become peons; (p. 319) local councils can adjust an unreasonable result, an enforcer with sovereign backing need not.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 319-320) the most violent inequalities arise from fictions of legal equality once sovereign power is added; promises turn into peonage when they become impersonal and transferable.

- **Chronology as politics.**
  **Premises**: (p. 283) Kroeber found no laws of cultural growth and decay; (pp. 283-284) "intermediate" periods cover about a third of ancient Egyptian history and include innovations such as the god's wives of Amun; (p. 284) the tripartite scheme dates from the late nineteenth century and Bismarck's Germany; (p. 285) the Middle Kingdom could as well be described by its forced labour and plunder.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 283, 313) periods without strong central rule are dismissed because nobody was building in stone, though significant political achievements occur in them.

- **Minoan Crete.**
  **Premises**: (p. 326) no clear evidence of monarchy despite abundant art; (p. 326) authority figures in the art are women, shown larger than men, holding staffs, enthroned, in assemblies; (p. 327) imports before the palaces come from Egyptian women's cults; (p. 328) the Knossos "Throne Room" has benches arranged for an assembly and a nearby basin linked at Akrotiri to female initiation.
  **Conclusion**: the evidence suggests female political rule, "effectively a theocracy of some sort, governed by a college of priestesses" (p. 328). My observation: this is the same kind of inference from imagery the authors treat cautiously elsewhere (figurines in ch06). They justify it by asking whether equivalent male imagery would be read as non-political (p. 327). Linear A is undeciphered, so the reading cannot be tested against texts.

## Evidence offered

- Definitions from Bodin, von Ihering, Weber, Marxist and functionalist theory (pp. 268-269). Textual.
- Aztec and Inca: Spanish chronicles including Cieza de León (1553) and Polo de Ondegardo; khipu; mit'a; Brides of the Sun; royal mummies (pp. 276-280). Textual, colonial.
- Maya: Classic inscriptions, the ninth-century collapse, Chichén Itzá's assembly halls, the books of Chilam Balam, revolts from 1546 to the Zapatistas (pp. 281-282). Textual and archaeological.
- Olmec: San Lorenzo and La Venta, colossal heads in ball-players' helmets; later Maya and Aztec ball games, the Popol Vuh, the Yaxchilán staircase of AD 752 (pp. 286-288). Archaeological, with later textual analogy.
- Chavín de Huántar (1000 to 200 BC): labyrinths, El Lanzón, snuff equipment, San Pedro cactus and vilca imagery, seventeenth-century testimony that it was an oracle (pp. 289-291). Archaeological and textual.
- Natchez: Le Petit and Charlevoix, early eighteenth century; survey of the Natchez Bluffs showing semi-autonomous districts (pp. 293-294). Textual and archaeological.
- Shilluk: an Italian missionary's account, a Shilluk legend, ethnography of the reth (pp. 297-298). Ethnographic.
- Royal retainer burials: Abydos First Dynasty tombs with titled gravestones, Ur, Kerma, Anyang, Cahokia, Moche, Wari (pp. 299-300). Archaeological.
- Predynastic Egypt: petty royal burials at Naqada, Abydos, Hierakonpolis and Qustul by 3500 BC; bakeries and breweries by cemeteries; bread moulds and gang graffiti at Giza; tomb of Ty; tomb biographies; Ankhtifi's inscription at El-Mo'alla (pp. 303-305, 311-313). Archaeological and textual.
- Shang China: oracle bones, divination procedure, mutilated captives at Anyang (pp. 299-300, 308-309). Archaeological and textual.
- Tell Sabi Abyad and 'Ubaid village archaeology, with the statement that one author excavated such villages in Iraqi Kurdistan (pp. 315-317). Archaeological, first-hand in part.
- Fijian sunrise ritual recorded by Lyth, 1852 (p. 322). Ethnographic.
- Philip Abrams on the state as mask (p. 323). Theoretical source.
- Minoan frescoes, seals, the Knossos Throne Room, Akrotiri, imports from Egypt; Mycenaean megaron at Pylos and Linear B (pp. 326-328). Archaeological.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Completes the sequence of "no origin" arguments: inequality (chs. 1-2), agriculture (chs. 6-7), cities and rulers (chs. 8-9), now the state.
- The three freedoms were first listed in ch04 (p. 105); here they are paired with three forms of domination, and ch12 uses the pairing to state the book's conclusion.
- Heroic societies and Uruk (ch08 pp. 231-235) supply the Mesopotamian sequence on p. 275.
- The Natchez Sun (ch04 pp. 122-123), play kings and seasonality (ch03 p. 94), caring labour and capture (ch05 pp. 146-147), and the property-as-sacred argument (ch04 p. 124) are all drawn together.
- "Play kingdoms" becoming serious (p. 322) parallels play farming (chs. 6-7); the authors suggest both may be driven by the rise of patriarchy, a question they leave open and return to in ch12.
- The chapter says it is "not truly radical", since it tells the old story of kingdoms and empires while dropping the teleology (p. 285).
- The typology risks the circularity the authors charge against "complex chiefdom": each case is assigned to the form it emphasises, and the missing form is said to have been displaced to the cosmos (p. 310), which makes the scheme hard to falsify. The authors do claim it has limits, saying the range of possibilities is "far from limitless" (pp. 329-330).
- The Inca are treated as extractive (p. 318) against the Enlightenment image of ch02; the Aztecs as a capturing society (p. 277), linking to ch05.
- The chapter concedes that Egypt does fit the conventional model (p. 314). The argument is that it is exceptional, not that the model fits nothing.

## Open questions

- Why did sovereignty and administration fuse in Egypt and Peru and not elsewhere? The answer offered for Egypt (the hunger of dead kings) describes a mechanism; why that idea "gained traction" is said to be unknown ("For whatever reasons", p. 304).
- Did first-order regimes really leave the three freedoms intact? The authors say it is "not at all clear" (p. 296).
- Is the decline of women's power a cause or a consequence of sovereignty's expansion (p. 322)?
- Were the Minoan palaces "even a regime of domination at all" (p. 330)?
