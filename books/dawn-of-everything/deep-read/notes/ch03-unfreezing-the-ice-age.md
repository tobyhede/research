# ch03: Unfreezing the Ice Age (pp. 64–95)

Scope note: the chapter heading falls on p. 64 and the text ends near the top of p. 95, where chapter 4 begins. Endnotes are not in this workspace's `source.txt`.

## Question this chapter answers

What does the evidence from the Upper Palaeolithic show about early human politics, and if it shows neither primordial equality nor primordial hierarchy, what is the right question to ask?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sapient paradox | p. 69 | The supposed gap of tens of thousands of years between biologically modern humans and the appearance of complex culture. The authors call it a mirage produced by where archaeologists have dug. |
| Upper Palaeolithic | pp. 69-70 | Roughly 50,000 to 15,000 BC. |
| Actuarial intelligence | p. 70 | Boehm's term: foragers understand what their society would look like if they did things differently, and act to prevent it. |
| Politics | p. 70 | Via Boehm: the ability to reflect consciously on the directions one's society could take and to argue for one over another. |
| Princely burials | p. 71 | Rich, isolated Ice Age burials (Sunghir, Dolní Věstonice, Liguria's Il Principe, Saint-Germain-de-la-Rivière). Read by the authors as the burial of anomalous individuals, not evidence of hereditary elites. |
| Monumentality | p. 74 | A relative concept: a structure is monumental in comparison with what its viewers have otherwise seen. |
| Extreme individuals | pp. 79, 83 | People physically or behaviourally anomalous, treated as exalted and perhaps dangerous; the Nuer prophets are the ethnographic model. |
| Double morphology | p. 86 | Mauss's term for societies with two social structures, one for each season, with two systems of law and religion. |
| Seasonal dualism / seasonality | pp. 84, 89 | Regular alternation between different forms of social organisation over the year. The only consistent feature across cases is the alternation itself (pp. 91-92). |
| Buffalo police | p. 87 | Lowie's Plains Indian "soldiers": a police force with full coercive power during the hunt and Sun Dance season, dissolved afterwards and rotated between clans or clubs. |
| Societies against the state | p. 90 | Clastres's thesis that stateless peoples consciously organised to prevent arbitrary power emerging. |
| Getting stuck | p. 92 | The authors' replacement for the origins question: how humans ended up in a single mode of social organisation and lost the habit of moving between them. |
| Play kings | p. 94 | Temporary or ritual rulers; the authors suggest the first kings may have been of this kind. |

## Leading propositions

- Accounts of human origins do for us what myths did for others; the gaps in the evidence invite stories that reflect our own concerns (pp. 64-65).
- Early human populations in Africa were far more physically diverse than living humans, and probably more socially diverse still; there is no original form of human society (pp. 66-67).
- The "Upper Palaeolithic Revolution" around 45,000 years ago is most likely an illusion created by the bias of research towards Europe (pp. 68-69).
- The cultural burst in Ice Age Europe may reflect climate and demography: denser seasonal interaction in confined environments (p. 69).
- Boehm is right that what is distinctively human is the conscious decision not to act on dominance instincts, but he then wrongly assumes everyone chose the same egalitarian arrangement for thousands of generations (pp. 70-71).
- Rich burials and monumental building existed among hunter-gatherers tens of thousands of years before farming (pp. 71-74).
- That evidence is sporadic, and it comes without fortifications, storehouses or palaces, so it does not show ranked societies either (p. 74).
- Modern writers still compare forager bands to apes and not to other humans, which denies them political consciousness; Harari is the example (p. 75).
- Human thought is inherently dialogic, and political consciousness comes before individual self-consciousness (p. 76).
- Sceptics and nonconformists exist in every society; what varies is how they are treated, and in some societies the odd are a reserve of leadership in crisis (pp. 78-79).
- Lévi-Strauss's Nambikwara chiefs moved each year between two social systems and were self-conscious political actors (pp. 80-81).
- Post-war anthropology (the 1966 Man the Hunter symposium) fixed the image of foragers as small egalitarian bands, on the basis of African groups who were in some cases refugee populations (pp. 81-82).
- A majority of the rich Upper Palaeolithic burials are of people with striking physical anomalies (p. 82).
- Most Palaeolithic dead were not buried at all, so the rich burials cannot stand as proxies for social structure among the living (p. 83).
- Almost all Ice Age sites with rich burials and monuments were made by societies that gathered and dispersed seasonally (p. 84).
- Seasonal alternation continued after farming: the builders of Stonehenge had given up cereal cultivation around 3300 BC while keeping livestock (pp. 84-85).
- Plains Indians knew state-like coercive power without ever developing a state, and took care to dismantle and rotate it (pp. 87-88).
- A sequence of stages from band to state is incoherent if the starting groups move between those forms as a matter of habit (pp. 88-89).
- Clastres was right about political self-consciousness but left out seasonality, which would have answered the objection that people cannot organise against something they have never experienced (pp. 90-91).
- The real question is how we got stuck (p. 92).
- Seasonal festivals and carnival kept political self-consciousness alive and served as laboratories of social possibility (pp. 93-94).
- What makes us human is the capacity to negotiate between alternatives such as hierarchy and equality (p. 94).

## Arguments

- **Against reading the burials as hereditary elites.**
  **Premises**: (p. 82) most of the richly buried skeletons show anomalies: congenital deformities at Sunghir and Dolní Věstonice, dwarfism at Romito, great height at Grimaldi; (p. 83) an elite made up largely of such people is extremely unlikely; (p. 83) most contemporaries were not buried at all, and whole clothed corpses were exceptional; (p. 83) many were weighted down, as if dangerous; (p. 74) no fortifications, stores or palaces accompany them.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 83) hereditary aristocracy is the least likely reading; these were extraordinary individuals. The authors say plainly that "Much here is speculation" (p. 83).

- **Seasonality as the explanation.**
  **Premises**: (p. 84) the big Upper Palaeolithic sites are tied to seasonal game migrations, fish runs and nut harvests; (p. 84) activity at Göbekli Tepe matches the midsummer-to-autumn gazelle season, and enclosures seem to have been short-lived and then filled in; (pp. 86-87) ethnography (Inuit, Kwakiutl, Plains nations, Nambikwara) shows societies changing structure with the seasons, including temporary coercive authority; (unstated) Ice Age societies with the same seasonal rhythm probably did likewise.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 89) Ice Age people shifted between social arrangements, raising and dismantling hierarchies, and the "princes" may have reigned only for a season. My observation: the archaeological premises establish seasonal gathering; that political structure alternated with it is carried by ethnographic analogy, which the authors elsewhere (p. 82) say cannot give a direct window on the past.

- **From flexibility to self-consciousness.**
  **Premises**: (p. 85) for Lévi-Strauss, living under two structures let chiefs see their arrangements at one remove; (p. 88) Lowie's Plains Indians were aware of the dangers of authority and rotated it; (p. 89) the same individual could live in something like a band, a tribe and a state within a year.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 89) institutional flexibility brings the capacity to step outside any one structure and to make and unmake political worlds.

- **Reductio of the stage sequence.**
  **Premises**: (p. 88) states are defined by a monopoly of legitimate coercive force; (p. 88) on that definition the Cheyenne or Lakota became a state each hunting season and reverted afterwards; (p. 88) that is silly.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 88-89) the assumption that societies progress through evolutionary stages is what is at fault.

- **Against Boehm's uniform egalitarian past.**
  **Premises**: (p. 70) humans are self-conscious political actors able to choose among arrangements; (p. 71) Boehm nonetheless holds that all humans were egalitarian until about 12,000 years ago.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 71) this implies that for some 200,000 years nothing happened, which sits badly with the evidence of Palaeolithic politics.

## Evidence offered

- Genetics and fossil evidence for diverse, long-isolated African populations and interbreeding with Neanderthals and Denisovans (pp. 66-67). Reported as the emerging scientific consensus.
- Early symbolic behaviour: South African rock shelters around 80,000 BC, Panga ya Saidi in Kenya (60,000 years), cave art in Borneo and Sulawesi (pp. 68-69). Archaeological.
- Burials: Sunghir and Dolní Věstonice (34,000 to 26,000 years ago), the Ligurian caves and Il Principe, the Lady of Saint-Germain-de-la-Rivière (16,000 years old; deer teeth from 190 miles away); an estimate of 10,000 work hours for the Sunghir beads (pp. 71-72). Archaeological.
- Göbekli Tepe: twenty megalithic enclosures from around 9000 BC, at least 200 pillars, built by people who were not farmers (pp. 72-73). Archaeological; the authors note open debates about planning and roofing.
- Mammoth-bone structures from Kraków to Kiev, 25,000 to 12,000 years ago; Yudinovo, Mezhirich, Kostenki (pp. 73-74). Archaeological, with the monument interpretation credited to research at Yudinovo.
- Stonehenge and Durrington Walls: hazelnut collection replacing cereals from around 3300 BC, winter feasting, bluestones from Wales (pp. 84-85). Archaeological.
- Ethnography: Boehm on levelling tactics (p. 70); Radin on the Winnebago (p. 78); Beidelman on Nuer prophets (pp. 78-79); Lévi-Strauss on the Nambikwara, 1944 (pp. 79-81); Mauss and Beuchat on the Inuit, 1903 (p. 86); Boas on the Kwakiutl (pp. 86-87); Lowie on Plains police (pp. 87-88); Clastres (pp. 89-90). All twentieth-century or late nineteenth-century observations, used as sources of hypotheses.
- Mauss's estimate that physical conditions explain at most 40 per cent of Inuit arrangements (p. 86) is reported without the basis for the figure.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Supplies the evidence for ch01's claim that foragers were not confined to small bands (ch01 p. 9), and states the limits of Clastres promised in ch02 (ch02 p. 61).
- Göbekli Tepe's builders are said to differ from nearby early farmers, with the explanation deferred (p. 73); ch06 takes it up.
- "How did we get stuck?" (p. 92) is the question chs. 4 to 10 work on and ch12 answers.
- "Play kings" (p. 94) links to ch10's treatment of sovereignty and ritual.
- The chapter says ethnographic cases cannot be windows on the past (p. 82) and then relies on them to interpret the Palaeolithic. The stated defence, repeated in ch04 (p. 96), is that they show what is possible.
- The chapter rejects any original form of society (p. 67) but ends by characterising "most of the last 40,000 or so years" (p. 89) as one of flexible alternation, which is itself a general claim about early society.

## Open questions

- How many rich burials are there in total, and what proportion show anomalies? The text says "a majority" (p. 82) without numbers.
- Does seasonal gathering at a site show seasonal change in political order, or only seasonal change in group size?
- Were the "princes" leaders at all? The authors leave it open ("If they reigned at all", p. 89).
