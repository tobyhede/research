# ch09: Hiding in Plain Sight (pp. 246–267)

Scope note: the chapter heading falls on p. 246 and the text ends on p. 267, where chapter 10 begins. One figure (p. 253) is not in the text. Endnotes are not in this workspace's `source.txt`.

## Question this chapter answers

Were there cities in the Americas that governed themselves without kings, and is there an indigenous American tradition of urban republics and democracy that historians have overlooked?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Teotihuacan | pp. 246-247 | City in the Valley of Mexico, founded about 100 BC, declining about AD 600, with a population conservatively put at around 100,000. Named and mythologised later by the Aztecs. |
| Standard package of Mesoamerican kingship | p. 248 | Royal temples, ball-courts, images of war and humiliated captives, calendrical rites for royal ancestors and records of kings' deeds, as among the Classic Maya. |
| Stranger-kings | pp. 250-251 | Outsiders from far away who are made rulers; the authors' explanation of Teotihuacan-style lords shown on Maya thrones. |
| Tollan | pp. 246, 249 | "Place of cattail reeds", the name of a primordial perfect city; Teotihuacan as "a Tollan for the people". |
| Social housing | p. 255 | Millon's reading of Teotihuacan's stone apartment compounds, built on a grid for nearly the whole population after about AD 300. |
| Three-temple complexes | p. 256 | District shrines, at least twenty across the city, which the authors suggest are traces of neighbourhood assemblies. |
| Collective governance | p. 249 | Scholars' term for Teotihuacan's arrangement after its reversal around AD 300. |
| Tlaxcala | p. 258 | City-state in the Valley of Puebla that resisted the Aztec Triple Alliance and allied with Cortés in 1519; described by the authors as an indigenous republic and democracy. |
| Teuctli | p. 265 | Elected officials forming Tlaxcala's council, answerable to the citizenry. |
| Chichimec | p. 266 | The ascetic desert hunter-gatherers from whom Nahuatl speakers claimed descent; the source of the qualities Tlaxcalteca councillors were trained to cultivate. |
| Hiding in plain sight | p. 262 | Said of Cervantes de Salazar's Crónica, unpublished until 1914 and little read since, and by extension of the indigenous political history it records. |

## Leading propositions

- Teotihuacan at its height governed itself without overlords, on a larger scale than any Eurasian case in ch08 and without their technology (p. 247).
- Among thousands of Teotihuacan images there is not one of a ruler striking, binding or dominating a subordinate; there are no ball-courts and no royal tombs despite determined search (p. 248).
- Following Pasztory, Teotihuacan's art deliberately inverted the conventions of its Maya and Zapotec contemporaries: schismogenesis between urban civilisations (pp. 248-249).
- The Teotihuacan-style lords on Maya monuments are best explained as adventurers who became stranger-kings abroad; they say more about Maya ideas of kingship than about Teotihuacan (pp. 250-252).
- Early Teotihuacan (to about AD 200) built pyramids with hundreds of human sacrifices and looked set on the path to aristocratic rule (pp. 253-254).
- Around AD 300 the Temple of the Feathered Serpent was desecrated and hidden, pyramid-building and ritual killing stopped, and resources went into high-quality housing for most of the population (pp. 254-255).
- Most of the city's roughly 100,000 residents came to live in comfortable stone apartments; few were deprived (pp. 255-256).
- Authority was probably distributed among local assemblies, perhaps answerable to a governing council; a top-down system can probably be ruled out (p. 256).
- Tensions among ethnically diverse groups persisted, and around AD 550 the city came apart from within (p. 257).
- Tlaxcala had no kings; Cortés compared its government to Venice, Genoa and Pisa (pp. 258-259).
- Fewer than 1,000 Spaniards could not have taken Tenochtitlan without indigenous allies, including some 20,000 Tlaxcalteca, so how Tlaxcala decided matters for world history (p. 259).
- Tlaxcala's council reached its decision on Cortés through lengthy reasoned debate and a search for consensus, as recorded by Cervantes de Salazar (pp. 263-264).
- Historians treat such accounts as literature or as Indians adapting to European expectations, although educated European opinion at the time was anti-democratic (pp. 264-265).
- Aspirants to Tlaxcala's council were subjected to public abuse, seclusion, fasting and moral instruction to subordinate them to the people (pp. 265-266).
- Archaeology confirms that Tlaxcala, by the fourteenth century, had no palace, central temple or major ball-court, but uniformly good housing around more than twenty district plazas (p. 267).
- Tlaxcala's politics belong to a tradition traceable to Teotihuacan; it was the Aztecs who broke with it (p. 267).

## Arguments

- **Teotihuacan without rulers.**
  **Premises**: (p. 247) kings leave unmistakable traces: palaces, rich burials, conquest monuments; (p. 248) at Teotihuacan these are absent from the art, the architecture and the tunnels under the pyramids; (p. 248) artists made figures the same size; (p. 249) those who know the site best have reached a general consensus that it was organised on self-consciously egalitarian lines.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 247, 249) the city found a way to govern itself without overlords. My observation: as in ch08 the core of the argument is absence of royal markers, here reinforced by an appeal to specialist consensus. The text also records that the specialists' usual first reaction is that the place is "weird" (p. 247).

- **The reversal around AD 300.**
  **Premises**: (p. 254) each early building phase came with ritual killing; (pp. 254-255) the Feathered Serpent temple was burned, defaced and masked by a new platform; (p. 255) no further pyramids or sacrifices; (p. 255) apartment construction continued until most residents were well housed.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 254, 256) the citizens "chose a different path", away from monarchy and aristocracy. The authors say "Without written sources, we can’t really say why" and that the sequence cannot yet be fixed with confidence (p. 254); grid housing may have begun a century earlier (p. 255). "Possibly there was a revolution of sorts" (p. 249) is others' suggestion.

- **Stranger-kings.**
  **Premises**: (p. 250) Maya monuments show enthroned figures in Teotihuacan dress, and inscriptions say some came from the Land of the Cattail Reeds; (p. 250) a military conquest over 600 miles is thought logistically impossible; (p. 251) dynastic origin stories worldwide feature men arriving from afar; (p. 251) skeletal study at Copán shows the founders led violent, mobile lives and may not have come from Teotihuacan; (pp. 251-252) the later Itzá and Xiu are a parallel.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 251) the Maya dynasts were probably traders, soldiers of fortune or the like who were elevated abroad. Flagged by the authors: "All this is only guesswork" (p. 252).

- **Tlaxcala as a democracy.**
  **Premises**: (p. 259) Cortés, experienced at finding kings, found none and reported a republic; (pp. 263-264) Cervantes de Salazar gives detailed council debates (Maxixcatzin, Xicotencatl the Elder, Temilotecutl's compromise); (p. 265) Motolinía independently reports a council of elected officials and their training; (p. 264) the Tlaxcalan Actas record later council proceedings; (p. 267) archaeological survey shows a city without palace or central temple.
  **Conclusion**: an indigenous republic existed at Tlaxcala before Cortés, and the written sources leave "little doubt as to its democratic credentials" (p. 267). My observation: the archaeology supports the absence of a monarch. "Democratic" rests on Spanish-language sources written 20 to 45 years after the events. The authors concede that how officials were chosen and who was eligible is unknown (p. 265), that the council had perhaps 50 to 200 members in a population Cortés put at 150,000, and that Tlaxcalteca may have presented themselves in terms Spaniards would recognise (p. 266). An oligarchic council would fit the same evidence, a possibility the chapter mentions once (p. 258) and does not pursue.

- **Against reading the sources as European projection.**
  **Premises**: (p. 263) the Crónica describes figures and institutions with no classical model, often corroborated elsewhere; (p. 265) sixteenth-century Spaniards had little experience of democratic procedure and no admiration for it; (p. 265) there are only so many ways to conduct a political debate.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 265) the recorded deliberations are what they seem, a view of indigenous collective government.

## Evidence offered

- The Teotihuacan mapping project (eight square miles; about 2,000 apartment compounds); excavations of the Sun and Moon Pyramids and the Feathered Serpent temple with sacrificial burials; murals from about AD 350; Teopancazco with Gulf Coast links and buried heads (pp. 252-257). Archaeological.
- Pasztory's art-historical analysis (pp. 248-249). Interpretive.
- Maya inscriptions and burials at Tikal and Copán naming Sihyaj K'ahk' and Teotihuacan-affiliated rulers; later Yucatec chronicles on the Itzá and Xiu (pp. 250-252). Textual and archaeological.
- Volcanic displacement around AD 50 to 150 (Popocatépetl, Cuicuilco, Tetimpa) and Sahagún's recorded tradition of a founding coalition (p. 253). Archaeological and a late textual tradition.
- Cortés's Letters of Relation, 1519 to 1526 (p. 259); Bernal Díaz (1568); Muñoz Camargo (1585); Motolinía's Historia (1541), using Tlaxcalteca informants (pp. 261, 265-266); Cervantes de Salazar's Crónica (1558 to 1563), rediscovered by Zelia Nuttall in 1911 (pp. 261-264); the Tlaxcalan Actas (p. 264). Textual, Spanish colonial.
- Archaeological survey of Tlaxcala and the Tizatlan civic complex (p. 267).
- Charles Mann's 1491 as the example of a secondary account calling Tlaxcala a confederation of kingdoms (p. 258).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Continues ch08's argument that cities do not need rulers, into the Americas, and its treatment of urban political revolutions (ch08 p. 245).
- Schismogenesis (ch02, ch05, ch06) is applied at the scale of civilisations: Teotihuacan against the Maya (p. 249), Tlaxcala against Tenochtitlan, compared to Athens and Sparta (p. 260).
- The complaint about historians treating indigenous political speech as European literature (p. 264) repeats ch02's case about Kandiaronk and Lahontan, and is named as the same pattern.
- The Chichimec ideal and its resemblance to Rousseau's State of Nature (p. 266) loops back to ch02 with a speculative suggestion that some of the "noble savage" story came from city-dwelling Amerindians.
- The Aztec empire as closer to what is called "the state" (p. 267) leads into ch10.
- The chapter attacks "guns, germs and steel" explanations of the conquest (p. 260) by pointing to indigenous political decisions, while ch07 (pp. 193-194) accepted Crosby's ecological account for the modern period. The two can stand together, but the emphasis differs.
- The authors criticise Mann for relying on secondary sources (p. 258) and then reconstruct Tlaxcala's politics from a chronicler they themselves describe as writing decades later from earlier accounts (pp. 262-263).

## Open questions

- Who could sit on Tlaxcala's council, how were members chosen, and did ordinary citizens have any voice? Unknown, by the authors' account (p. 265).
- What governed Teotihuacan day to day? The neighbourhood-council reading of the three-temple complexes is a suggestion (p. 256).
- If Teotihuacan dissolved "from within" (p. 257), what does that say about the stability of collective governance at that scale?
