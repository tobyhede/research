## Question this chapter answers

Does living together in very large numbers require rulers, administrators and social classes, and what do the first cities of Eurasia actually show?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scale | p. 208 | The textbook assumption that small groups can be equal and large ones cannot, so cities must bring states. The chapter's target. |
| Dunbar's Number | p. 210 | About 150, proposed by Dunbar as the upper limit of stable relationships, with larger groups said to lack solidarity. |
| Imaginary (virtual) society | pp. 208, 211 | Very large social units always exist mostly in the mind; humans live at once among the people they know and inside imagined structures shared with millions. Foragers too. |
| Early urban world | p. 214 | The authors' term for the phase beginning about 6,000 years ago when settlements of tens of thousands first appeared. |
| Mega-sites | p. 217 | The Cucuteni-Tripolye settlements of Ukraine and Moldova (Taljanky, Maidenetske, Nebelivka), about 4100 to 3300 BC. The authors say the word is a euphemism that avoids calling them cities. |
| Corvée (dubsig) | p. 224 | Obligatory seasonal labour on civic projects by free citizens in Mesopotamia; older than kingship and done in a festive, levelling spirit. |
| Primitive democracy | p. 226 | Jacobsen's 1940s term for Mesopotamian councils and assemblies. The authors accept the finding and reject the word "primitive". |
| Houses of the gods | pp. 229, 231 | Uruk's temples: partly assembly halls, partly workshops that organised labour, standardised products and kept accounts. |
| Uruk expansion | p. 232 | The planting of Uruk-style colonies along trade routes to the Taurus and Zagros, without evidence of conquest. |
| Heroic societies | p. 234 | Chadwick's term: aristocracies without central authority, built on personal loyalty, competitive feasting and contests, rejecting writing and commerce; found on the margins of bureaucratic cities. |
| Faceless civilization | p. 238 | A label for the Indus cities, which lack royal sculpture or monuments to individuals. |
| Caste before kingship | pp. 235, 238-239 | The authors' tentative reading of Mohenjo-daro: a hierarchy of purity (the Upper Citadel and its Great Bath) without warrior nobles or rulers. |
| Sangha | p. 240 | First the popular assemblies governing South Asian cities around the fifth century BC, then Buddhist monastic communities deciding by consensus. |
| Seka | p. 241 | Balinese system in which practical community affairs are run by equal participation and consensus inside a society that is otherwise a total hierarchy. |

## Leading propositions

- Large social units are always in a sense imaginary; mass society exists in the mind before and after it becomes physical (pp. 208, 211).
- The claim that small groups are naturally egalitarian and large ones are not is less commonsensical than it seems (p. 209).
- Forager residential groups are not made up of close kin (under 10 per cent among the Martu), and kinship terms work as a metaphor extending over great distances (pp. 210-211).
- Foragers may live in small groups, but they do not, and probably never did, live in small-scale societies (p. 211).
- Cities therefore posed no special cognitive challenge; they compressed an existing regional, imagined community into one place (p. 212).
- Early cities vary so much as to imply conscious experimentation in urban form, and surprisingly few show signs of authoritarian rule (p. 213).
- The largest early cities arose in Mesoamerica, without wheels, draught animals, or much metallurgy or bureaucracy (pp. 214-215).
- Cities in much of Eurasia followed a mid-Holocene change in rivers and sea levels that created fertile floodplains and deltas from about 5000 BC (p. 215).
- Extensive agriculture may have been an outcome of urbanisation, not its cause; peasantries were a later development (p. 216).
- The Ukrainian mega-sites were larger than early Uruk, lasted about eight centuries, and show no central administration, storage, fortification, monuments or elite (pp. 217-219).
- Their concentric plans arose from the bottom up through local decisions within a shared conceptual framework (p. 221).
- Egalitarian coordination by rotation and substitution, as in Basque villages, shows how such a system could work without central control and could scale (pp. 221-223).
- The earliest Mesopotamian cities show no clear evidence of monarchy; when monarchy arrives after about 2800 BC it is unmistakable (p. 224).
- Popular councils and assemblies existed in virtually every ancient Near Eastern city and persisted under later empires (p. 226).
- In early Uruk, what we would call the state sector (public works, external relations) was run by assemblies, while top-down bureaucracy was confined to the temple economy (p. 231).
- Aristocracy, and perhaps monarchy, first emerged in opposition to the egalitarian cities of the plains, among heroic societies on their fringes (pp. 233-235).
- At Mohenjo-daro wealth, craft, writing and weights are spread across the Lower Town, and absent from the Upper Citadel (p. 237).
- Overarching social hierarchy and the mechanics of everyday governance need not correspond (p. 241).
- Scholars demand strict proof for ancient democratic institutions and none for top-down authority, which they treat as history's default (p. 240).
- Self-conscious egalitarianism can take two opposed forms: everyone the same, or everyone incomparably different (p. 242).
- The claim is not that all first cities were egalitarian, but that this was a surprisingly common pattern, which shifts the burden of proof (p. 242).
- In the very long run history did move in an authoritarian direction (p. 243).
- At Taosi, around 2000 BC, an elite order was destroyed and the city went on, larger, for two or three centuries; possibly the first documented urban social revolution (pp. 244-245).

## Arguments

- **Against scale as destiny.**
  **Premises**: (p. 210) the scale argument assumes social ties grow out from kin-based bands of limited size; (pp. 210-211) forager groups are mostly non-kin and membership is open across a whole people; (p. 211) humans everywhere live inside imagined communities far larger than their acquaintance; (p. 212) city dwellers, ancient and modern, know only a small part of their city.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 212) there is no reason to assume cities needed new machinery of rule simply because of their size.

- **Mega-sites as egalitarian cities.**
  **Premises**: (p. 219) Taljanky covers 300 hectares with over 1,000 houses and an empty centre, with no administration, storage, fortification or monumental building; (p. 220) a surplus was produced, yet over eight centuries there is little evidence of warfare or elites; (pp. 220-221) houses share a plan but each has its own pottery style, and neighbourhoods have assembly houses; (pp. 221-223) the Basque analogy shows a workable mechanism.
  **Conclusion**: the mega-sites are "proof that highly egalitarian organization has been possible on an urban scale" (p. 223). My observation: the premises are absences plus one modern analogy that the authors admit is unlikely to match ancient Ukraine. Proof is a stronger word than the support allows; strong evidence that hierarchy is not required would fit it better. The authors also note doubt about how permanently the sites were occupied (pp. 219-220) and that no one knows why they were abandoned (p. 223).

- **Assemblies before kings in Mesopotamia.**
  **Premises**: (p. 224) palaces, royal burials and inscriptions appear only from about 2800 BC, so their earlier absence is meaningful; (pp. 224-225) corvée and its levelling ethos predate kings in Mesopotamian tradition; (pp. 226-227) assemblies and wards are documented in every later period, including jury lists of manual workers at Nippur and governors' letters to Assyrian kings; (p. 229) fourth-millennium Uruk had assembly-style halls and a Great Court larger than the Athenian Pnyx; (p. 232) lists of early Uruk officials contain no lugal.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 228, 232) early Mesopotamian cities were probably self-governing. The authors say the case for the pre-royal period rests "to a slightly alarming degree" on one site (p. 228) and that much "remains speculative" (p. 230).

- **Heroic societies as counter-reaction.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 232-233) Uruk's expansion spread standardised, temple-administered ways of life; (p. 233) at Arslantepe a temple of Uruk type was replaced within generations by a palace with an armoury; (pp. 233-234) from 3100 BC warrior burials and hill forts appear across the highlands, and bureaucracy disappears; (p. 235) some early aristocratic tombs were dug into abandoned Uruk colonies; (pp. 234-235) later epic traditions describe societies of this type rejecting writing and commerce.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 235) aristocracies, perhaps monarchy itself, first emerged in opposition to egalitarian cities. A further case of schismogenesis, though the word is not used here.

- **Levelling the playing field for the Indus.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 237-239) Mohenjo-daro has no palaces, royal images, warrior burials or scenes of prowess, and its wealth sits in the Lower Town; (p. 238) the citadel centres on bathing and purity; (pp. 240-241) later South Asian republics, Buddhist sangha and Balinese seka show ascetic or caste hierarchy coexisting with egalitarian decision-making.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 240-241) it is possible that Indus cities were governed on egalitarian lines despite a hierarchy of purity. The authors say they have "no way of knowing" (p. 241). My observation: this is an argument that a reading is admissible, not that it is correct; the counter-question the authors voice on p. 240 is not answered with evidence from the site.

- **Taosi reread.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 244-245) around 2000 BC Taosi's wall was razed, the palace area given over to trash pits and workshops, commoner graves cut into the elite cemetery, and a mass grave shows violence; (p. 245) the excavator calls this collapse and anarchy; (p. 245) the city then grew from 280 to 300 hectares and continued for two to three centuries.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 245) this looks more like prosperity after the abolition of a class system than collapse. The authors add that "Other interpretations are no doubt possible."

## Evidence offered

- Demographic studies of Hadza, Martu, BaYaka and !Kung residence; genomics of ancient foragers (pp. 210-211). Ethnographic and genetic.
- Mid-Holocene river and sea-level changes (p. 215). Geological.
- Early large sites in Shandong (Liangchengzhen, Yaowangcheng, by 2500 BC), Caral in Peru, and Maya lowland ceremonial centres from 1000 BC (p. 216). Archaeological, briefly cited.
- Ukrainian mega-sites: plans from survey and excavation, house counts, diet (cereals, orchard fruit, game), salt, flint, copper and pottery (pp. 217-221). Archaeological.
- Basque village rotation at Sainte-Engrâce (pp. 221-222). Ethnographic analogy.
- Mesopotamia: the Atrahasis myth, the Plaque of Ur-Nanshe, the Hymns of Gudea (pp. 224-225); Assyrian governors' letters from Nineveh, a Nippur jury list (pp. 226-227); survey of Mashkan-shapir showing even distribution of wealth across districts (p. 227); Mari and Urkesh correspondence on hostile assemblies (pp. 227-228). Textual and archaeological, mostly from periods after kingship began.
- Uruk: Eanna precinct, Great Court, cuneiform accounts and sign lists, the Uruk Vase, colonies (pp. 228-232). Archaeological and textual; residential districts unexcavated.
- Arslantepe palace and armoury; Başur Höyük cemetery (pp. 233-234). Archaeological.
- Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Dholavira, Shortugai, Lothal; undeciphered script; early excavation methods that blurred chronology (pp. 235-239). Archaeological.
- Shimao (400 hectares, about 2000 BC, mass killing, jades in the wall) and Taosi (2300 to 1800 BC) (pp. 244-245). Archaeological; Taosi quoted from the excavator.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Answers the sceptic of ch07 (p. 206) who held that large populations need chains of command.
- Builds on ch04's shrinking social worlds (ch04 pp. 97-98), cited directly on p. 212, and on forager cosmopolitanism.
- "Play farming on a grand scale" at the mega-sites (p. 220) applies ch07's ecology of freedom to cities.
- Heroic societies are compared to the Northwest Coast (pp. 233-234), tying to ch05; their opposition to cities continues the schismogenesis theme.
- The separation at Uruk of assembly-run public affairs from bureaucratic temple economy (p. 231) anticipates ch10's splitting of "the state" into separate principles, with administration as one of them.
- ch09 continues with Teotihuacan and Tlaxcala in the Americas (pp. 245-246).
- The authors criticise scholars for treating hierarchy as the default while requiring proof of democracy (p. 240). Their own practice leans the other way: absence of palaces is read as presence of self-government. They defend this for Mesopotamia by pointing out that royal evidence is conspicuous when it exists (p. 230).
- The chapter admits that in the long run rulers appeared "almost everywhere" (p. 243). It shows cities did not require them at the start; it does not explain why they arrived.

## Open questions

- What happened to the mega-sites and the Indus cities, and does their abandonment say anything about the durability of the arrangements described?
- How were decisions actually made in any of these cities before writing? The authors say the "unwritten constitutions" may never be reconstructed (p. 213).
- Can a hierarchy of purity be called egalitarian in any useful sense, even if assemblies ran daily affairs?
