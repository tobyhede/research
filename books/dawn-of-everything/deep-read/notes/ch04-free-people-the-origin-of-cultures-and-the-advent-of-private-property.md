# ch04: Free People, the Origin of Cultures, and the Advent of Private Property (pp. 95–127)

Scope note: the chapter heading falls on p. 95 and the text ends on p. 127, where chapter 5 begins. Endnotes are not in this workspace's `source.txt`.

## Question this chapter answers

What happened between the Ice Age and farming, what is actually equal in "egalitarian" societies, and where does private property come from if not from agriculture?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Culture areas | p. 98 | Regional populations with their own styles of food, dress and custom, first traceable after about 12,000 BC. Very large at first, shrinking over time. |
| Mesolithic | p. 98 | Prehistorians' term for postglacial forager populations. |
| Egalitarian society | pp. 99-100 | First approximation: one where most people feel they ought to be the same in some way agreed to be important, and largely are. The authors find the term unworkable because societies value different things. |
| Surplus | p. 101 | What humans produce beyond need. Ruling classes are those who organise society to take most of it. |
| Immediate return / delayed return | p. 102 | Woodburn's distinction: economies where food is eaten within a day or so and nothing is stored, against those that invest in future returns. He holds that only the first are truly egalitarian. |
| Autonomy | p. 103 | Leacock's suggestion for what members of "egalitarian" societies actually care about: living and deciding without interference. |
| Free societies / free people | p. 103 | The authors' replacement for "egalitarian societies", after Lallemant's description of the Wendat. |
| Formal and substantive freedoms | p. 103 | A right in principle (to travel) against the real possibility of acting on it (being fed and housed on the way). Free societies cared about the second. |
| The three freedoms (first statement) | p. 105 | The freedom to leave one's community and be welcomed elsewhere, the freedom to shift between social structures by season, and the freedom to disobey authorities without consequence. |
| Original affluent society | pp. 106-107 | Sahlins's 1968 thesis that foragers had easy access to all they felt they needed and worked two to four hours a day. |
| Archaic / Jōmon | pp. 114-115 | Period labels (about 8000 to 1000 BC in North America; about 14,000 to 300 BC in Japan) that flatten millennia of forager history into a prelude. |
| Agricultural Argument | p. 117 | The legal doctrine, traced to Locke, that land not worked by European-style farming was vacant; used to dispossess indigenous peoples. |
| No True Scotsman | p. 119 | Flew's name for protecting a generalisation by redefining its terms; applied to the claim that rich or hierarchical foragers are not real foragers. |
| Foragers | p. 120 | Populations that do not rely on biologically domesticated plants and animals as their primary food source. Nothing else follows from the word. |
| Savage slot | p. 120 | Trouillot's phrase for the conceptual box in which peoples are defined by their mode of subsistence. |
| Sacred | p. 124 | After Durkheim, that which is set apart. The authors argue it has the same form as private property: a structure of exclusion. |
| Sacra | p. 125 | Lowie's term for sacred, often immaterial property (formulae, songs, rights to perform), frequently the only private property in a free society. |
| Usus, fructus, abusus | p. 126 | The three Roman Law rights in property; true ownership includes abusus, the right to damage or destroy, so the duty of care is removed. |
| Possessive individualism | p. 125 | Macpherson's term for treating all rights on the model of owning property, including one's own body. |

## Leading propositions

- Upper Palaeolithic "society" spanned continents: similar tools, instruments, figurines and funeral rites from the Alps to Mongolia (pp. 96-97).
- Modern forager groups are cosmopolitan; close biological kin make up about 10 per cent of residential groups (p. 97).
- Over history the scale of most people's social world has shrunk as population grew; the long-run direction is the opposite of globalisation (pp. 97-98).
- The multiplication of bounded cultural worlds made durable domination easier, because exit and seasonal regrouping became harder (pp. 98-99).
- Measuring equality needs an external standard, and since Rousseau and Smith that standard has been property; hence the assumption that without farm surpluses no one could dominate anyone (p. 100).
- Saying cereal farming caused states is like saying calculus caused the atom bomb; some 6,000 years separate the first Middle Eastern farmers from the first states (p. 101).
- Woodburn's immediate-return thesis repeats the Eden story with the bar set higher, because it makes equality possible only for the simplest foragers (p. 102).
- What such societies value and distribute equally is freedom; mutual aid was understood as its condition (pp. 103-104).
- Hierarchies can exist and stay theatrical or confined to one sphere, as with Nuer rank, which mattered for marriage and almost nothing else (p. 104).
- Humans began with a self-conscious aversion to being told what to do; the puzzle is when it stopped being possible to laugh chiefs and kings out of court (p. 105).
- Sahlins was broadly right about working hours, but his argument rested on treating African foragers as typical, which he himself doubted (pp. 108, 110).
- Foragers knew how to plant and chose not to (p. 109).
- There was no original state of affairs; foragers ranged from the Hadza to industrious Californians and stratified Northwest Coast societies (p. 110).
- Poverty Point (around 1600 BC) was a hunter-gatherer centre whose ceremonial precinct was as large as the first Eurasian cities, drawing materials from hundreds of miles away, with no sign of commodity trade (pp. 111-112).
- Earthworks across the Mississippi valley and beyond share units of measurement and geometry, on John Clark's analysis, implying long-distance transmission of knowledge (p. 113).
- Forager monumentality is now attested in North America, Japan and northern Europe; what politics lay behind it is still largely unknown (pp. 114-116).
- The idea of the idle forager persists partly because of colonial law: the Agricultural Argument needed natives who did not work the land (pp. 116-117).
- Indigenous land was actively managed and governed by indigenous property law (p. 118).
- The Calusa of Florida had a king, nobles, tribute and human sacrifice without agriculture; they "got stuck" without planting a seed (pp. 118-119).
- Foragers historically lived mostly in rich environments such as coasts and rivers; twentieth-century foragers lived where no one else wanted to (pp. 120-121).
- A rich resource base does not make kingship inevitable, and even a "divine" king's power may reach no further than his presence, as with the Natchez Sun (pp. 122-123).
- In free societies, command and exclusive property are found together in one place: the sacred (pp. 123-124).
- Private property and the sacred are both structures of exclusion (p. 124).
- European thought is unusual in making absolute private property the model for all rights and freedoms (p. 125).
- Roman Law property is unique in removing the duty of care (p. 126).
- If private property has an origin, it is as old as the idea of the sacred; the real question is how it came to order so much else (p. 127).

## Arguments

- **Shrinking social worlds and getting stuck.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 96-97) Palaeolithic material culture is uniform over vast distances and people travelled far; (p. 97) clan and moiety systems let Australians and North Americans find hospitality across a continent; (p. 98) after 12,000 BC distinct culture areas appear and keep subdividing; (p. 99) mixed, mobile groups allow exit and seasonal regrouping; (p. 99) it is hard to wield arbitrary power in January over someone you will meet as an equal in July.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 99) the hardening of cultural boundaries made it more likely that a society would settle into permanent hierarchy. The authors add that this does not explain why any particular society did.

- **From "egalitarian" to "free".**
  **Premises**: (p. 100) societies value different things, so any internal definition of equality lets a society of propertyless sharecroppers count as equal before the gods; (p. 100) the external standard used since Rousseau is property; (p. 103) Leacock found that what Montagnais-Naskapi women cared about was autonomy; (p. 103) Wendat chiefs could not enforce orders.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 103) call them free societies; equality there was a consequence of liberty.

- **Against agriculture as the necessary basis of inequality.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 118-119) the Calusa had a kingdom on a fishing economy; (pp. 119-120) calling them not true foragers is ad hoc rescue; (pp. 120-121) calling their environment atypical ignores that foragers would choose rich environments and that such coastal peoples were the first destroyed by colonisation; (pp. 121-122) early coastal settlement is now better evidenced, though it was long an argument from silence.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 123) the view that humans lived like Kalahari foragers until agriculture can be buried. Qualification stated by the authors: there is as yet no clear evidence that most rich forager societies had courts or armies (p. 123).

- **Property and the sacred.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 123-124) even among Hadza and Pygmy groups, exclusive ownership and direct command occur in ritual contexts, guarded by secrecy and threat; (p. 124) Durkheim defines the sacred as what is set apart; (p. 124) property rights are rights to exclude, held against the whole world; (p. 125) on the Plains, sacred bundles were often the only things owned, inherited, bought and sold; (pp. 126-127) Aranda initiation enforces obedience and exclusive knowledge of churinga through cruelty.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 127) ritual theatres like Göbekli Tepe or Poverty Point were the kind of place where exclusive property claims and demands for obedience were likely to be made among otherwise free people. My observation: the formal similarity between property and the sacred is shown by example; that property historically grew out of the sacred is suggested, and the authors say they cannot know what forms of ownership existed at the prehistoric sites (p. 127).

- **Against Sahlins's generalisation.**
  **Premises**: (p. 108) the time-allocation data came from San, Mbuti and Hadza; (p. 110) Sahlins conceded such marginal hunters might not represent the Palaeolithic; (p. 110) Californian and Northwest Coast foragers valued work and wealth.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 110) there were many ways to be affluent and no original condition.

## Evidence offered

- Hadza and Martu residence studies (p. 97); Australian moieties and North American clans as long-distance hospitality systems (p. 97). Ethnographic.
- Woodburn on the Hadza and comparable groups (pp. 101-102, 123-124); Leacock on the Montagnais-Naskapi (p. 103); Evans-Pritchard and Gough on the Nuer (pp. 104-105). Ethnographic.
- 1960s time-allocation studies of Kalahari foragers; the !Kung remark about mongongo nuts (pp. 108-109). Ethnographic, quantitative.
- Poverty Point, Louisiana: earthworks from around 1600 BC over 200 hectares, about a million cubic metres of soil, antecedents to around 3500 BC, imported stone and copper (pp. 111-112). Archaeological. The authors say how often people came and why is not known.
- John E. Clark's 2004 analysis of shared measurement and geometry in early mound complexes, described as accepted by some specialists and ignored or doubted by others, though not refuted (p. 113). Contested interpretation, flagged as such.
- Watson's Brake; Florida shell rings; British Columbia fortified settlements from around 2000 BC (p. 114). Archaeological.
- Jōmon Japan: cycles of nucleation and dispersal, storage, Sannai Maruyama (p. 115). Archaeological, summarised.
- Finnish Giants' Churches (3000 to 2000 BC), the Shigir Idol (around 8000 BC), Karelian burials (p. 115). Archaeological.
- Locke's Second Treatise and Tully on its use in dispossession (p. 117). Textual.
- The Calusa: Spanish first-hand accounts from 1513 on, Mound Key (pp. 118-119). Textual and archaeological; the authors note that Spanish observers may have been the intended audience for royal theatrics (p. 123).
- The Natchez Sun, from better documentation (pp. 122-123). Textual.
- Lowie on sacra, Plains sacred bundles, Kwakiutl feast dishes, Amazonian ownership as mastery and care (pp. 125-126). Ethnographic.
- Strehlow on Aranda initiation (p. 127). Ethnographic.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Continues ch03's story past the Ice Age and begins to answer "how did we get stuck?" (p. 96).
- The three freedoms on p. 105 are restated in their settled form (to move, to disobey, to reshape social relations) in ch12.
- Why people work to differentiate themselves from neighbours (p. 98) is deferred to ch05.
- Clark's measurement system is to be revisited in ch11 (p. 113).
- The surplus discussion (p. 101) and the "broad truth" conceded to the conventional view anticipate ch06 and ch07 on farming and ch10 on states.
- The property-as-sacred argument links back to Rousseau's "This is mine" (ch02 p. 55) and to the wealth-into-power question.
- The chapter says rich environments do not determine politics (p. 122), yet uses the shrinking of social worlds as a quasi-structural cause of domination (p. 99). The authors call it a contributing condition, not a cause.
- The authors reject sub-categories such as "complex hunter-gatherers" (p. 120) while conceding the Calusa "got stuck" (p. 119), so a difference between kinds of forager society is still being marked, by politics and not by subsistence.

## Open questions

- How did sacred, ritual-bound exclusion come to "order so many other aspects of human affairs" (p. 127)? The chapter poses this and stops.
- How was labour at Poverty Point organised? The authors say they do not know (p. 123).
- Was Calusa kingship strong or theatrical? Left open (p. 123).
