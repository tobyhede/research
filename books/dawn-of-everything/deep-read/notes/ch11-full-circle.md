# ch11: Full Circle (pp. 330–368)

Scope note: the chapter heading falls on p. 330 and the text ends on p. 368, where chapter 12 begins. Two figures (a map on p. 339 and a diagram of Osage village and lodge arrangement on p. 357) are not in the text. Endnotes are not in this workspace's `source.txt`.

## Question this chapter answers

Were today's arrangements inevitable once grain farming and kingdoms existed, and where did the indigenous critique of ch02 come from?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Evolutionary framework | p. 331 | The eighteenth-century scheme that classifies societies by subsistence, assumes larger means more complex, and takes complexity to mean top-down hierarchy. |
| Grain state | p. 333 | James C. Scott's term for early states built on cereals, which are visible, storable, divisible and so taxable. Grain predisposed to states but did not cause them. |
| Barbarians | p. 333 | Scott's term for the peoples around the grain states, living in symbiosis with them by raiding, trading and avoidance; the period from about 3000 BC to AD 1600 was their golden age. |
| Bands, tribes, chiefdoms, states | pp. 335-336 | The neo-evolutionist sequence, summarised in order to show it does not fit North America. |
| Axial Age | p. 337 | Jaspers's term for the simultaneous rise of speculative philosophy in Greece, India and China, which the authors tie to coinage and chattel slavery, and take as possibly a Eurasian pattern only. |
| Independent point of comparison | p. 338 | The Americas before 1492, which were not part of the Eurasian world system and so allow a test of whether history had to go one way. |
| Ondinnonk | p. 340 | Wendat concept of a secret desire of the soul revealed in dreams, recorded by Ragueneau in 1649. |
| Clan system | pp. 341-342 | The continent-wide set of totemic clans that functioned as ritual societies and as a diplomatic and hospitality network; names were titles. |
| Hopewell Interaction Sphere | p. 342 | Network centred on Ohio, about 100 BC to AD 500, of geometric earthworks, rich deposits of exotic materials and periodic gatherings, without towns, kings or evident warfare. |
| Cahokia | p. 339 | Mississippian city at present-day East St Louis, about AD 1050 to 1350, peak about 15,000 people (about 40,000 in the American Bottom). |
| Chunkey | p. 348 | A game of throwing poles at a rolling disc, which became an elite spectator sport at Cahokia. |
| Vacant Quarter | pp. 339, 350 | The region around Cahokia left without permanent settlement for centuries after about 1400. |
| Third-order regime | p. 351 | A polity in which all three forms of domination come together. Suggested, tentatively, as what Cahokia and the Classic Maya were attempting. |
| Moving to a new country | p. 351 | Osage expression for constitutional change. |
| Nohozhinga | p. 358 | The Osage "Little Old Men", initiates of the highest degree who deliberated daily on natural philosophy and affairs of state. |
| Gayanashagowa | p. 362 | The epic of the founding of the Haudenosaunee League of Five Nations. |
| Adodarhoh | p. 362 | The monstrous sorcerer of the epic who commands obedience and is reformed into the keeper of the League's records. |
| Jigonsaseh | p. 362 | The Mother of Nations, highest-ranking female title in the League. |
| Attiwandaronk | p. 361 | The "Neutral" confederation of Ontario, the largest Iroquoian grouping until the 1630s and 1640s, under Mississippian influence. |

## Leading propositions

- The conventional account challenged throughout the book has its source in Turgot, Smith and the reaction against the indigenous critique (p. 331).
- Scholars say they have abandoned evolutionary schemes but have put nothing in their place, so everyone else still uses them (pp. 335-336).
- For most of history kingdoms abolished the freedom to ignore orders far more easily than the freedom to move away; until about 500 years ago much of the world's population lived beyond the tax collector or could escape him (p. 334).
- Evolutionism turns ways of life that developed in symbiosis into successive stages (p. 334).
- Because the world has been one system since the European empires, and Eurasia arguably long before, the pre-Columbian Americas are the only independent test of whether history had to take its course (pp. 337-338).
- On the North American evidence, the answer to whether monarchy was inevitable and whether cereal farming is a trap is no (p. 338).
- The societies Europeans met in North America were products of centuries of political conflict and self-conscious debate (p. 339).
- Iroquoian dream theory anticipates Freud by 250 years, which shows these intellectual traditions should not be treated as inferior or wholly alien (pp. 340-341).
- The Hopewell network may be the origin of the clan system that later spanned North America (pp. 345, 347).
- Hopewell combined monumental earthworks and continental reach with scattered homesteads, play farming and no evident elite or warfare (pp. 344-345).
- Hopewell and the 'Ubaid are opposites: one unified by celebrating individual difference, the other by suppressing it (p. 346).
- Cahokia rose abruptly around AD 1050 with planned neighbourhoods, surveillance from Monk's Mound, dismantling of rural communities, and mass public killing at noble funerals (pp. 348-349).
- Cahokia was a second-order regime joining sovereignty and charismatic politics, as among the Classic Maya (p. 351).
- Its end was mass defection, and the emptiness left behind implies conscious rejection of what it stood for (pp. 350-351).
- The petty kingdoms seen by de Soto's expedition in 1540 were the wreckage of the Mississippian project; by about 1715 they had given way to tribal republics governed by councils (pp. 352-353).
- Those changes continued processes centuries old; they were not simply reactions to European invasion (p. 353).
- In North America the state-making project came first and chiefdoms afterwards, as its rubble (p. 355).
- Esoteric knowledge is the recurring basis of domination in the pre-Columbian Americas; Cahokia tried to turn it into sovereignty and failed (pp. 355-356).
- The Osage remembered their history as a series of constitutional reforms and understood their social order as a set of discoveries, not something handed down (pp. 357-359).
- An Osage and Missouria delegation visited Paris in 1725, and Montesquieu's chapters on savage government may reflect what they said (p. 360).
- Indigenous doctrines of liberty, mutual aid and political equality were the outcome of a specific political history whose direction, for at least three centuries, was anti-authoritarian (pp. 360-361).
- In Haudenosaunee thought, giving orders is treated as almost as grave an outrage as cannibalism (p. 363).
- The political question was never settled inside indigenous society: Tsouharissen of the Attiwandaronk was something like a king in the 1620s (pp. 366-368).
- Indigenous North Americans largely sidestepped the path from agriculture to state, and the political sensibilities they developed in doing so shaped the Enlightenment (p. 368).

## Arguments

- **Not inevitable: the American test.**
  **Premises**: (p. 332) the objection: once grain surpluses exist, kingdoms follow, expand and eventually conquer; (p. 338) the Americas were outside the Eurasian system; (pp. 338-339) in western North America people moved away from agriculture; (pp. 339, 350) Cahokia, a budding grain state, was abandoned and its region left empty; (p. 353) its successor kingdoms were dismantled in favour of council-governed republics.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 338, 360) cereal farming is not a trap from which there is no exit, and falling into state formation is reversible. My observation: the case shows reversal is possible where, as the authors say, population is sparse and there is empty land to move to (pp. 351, 353). That condition did not hold in the Nile valley or Mesopotamia, so the American test bears on inevitability in general but less directly on whether exit was available elsewhere. The authors also leave open that ecological catastrophe contributed to Cahokia's end (p. 339).

- **Hopewell as the source of the clan system.**
  **Premises**: (p. 341) the same clans existed across a continent of unrelated languages, and are too recent to have come with the first settlers; (p. 342) Hopewell is the first evidence of ties among virtually all parts of North America; (p. 345) Hopewell burials contain totem-animal parts representing the later familiar clans; (p. 347) later Feasts of the Dead "resurrected" names.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 347) Hopewell "may have" spread the structure of the clan system, and its spectacular burials may have stopped because that work was done. Presented as a possibility.

- **Rejection, not just collapse.**
  **Premises**: (p. 350) Cahokia and its mythology are absent from later oral tradition; (p. 350) the region stayed empty of permanent settlement; (p. 352) at Etowah a royal tomb was built on a razed council house, and the site was later sacked and abandoned; (p. 354) Cherokee tradition tells of the Aní-Kutánî, a priestly caste massacred for its abuses; (p. 354) post-Mississippian towns built council houses and square grounds with no Mississippian precedent.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 351, 361) later societies were self-conscious creations designed as a barrier against what Cahokia represented. My observation: the Etowah sequence and the oral traditions are suggestive. The link from Cahokia's fall to Iroquoian political thought far to the northeast is indirect, and the authors say it "would be going a bit too far" to call the views Lahontan recorded the ideology that overthrew Mississippian civilisation (p. 361).

- **The Osage as constitutional thinkers.**
  **Premises**: (p. 357) La Flesche, a native Omaha speaker, recorded Osage tradition as a history distributed among 24 clans and seven degrees of initiation; (pp. 358-359) the tradition narrates successive crises each resolved by a move to a new country, including requiring every clan's consent for war and later separating civil from military authority; (p. 359) it begins with neutralising a sorcerer-chief by giving him a place in the new order.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 359) the idea of a people consciously creating its institutions was commonplace in North America before Europeans arrived, and is not an Enlightenment invention. The Montesquieu connection is conjecture: "We don’t know whom specifically they met with" (p. 360).

- **The answer to "organising against something never experienced".**
  **Premises**: (p. 363) the Gayanashagowa makes the villain a ruler who commands; (p. 363) critics ask how people could design institutions against a state they had not known, and suggest the motif was added after experience of the United States; (pp. 364-366) archaeology shows Iroquoian society forming in the heyday of Cahokia, Mississippian influence in Ontario, and a near-king among the Attiwandaronk in 1627.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 363, 368) the later addition is unlikely; arbitrary rule was a known possibility within the region's own history.

## Evidence offered

- James C. Scott's account of grain states and barbarians (pp. 332-334). Secondary, summarised with approval.
- Ragueneau's Jesuit Relation of 1649 on Wendat dream theory (pp. 340-341). Textual.
- Elizabeth Tooker on North American clans (pp. 341-342). Ethnological.
- Hopewell archaeology: Scioto and Paint Creek sites, the Newark Earthworks (over two square miles, embankments over sixteen feet), deposits of mica, obsidian, copper, shell, grizzly teeth; homestead settlement; three site clusters with men, women or both buried with badges of office (pp. 342-346). Archaeological.
- Muskogee mound maintenance by rotating officials (p. 343). Ethnographic analogy.
- Cahokia archaeology: Monk's Mound, woodhenge, planned neighbourhoods, a mound with four mass graves of mainly young women, a palisade from about 1150, depopulation (pp. 348-350). Archaeological.
- Etowah valley sequence, AD 1000 to 1500 (pp. 351-352). Archaeological.
- De Soto expedition account of the Coosa mico, 1540 (p. 352). Textual.
- Ethnography and history of the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Creek and Seminole; the Aní-Kutánî tradition; Creek daily councils with tobacco and the black drink (pp. 353-354). Ethnographic and oral.
- Francis La Flesche on the Osage (pp. 357-359). Ethnographic, early twentieth century.
- Bourgmont's 1725 delegation to Paris (p. 360). Historical; the meeting with Montesquieu is inferred.
- The Gayanashagowa in nineteenth-century and later recordings (pp. 362-363). Oral epic.
- Iroquoian archaeology: maize in Ontario by AD 1100, Late Owasco palisaded towns (1230 to 1375), evidence of warfare and prisoner sacrifice from 1050, stabilising population (pp. 364-365). Archaeological.
- Daillon's 1627 report on Tsouharissen; a contested oral history attributed to his third wife, which the authors note almost all historians discount (p. 366). Textual and oral.
- The 1687 Denonville episode and the Jigonsaseh's campaign (pp. 367-368). Historical, source not visible in this text.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Closes the loop opened in ch02: the indigenous critique is given a history of its own, so Kandiaronk appears as heir to a political tradition and not as a child of nature.
- Applies ch10's vocabulary (first- and second-order regimes, the three forms) to North America and adds a "third-order" possibility (p. 351).
- Clark's shared measurement system, deferred from ch04 (ch04 p. 113), returns on p. 344 with the note that Hopewell introduced a different metrical system.
- The 'Ubaid comparison (p. 346) uses ch10 (pp. 316-317); the two forms of egalitarianism come from ch08 (p. 242).
- The Natchez (chs. 4 and 10) are placed as a late remnant of the Mississippian world (p. 353).
- The Scott passage concedes a good deal: grain is "predisposed" to the fiscal needs of states (p. 333), and for at least 2,000 years most people have lived under kings or emperors (p. 332). The chapter argues against inevitability, not against the pattern.
- ch02 argued that Enlightenment thinkers were right to credit indigenous sources; here the authors step back from saying the Enlightenment began in North America ("it would be too much to suggest", p. 355) while holding the door open.
- The chapter relies on oral traditions recorded in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries to reconstruct attitudes of the twelfth to seventeenth, and on one oral history that historians reject. The authors flag both (pp. 363, 366).
- The Osage are offered as showing democratised esoteric knowledge, though they are described as a theocracy or oligarchy of initiates who needed time and wealth to rise (p. 358). The authors say so themselves (p. 356).

## Open questions

- What ended Cahokia: revolt, defection, ecological failure, or a mix? Unknown (p. 339).
- How far did Mississippian experience actually reach the Wendat and Haudenosaunee, who by the authors' account had little direct contact (p. 365)?
- Did Montesquieu meet the Osage delegation, and can any passage of The Spirit of the Laws be tied to it?
- What made exit possible in North America that was absent in Eurasia's river valleys?
