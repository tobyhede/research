Scope note: p. 4 also carries the end of the authors' biographies, the list of their other books, and the list of the seven maps and figures. The figures themselves are images and are not in this workspace's `source.txt`. The foreword begins near the foot of p. 4; the acknowledgements run from p. 5 to p. 6, where chapter 1 begins.

## Question this section answers

How was the book written, by whom, and what does it set out to be?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grand dialogue | p. 4 | Wengrow's description of the project: an anthropologist and an archaeologist reconstructing the kind of large conversation about human history once common in their fields, using modern evidence. |
| Synthesis | p. 5 | Wengrow's word for the result: a single voice, not a patchwork of two authors' sections. |
| The archive | p. 5 | The shared store of ideas, facts and examples the two authors accumulated, which outgrew one book. |

## Leading propositions

- David Graeber died on 2 September 2020, just over three weeks after the book was finished; the foreword and acknowledgements are by Wengrow alone (pp. 4-5).
- The book took more than ten years and began as a diversion from the authors' academic duties, "an experiment, a game almost" (p. 4).
- The authors often lost track of who contributed which idea; the styles converged into one (p. 5).
- Three sequels were planned to develop concepts introduced here (p. 5).
- Earlier versions of parts of the argument were given as lectures and articles: Chapter Two in French in La Revue du MAUSS, Chapter Three as the 2014 Henry Myers Lecture, Chapter Four in American Anthropologist, Chapter Eight as the 2015 Jack Goody Lecture, Chapter Nine in Lapham's Quarterly, and a general statement in Eurozine as "How to change the course of human history" (p. 6).
- Both authors used the material in their teaching, at the LSE Department of Anthropology and the UCL Institute of Archaeology (p. 6).

## Evidence offered

- The list of maps and figures credits their sources: Wissler and Kroeber for the North American culture areas and the Californian shatter zone, Sherratt for the Fertile Crescent, Fuller for centres of domestication, Chapman and Gaydarska for Nebelivka, Millon for Teotihuacan, Pauketat for the Mississippi basin, Fletcher and La Flesche for the Osage village (p. 4). This is useful as a guide to whose work the corresponding chapters lean on.
- The acknowledgements name specialists consulted, among them James C. Scott, Marshall Sahlins, Philippe Descola, Barbara Alice Mann, Timothy Pauketat, Dorian Fuller, John Chapman and Bisserka Gaydarska (pp. 5-6).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The description of the project as play (p. 4) is echoed at the end of the book, in ch12, where the authors say the research began "essentially as a form of play" (p. 391), and fits the book's argument that play is where new things are tried (ch12 pp. 374-375).
- The attribution of the article behind Chapter Four ("Many seasons ago: slavery and its rejection among foragers on the Pacific Coast") does not match the chapter numbering of this edition, where that material is Chapter Five (p. 6). Likewise the Henry Myers Lecture title, "Farewell to the childhood of man", is close to the title of Chapter One though it is credited to Chapter Three. I record the mismatch and draw no conclusion from it.
- The planned sequels (p. 5) explain why several questions are left open in ch12.
