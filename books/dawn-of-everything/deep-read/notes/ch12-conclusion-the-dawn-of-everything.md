# ch12: Conclusion: The Dawn of Everything (pp. 368–394)

Scope note: the chapter heading falls on p. 368 and the text ends on p. 394, followed by the bare headings of the Notes section (the endnote text itself is absent from this workspace's `source.txt`).

## Question this chapter answers

If the origin of inequality is the wrong question, what went wrong in human history, how did we get stuck, and how stuck are we?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Authors' meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tragic and optimistic narratives | p. 369 | The two standard stories: a fall from equality with no redemption (Rousseau), and progress redeeming a brutish start (Hobbes). The second is said to be popular among billionaires. |
| Traditional and historical societies | pp. 370-372 | The claim, here taken from Eliade, that pre-Enlightenment peoples lived in cyclical time and did not consciously make history. Rejected as absurd. |
| Illo tempore / the dawn of everything | p. 371 | Eliade's mythic time of creation when genuinely new things could be made. The source of the book's title. |
| Zone of ritual play | pp. 374-375 | Ritual, games and play as the place where both technical and social innovations were first tried and stored: a laboratory and an encyclopaedia of social possibilities. |
| The three basic freedoms (final form) | p. 376 | (1) to move away or relocate; (2) to ignore or disobey commands; (3) to shape entirely new social realities or shift between different ones. The first two are scaffolding for the third. |
| Play kings | p. 377 | Rulers whose subjects can ignore them or leave. They stop being play kings when they start killing people (p. 378). |
| Social substitutability | p. 379 | Raymond Kelly's principle underlying war: any member of one side is a legitimate target for any member of the other. |
| Dominium / familia | p. 382 | Roman terms linking household, slavery, property and sovereignty: family from famulus (house slave), dominium for both the emperor's sovereignty and a citizen's power over property. |
| Mourning wars | p. 383 | Wendat wars fought to assuage grief; captives were either fully adopted or tortured to death. |
| Nexus of violence and care | pp. 382, 385, 389 | The entanglement of arbitrary violence with relations of care in household and kingdom; the authors' main candidate for how people got stuck. |
| Pre-servile institutions | p. 389 | Franz Steiner's term for arrangements in which the unmoored (orphans, debtors, runaways, captives) are taken into a chief's household, becoming dependants and enforcers. |
| Potestas | p. 390 | Roman Law term for a father's arbitrary power over dependants, used by Steiner for a chief's power over his wards. |
| Kairos | p. 393 | A moment when a society's frames of reference shift and real change becomes possible. |

## Leading propositions

- The optimistic story of Western progress cannot explain why that civilization had to be imposed at gunpoint for 500 years, or why Westerners offered a choice so often defected (p. 369).
- Modern social theory descends from nineteenth-century conservative reactions to the French Revolution (Bonald, Maistre, Burke), organised around why Enlightenment projects failed (pp. 369-370).
- All sides came to agree that deliberate reshaping of society is a post-Enlightenment possibility; the book denies this (pp. 370-371).
- Social science has largely been the study of how humans are not free, which is one reason big histories focus on technology (p. 373).
- Technology matters, but its role in setting the direction of social change is easily overstated: stone-tool Teotihuacan and metal-using Mohenjo-daro differed little in organisation or size (p. 373).
- Neolithic innovation was collective, cumulative and largely women's work (pp. 373-374).
- Many inventions appeared first in ritual or play before any practical use: ceramics for figurines, mining for pigments, wheels on Mesoamerican toys, Greek steam devices, Chinese gunpowder for fireworks (p. 374).
- The authors pose as a research question whether women's freedom correlates with a society's degree of innovation (p. 375).
- Private property, police powers, command, and later election and sortition, all first appear in sacred or ritual contexts (p. 375).
- From the Palaeolithic on, many people actually lived in different social orders at different times of year, not merely imagined them (p. 376).
- If something went wrong, it began when people lost the freedom to imagine and enact other forms of social existence (p. 376).
- Where the first two freedoms hold, kings can only be play kings (p. 377).
- How we got stuck remains partly speculation; the authors offer points of departure (p. 377).
- One factor is the division of humanity into culture areas defining themselves against each other (pp. 377-378).
- War in the strict sense is not primordial; the Palaeolithic record shows little of it, and its history is uneven, with long peaceful periods (p. 379).
- The early kingdoms share spectacular violence at the top and courts modelled on the patriarchal household (p. 380).
- Roman Law's idea of freedom as absolute power over property derives from slave law, so the logic of war was built into the household and into our concepts of freedom and property (pp. 381-382).
- The Wendat kept violence wholly outside the household and directed it at outsiders; seventeenth-century France used public torture on its own people and modelled kingdom on patriarchal family (pp. 384-385).
- The confusion of care with domination is critical to how we lost the ability to recreate ourselves (p. 385).
- The most stubborn misconception is that scale requires hierarchy (p. 386).
- The first cities are better seen as regional confederacies compressed into a small space (p. 387).
- Monarchy as a permanent institution seems to begin among heroic societies on the edges of cities, not in cities (pp. 387-388).
- In Sumer, kings moved in beside temples that were also charitable institutions and the first factories; the status of their dependent workers, and perhaps of women generally, declined (pp. 388-389).
- Loss of the first freedom led to loss of the second, and that to the effacement of the third (p. 389).
- Following Steiner, top-down power may have begun in charity: refuge given to the unattached created dependants and enforcers (p. 390).
- Slavery was probably abolished many times in history, and perhaps war too (p. 392).
- Periods of free or relatively free societies make up most of human social experience if the Eurasian Iron Age is bracketed (p. 392).
- Mass enslavement, genocide, prison camps, patriarchy and wage labour never had to happen, and the scope for human intervention now is greater than we suppose (p. 393).
- The mythic structures of history used for the last few centuries no longer fit the evidence (p. 394).

## Arguments

- **Ritual play as the seedbed of institutions.**
  **Premises**: (p. 374) techniques from ceramics to gunpowder were first used in ritual or play; (p. 375) Hocart proposed that kingship derived from ritual and that the first kings were dead kings; (p. 375) Palaeolithic grand burials fit; (p. 375) property, police and command appear first in sacred contexts (chs. 3, 4).
  **Conclusion**: (p. 375) the zone of ritual play was a site of social experiment, and (p. 376) people did not merely play at alternatives but lived them seasonally.

- **The scaffolding argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 377) if people can leave and can refuse orders, any authority exists on sufferance; (p. 377) seasonal, rotating police are play police; (p. 376) the third freedom, to remake social arrangements, depends on those two.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 376-377) the three freedoms stand or fall in sequence, and their recession is what has changed in human societies.

- **How play became permanent: violence.**
  **Premises**: (p. 378) a person killed in theatrical display stays dead; (p. 378) war's injuries outlast the contest; (p. 378) excesses of ritual killing mark transitions to real kingship (ch10).
  **Conclusion**: (p. 378) violence is often the route by which forms of play take on permanent features. The authors then ask, without answering, how direct the link is between external war and internal loss of freedom (p. 379).

- **Violence and care.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 381-382) Roman jurists defined freedom as a household head's absolute power over property, modelled on power over slaves legally classed as war captives; (p. 382) family, domestic, dominion and dominate share roots; (p. 385) James I describes kingship as fatherly care and chastisement; (pp. 383-384) the Wendat instead excluded violence from the household entirely; (ch05, ch10) capturing societies and Egyptian kingship also fuse care and violence.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 385) the confusion of care and domination is critical to how relations of violence became normal. Marked as the authors' view ("It seems to us").

- **Steiner's route from charity to command.**
  **Premises**: (p. 390) in Amazonian, Safwa and Lushai cases, those without kin took refuge with the chief and became his retinue, the young men his enforcers; (p. 390) the chief's power over them depended on how easily they could leave; (p. 391) royal courts everywhere gathered the detached and claimed to protect widows and orphans; (pp. 388-389) Sumerian temples sheltered the needy and put them to work.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 389-390) the erosion of hospitality and asylum, the first freedom, opened the way to arbitrary power, without need of the religious revelation that Lowie and Clastres fell back on. My observation: this is the chapter's most concrete mechanism and it rests on one scholar's comparative study plus the Sumerian case, which the authors themselves frame as a question, asking whether this nexus marks "the point where everything begins to get confused" (p. 389).

- **Against scale.**
  **Premises**: (p. 386) complex systems need not be organised top-down (Crumley); (p. 387) early cities often lacked administrative hierarchy; (p. 387) hereditary aristocracies arose in small societies.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 387) large settlements did not cause structures of domination; their origin lies elsewhere.

## Evidence offered

- Intellectual history: Bonald, Maistre, Burke, Eliade, Hocart (pp. 370-372, 375). Textual.
- Examples of inventions first used in ritual or play (p. 374). Historical and archaeological, stated without sources in the text.
- Kelly on war and Scarry on injury (pp. 378-379); systematic studies of the Palaeolithic record showing little warfare; Neolithic massacres in central Europe (p. 379). Secondary.
- Patterson on Roman property and slave law; Latin etymologies (pp. 381-382). Legal-historical.
- Jesuit accounts of Wendat mourning wars and torture; Delâge on Wendat reactions to French punishment (pp. 383-384). Textual.
- James I, The True Law of Free Monarchies, 1598 (p. 385). Textual.
- Sumerian temple records of dependent workers (pp. 388-389). Textual, summarised.
- Steiner's comparative cases: Huitoto, Safwa, Lushai (p. 390). Ethnographic, second-hand.
- No new archaeological evidence is introduced; the chapter draws conclusions from earlier ones.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Answers "how did we get stuck?" (ch03 p. 92) as far as the book ever does, and states the limit plainly: "this must remain a matter for speculation" (p. 377).
- Gathers the three freedoms (ch04 p. 105; ch10 pp. 270, 320) and the three forms of domination (ch10) into one statement.
- Culture areas and schismogenesis (ch04, ch05), play kings and seasonality (ch03), property and the sacred (ch04), caring labour and capture (ch05), Egyptian kingship and Sumerian temples (ch08, ch10), Cahokia and the Wendat (ch11) are each called back.
- "Domination begins at home" (ch05 p. 159) becomes the nexus of violence and care.
- The opening epigraph on kairos (ch01 p. 6) is returned to on p. 393.
- The book rejected the search for a point where things went wrong (ch01 p. 13) and ends by saying "something did go terribly wrong" (p. 376) and asking when. The difference claimed is that the loss was gradual, plural and reversible, with no single cause or date.
- "If you bracket the Eurasian Iron Age" (p. 392) is a large bracket: it sets aside the period and region in which most of the people who have ever lived under states did so.
- The authors admit they did not weigh competing interpretations case by case, to keep the book readable (p. 386). A reader cannot tell from the text where the specialist consensus lies and where the authors are in a minority.
- The explanation leans on patriarchy and war without giving either an origin; the authors say evidence is only beginning to be gathered (p. 391).
- ch05 said that where one sets the dial between freedom and determinism is a matter of taste (ch05 p. 158); the conclusion's claim that the worst institutions "never had to happen" (p. 393) is an application of that setting, not an independent finding.

## Open questions

- Is there a correlation between war abroad and loss of freedom at home, and how direct is it (p. 379)?
- Is there a correlation between women's freedom and innovation (p. 375)?
- Why did the first freedom erode where it did? Steiner's mechanism explains what follows from lost hospitality, not why hospitality was lost.
- What would count as evidence against the book's central claim that current arrangements were not inevitable, given that the authors hold that such questions about what might have happened cannot be definitively answered (ch11 p. 337)?
