# ch08: Into the Danger Zone (pp. 196–227)

## Question this chapter answers

What happened to the German economy and to the regime's strategic outlook in 1938? Tooze's answer: after the May crisis Hitler accepted that Britain would oppose him and ordered an all-out armaments surge. That pushed the economy to the limit of non-inflationary growth, convinced generals and ministers that war would be lost, and produced the retreat at Munich. The same pressures, with the foreign exchange block on emigration, fed the assault on the Jews, which in turn hardened American and British hostility.

## Leading propositions

- The Anschluss was strategic in motive; economically it added under 8 per cent to industrial output, 401,000 unemployed and a foreign exchange windfall that let Germany run a 450 million Reichsmark trade deficit in 1938 (pp. 197-198).
- After May 1938 Berlin had to treat the British Empire as an opponent of eastern conquest. This undid the one rational premise of the Mein Kampf strategy (pp. 199-200).
- Nobody in 1938 anticipated Blitzkrieg; a Western coalition backed by America looked overwhelming to German planners (pp. 199-200).
- Hitler ordered the wartime army completed by April 1939, with three months of ammunition; the army tripled its steel request in a week (pp. 200-201).
- The Wehrmacht's steel quota rose from 325,000 to 658,333 tons a month; the military share of steel passed 40 per cent (p. 202).
- Schacht and Krosigk's plan to end Mefo finance in March 1938 was overtaken; spending was funded with short-term Treasury bills. Military spending exceeded 19 per cent of national income in 1938 (pp. 202-203).
- Steel for non-Wehrmacht uses was cut 25 per cent between March and July 1938 even as output hit a record 21.5 million tons (p. 203).
- Germany was already the most heavily taxed society in Europe, so finance came from corporation tax, raids on local government, forced placement of state paper and a ban on new mortgages (pp. 205-206).
- The Reichsbahn neared collapse in September 1938; under half of freight car requests were met on time (p. 206).
- Unemployment in the old Reich was 292,327 in July 1938, of whom 28,000 were fully fit (p. 207).
- Paid farm labour fell by 400,000 (almost 20 per cent) between 1933 and 1938; the leadership feared for the food supply, and the October date for action against the Czechs was set partly to follow the harvest (pp. 209-210).
- The Reichsbank concluded that the Reichsmark no longer had a definable external value (pp. 212-213).
- Beck, Halder, Krosigk, Weizsaecker, Schacht and even Goering opposed war in 1938 on the ground that a Western coalition with American supply would win a war of attrition (pp. 214-218).
- On 29 September 1938 it was Hitler who stepped back, and in doing so he almost certainly saved his regime (p. 218).
- Jewish emigration had stalled at about 20,000 a year because of the foreign exchange cost; the Reichsbank put the cost of transferring German Jewish wealth at 2.2 to 5.15 billion Reichsmarks. Terror substituted for transfer (pp. 219-220).
- Registered Jewish wealth was 8.5 billion Reichsmarks gross, of which only 1.19 billion was business assets. Aryanisation was no turning point in the economy, and Jewish economic dominance was an anti-Semitic myth (pp. 220-221).
- The state, not business, gained most; yet the levy and flight tax were about 5 per cent of Reich revenue in 1938-9 and could not fund rearmament (pp. 221-223).
- More than 200,000 Jews left Germany and Austria between March 1938 and September 1939, stripped of their assets (p. 224).
- Kristallnacht turned Halifax and American opinion; from 1938 the regime equated the international Jewish question with America, and Hitler's 30 January 1939 prophecy belongs to that context (pp. 225-227).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 199-200) Britain, France and America together outweighed Germany. (pp. 214-215) Germany could not beat France quickly in 1938. (p. 215) So the West could wait.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 215, 218) War in 1938 would have been a disaster for Germany; Munich was Hitler's retreat.
- **Premises**: (p. 203) No slack remained. (pp. 205-206) Finance fell short by 8.1 billion Reichsmarks. (pp. 207-209) Wage and price stops removed market signals.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 207, 211-213) Allocation became political throughout, and the economy entered an inflationary danger zone.
- **Premises**: (pp. 219-220) Emigration needed foreign exchange the Reichsbank lacked. (p. 219) The SS feared war with Jews still in Germany.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 220) Violence had a functional role in forcing emigration; Tooze adds that the SD's own reports link the pogrom to the Sudeten crisis (p. 218).

- Reichsbank reports on import price premiums by country and commodity (pp. 212-213).
- Beck's memoranda and Krosigk's memorandum to Hitler, summarised at length (pp. 214-218). Tooze warns the sources on the internal opposition are scanty and unreliable (p. 218).
- The 1938 register of Jewish assets, Finance Ministry tax receipts, insurers' loss records, minutes of the 12 November conference (pp. 220-223).
- Goering's speech of 8 July 1938 to aircraft industrialists (pp. 204, 225).
