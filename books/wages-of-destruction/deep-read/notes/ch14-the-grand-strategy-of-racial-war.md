# ch14: The Grand Strategy of Racial War (pp. 360–378)

## Question this chapter answers

How do strategy, economics and racial ideology fit together in the invasion of the Soviet Union, and what programmes of mass killing went with it? Tooze's answer: for Hitler strategy was race struggle, so asking whether Barbarossa was "strategic" or "ideological" is a false alternative. The invasion was the last great colonial land grab. It carried two murderous programmes: the SS's long-term Generalplan Ost with the Final Solution, and the short-term Hunger Plan agreed by the Wehrmacht and the ministries to feed Germany and its army from Soviet grain.

## Leading propositions

- A consistent, if "mad", strategic logic ran from September 1939 through France to Barbarossa: strike fast and keep the initiative before American strength told (p. 360).
- That logic does not make Barbarossa less ideological; Hitler saw the Western war as forced on Germany by a Jewish conspiracy, which made eastern conquest more urgent (pp. 360-361).
- In execution Barbarossa was a departure: with it came genocide. The murder of the Jews was the first step in a larger project of clearing and colonising the East (p. 361).
- Deportation plans in annexed Poland were wildly ambitious and largely failed: 305,000 displaced by end 1940 against targets in the millions; 530,000 ethnic Germans repatriated, most left in transit camps (pp. 362-363).
- Hitler's 30 January 1941 repetition of his prophecy was no longer conditional (p. 363).
- By mid-June 1941 planners were considering removing the whole Polish population of the General Government (p. 364).
- The Wannsee proposal met no criticism from the State Secretaries, whereas the RSHA's draft of the Generalplan Ost was fiercely attacked inside the administration (pp. 364-365).
- The Generalplan foresaw removing 80 to 85 per cent of Poles, 64 per cent of Ukrainians and 75 per cent of White Russians (p. 365). It should be taken as seriously as Wannsee (p. 366).
- The planners thought of themselves as modernisers. Hitler's model was the American frontier: the Volga as Germany's Mississippi (pp. 366-367).
- The plan aimed at a mixed economy (about a third in agriculture) at 80 people per square kilometre, tied to consolidation of small farms inside Germany; 220,000 families were to come from overcrowded rural districts (pp. 367-369).
- Its cost, 67 billion Reichsmarks, was about two-thirds of 1941 GDP; slave labour was to cut construction cost by 40 per cent, which linked it to the camps. Birkenau and Majdanek were ordered on 27 September 1941 for 50,000 inmates each (pp. 369-371).
- When Soviet prisoners were no longer available, Himmler ordered 150,000 Jews into the camps for "economic tasks" (January 1942) (p. 371).
- The Wehrmacht entered the Soviet Union committed to a second programme of mass murder that was not secret and was agreed by all major ministries (p. 371).
- The Ukraine produced only a small net surplus because Stalin had built an urban population of 30 million since 1928. Backe's answer was to starve those cities (p. 372).
- Thomas backed the plan because feeding the army locally freed scarce rail capacity for fuel and ammunition (p. 373).
- The 2 May 1941 minute records that "many millions" would starve; Backe, Himmler and Goering spoke of 20 to 30 million (pp. 373-374).
- Einsatzgruppe A alone killed over 270,000 by spring 1942; second sweeps in 1942 killed at least 360,000 more in the Ukraine and Belorussia (pp. 374-375).
- Starvation by inaction proved naive: only confined or marked groups could be killed that way, that is, urban Jews and prisoners of war (p. 375).
- Of 3.35 million Soviet prisoners taken by end December 1941, 2.25 million were dead, at least 600,000 of them shot. Had the war stopped in early 1942 this would have been the regime's greatest single crime (p. 376).
- The Hunger Plan was never fully carried out because Moscow and Leningrad were not taken; but the front cut Soviet cities from Ukrainian grain. In Leningrad 653,000 died in the first eleven months of siege (pp. 377-378).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 360) Germany's resources were modest against the coalition forming. (p. 361) Hitler believed that coalition was directed by world Jewry. (p. 361) Lebensraum had always been the goal.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 361) Strategic urgency and ideological purpose pointed the same way; neither explains Barbarossa alone.
- **Premises**: (p. 372) Europe's grain deficit could be met only from the Ukraine. (p. 372) The Ukrainian surplus was consumed by Soviet cities. (p. 373) Rail capacity could not carry food for the army.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 372-373) Mass starvation of Soviet city-dwellers was adopted as a practical requirement of the war, which is why it won broad bureaucratic support.
- **Premises**: (p. 369) Settlement needed huge investment. (p. 370) Cash was short; slave labour was cheaper.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 370) The settlement plan and the camp system were joined by cost accounting.

## Evidence offered

- Drafts of the Generalplan Ost from the Madajczyk document collection (Tables 14 and 15; Table 14 extracted only in fragments) (pp. 364-371).
- The minute of the 2 May 1941 State Secretaries' meeting from Thomas's secretariat, and the OKW "Green Book" guidelines, both quoted (pp. 373-374). These are the perpetrators' own planning documents.
- Himmler's speeches to SS leaders (June 1941, summer 1942) and his January 1942 letter on sending Jews to the camps (pp. 370-371, 373).
- Wehrmacht prisoner statistics (p. 376); the 18th Army's memorandum on options for Leningrad (pp. 377-378); a local Wehrmacht administrator's note on feeding civilians (pp. 376-377).
- Einsatzgruppen death totals (pp. 374-375). Sources are named only in endnotes not read here.

- Fulfils the preface's promise to connect the Holocaust to land, food and labour (fm01 pp. 9, 13).
- Darré's 1932 and 1936 visions of eastern settlement (ch06 pp. 147-148, 162-163) become plans; Backe moves from the peacetime RNS to genocide.
- The General Government rationing of 1940 (ch10 p. 290) is the precedent.
- America as model and rival (ch01 Second Book, ch05) reappears in the frontier analogy.
- Labour as a motive for keeping prisoners and Jews alive, against food as a motive for killing them, is the subject of ch16.
- Tension: the chapter insists on economic rationales for killing while also showing the plans failing on practical grounds (deportations in Poland, Zamość, starvation by inaction).
