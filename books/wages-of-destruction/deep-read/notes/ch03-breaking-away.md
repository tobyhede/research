# ch03: Breaking Away (pp. 62–85)

## Question this chapter answers

How did the regime get through the balance of payments crisis of 1934, and what did the way out cost? Tooze's answer: it refused devaluation, defaulted on its foreign debts, split its creditors, and built a bureaucratic trade-control system (the New Plan) that gave armaments and exports first call on scarce imports. The price was a lopsided recovery in which consumer industries stagnated, and a sour public mood.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Foreign exchange (transfer) crisis | pp. 63-64 | March to September 1934: Reichsbank reserves fall below 100 million Reichsmarks, about a week of imports (p. 66). The nearest thing to a comprehensive socio-economic crisis in the regime's twelve years. |
| Complete moratorium | p. 64 | Schacht's 14 June 1934 suspension of foreign-currency payment on all international debt. |
| Clearing agreement | p. 66 | Bilateral payments deal; in Western Europe these tended to equalise trade and so removed the surpluses Germany needed to pay for overseas imports. |
| Reich flight tax | p. 68 | Tax on emigrants' assets; threshold cut from 200,000 to 50,000 Reichsmarks in May 1934. With the transfer discount it taxed the emigration the regime wanted. |
| Bond buy-back subsidy | pp. 69-70 | Exporters buy depreciated German bonds abroad and resell them to the Reichsbank near face value: a selective devaluation paid for by foreign creditors. |
| Surveillance agencies (Ueberwachungsstellen) | p. 72 | Commodity-by-commodity import rationing bodies, first for wool and cotton; 25 under the New Plan (p. 80). |
| Krogmann Plan | p. 75 | RWM proposal of July 1934 for tradable foreign exchange vouchers, producing an internal market devaluation. Dropped when Schacht took the ministry. |
| New Plan | p. 80 | Schacht's system of autumn 1934: Reichsbank allocates foreign exchange, agencies issue Exchange Certificates by national priority, imports without a certificate banned from 1935. |
| Autarchy | p. 76 | Not general self-sufficiency: selective disengagement from the United States, the British Empire and to a lesser extent France. |
| Anglo-German Payments Agreement | p. 77 | 1 November 1934: 55 per cent of sterling earnings for British goods, 10 per cent for debt service, the rest free. Split the Anglo-American creditor front. |
| Haavara Transfer | p. 79 | Arrangement matching Jewish emigrants' capital to German export orders to Palestine; used by 50,000 people. |
| Export levy | p. 81 | From May 1935 a tax of 2 to 4 per cent on industrial turnover to fund export subsidies; 700 million Reichsmarks in the first year. |

## Leading propositions

- The summer 1934 crisis was economic as well as political: the Roehm purge and the failed Austrian coup coincided with a near halt in foreign trade (pp. 63-64).
- Export earnings in early summer 1934 were 20 per cent below a year earlier; export volume was 40 per cent below 1932 (p. 66).
- Germany was not only a victim of protectionism: its own protection since 1930 and its debt diplomacy had worsened matters (pp. 66-67).
- The foreign exchange shortage contradicted the regime's aim of forcing Jews to emigrate. Emigrant transfers cost 132 million Reichsmarks between January 1933 and June 1935; tighter controls cut the drain and cut emigration from 37,000 in 1933 to 23,000 in 1934 and 21,000 in 1935 (pp. 67-68).
- The basic cause of uncompetitiveness was the overvalued Reichsmark after the sterling and dollar devaluations, not wages or taxes (pp. 68-69).
- Devaluation was refused chiefly because it would raise the Reichsmark value of foreign debt; Schacht said he had never rejected it on principle (p. 69).
- The regime could protect either consumer-goods imports or the imports needed for state investment and rearmament. The balance of payments, not preference, ruled out a recovery built on both (pp. 70-71).
- In March 1934 Schacht outflanked Schmitt before Hitler, declaring no sum too large for rearmament; military spending was then removed from Finance Ministry scrutiny (pp. 71-72).
- Schacht appears to have forced the crisis deliberately to bring down Schmitt (p. 74).
- The alternative (devaluation and rapprochement with the West) was dropped in late July 1934 when Schacht became Acting Minister with the military's backing (pp. 75-76).
- Schacht's brinkmanship with Britain paid off; the united creditor front broke and American creditors were then singled out for the worst treatment (pp. 77-78).
- German-American trade collapsed: American exports to Germany fell from 2 billion Reichsmarks in 1928 to 232 million in 1936 (p. 78). This was the substance of autarchy.
- Trade shifted to south-east Europe and Latin America, limited by those countries' purchasing power (pp. 78-79).
- The New Plan stopped the drain; by the third quarter of 1935 import volume matched the 1932 trough while industrial output was almost double (p. 80).
- The organisations created in 1934 were still at the heart of the war economy ten years later (p. 82).
- The Reichsbank ran the trade of a major economy on about one week's reserve cover (p. 82).
- From spring 1934 consumer-goods output hardly grew for more than two years. Textiles and clothing employed just under 20 per cent of the industrial workforce and relied on imports for 80 per cent of raw materials (pp. 83-84).
- Gestapo reports show the public more disturbed by shortages and prices in 1934 than by the Night of the Long Knives; the idea that work creation won Germans over is not borne out (pp. 84-85).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 69) Devaluation would undo the gain from creditors' devaluations. (p. 70) Debt buy-backs seemed to give a selective devaluation at creditors' expense. (pp. 70-71) By spring 1934 that scheme was failing.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 70-71, 79-80) Refusing devaluation meant import rationing by priority, and so more bureaucracy.
- **Premises**: (p. 76) American debt service and the bilateral deficit claimed about 800 million Reichsmarks a year. (pp. 76-77) After Lausanne Germany needed no American favour and risked only a deficit in a trade war.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 77) Default against the United States was low-risk provided the creditors could be divided, which the 1934 crisis achieved.
- **Premises**: (p. 83) Textile and leather materials were 26 per cent of the import bill. (pp. 70-71) Rearmament imports were protected.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 83) The consumer industries were bound to be the victims.
- **Interpretive claim, flagged as inference**: Schacht's deliberate escalation is concluded from the continued fall of reserves and the timing; Tooze writes that it is "hard to escape the conclusion" (p. 74), not that it is documented.

## Evidence offered

- Reichsbank internal papers and its economics department reports (devaluation estimates, emigration transfer accounts, subsidy costs) (pp. 68, 69, 81). Institutional and self-justifying, but internal.
- Trade statistics by partner (pp. 76, 78); Figures 2 to 5 did not extract.
- Schmitt's and Krosigk's later recollections of meetings with Hitler and Blomberg (pp. 71-72): post-war testimony by losers of the argument.
- The Hamburg journal Wirtschaftsdienst as a window on the suppressed devaluation debate (pp. 72-74).
- Gestapo regional mood reports for public opinion (pp. 84-85): the regime's own surveillance, likely to register grumbling.
- 1933 census employment figures for textiles and clothing (p. 83).

## Quotes

- "Between March and September 1934 the Nazi regime suffered the closest thing to a comprehensive socio-economic crisis in its entire twelve-year history." (p. 63)
- "From 1934 onwards the lack of foreign exchange was to become the central obstacle to a coherent policy of forced emigration." (p. 68)
- "It could not have both." (p. 71)
- "The system survived because it worked." (p. 82)
- "The means to do so would be divisive bilateral diplomacy abroad and authoritarian organization at home." (p. 76)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Explains the December 1933 decision to end civilian work creation funding described in ch02 (p. 71).
- The contradiction between emigration policy and foreign exchange (pp. 67-68) returns in ch08 and ch09 with the events of 1938.
- The control apparatus (agencies, levy, cartels, price controls) is the administrative base for ch04 on business and for the war economy chapters (p. 82).
- The squeeze on consumption (pp. 83-84) is the background for ch05.
- Schacht here is the driver of rearmament finance; his later role as a brake (ch07) needs to be read against this.
- Tension: the New Plan is called a remarkable organisational achievement (p. 82) and also a corset rescued by outside factors, chiefly world recovery and British compliance (p. 81).

## Open questions

- How large was the effect of the anti-Nazi boycott on exports? Tooze says it is hard to assess (p. 67).
- No record survives of the Bayreuth meeting that gave Schacht the ministry (p. 75).
- Would devaluation have worked? The chapter presents it as the liberal alternative but does not estimate its effect beyond the Reichsbank's cost-of-living figures (p. 69).
