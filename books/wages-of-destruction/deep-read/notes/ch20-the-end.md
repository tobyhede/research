# ch20: The End (pp. 513–530)

## Question this chapter answers

What does the book conclude about the Nazi economy, and what followed its destruction? Tooze summarises his findings: the regime was a highly effective mobiliser aimed at conquest; it had no coherent war plan; Hitler's escalations of 1939, 1940-41 and December 1941 follow one pattern of economic and strategic pressure read through anti-Semitic ideology; the war was lost by December 1941; and the Speer "miracle" was political. After 1945 it was Stresemann's logic, not Hitler's, that prevailed, inside a Europe whose age of great-power politics was over.

## Leading propositions

- Europe's history in the first half of the century, and Hitler's regime, can only be understood against American economic dominance and Soviet transformation (pp. 513-514).
- The coincidence of Hitler's seizure of power with America's temporary retreat in 1933 was of incalculable importance (p. 514).
- The Second Book posed modern questions about American hegemony and affluence; Hitler's answer was Lebensraum by conquest, the sustaining ambition of the regime (pp. 514-515).
- Rearmament was the determining force of economic policy from the start; work creation and consumer projects were interim measures. The military share of output rose from under 1 to almost 20 per cent between 1933 and autumn 1938, the largest and fastest peacetime shift by a capitalist state (pp. 515-516).
- That this was achieved shows an effective system with solid internal foundations, and the advantage in domestic mobilisation held through the war (p. 516).
- Business accepted state control for four reasons: the Depression broke its power; it liked domestic authoritarianism and profits; coercion was selective and initiative was used; and divided interests could be won by tactical alliances (pp. 516-517).
- Mobilisation was not part of a coherent war plan. Germany began the war with no substantial material or technical superiority; France fell through the interlocking of Allied and German plans (pp. 517-518).
- Hitler did not miscalculate in 1939. He knew his long-term preparation had failed, the balance of payments allowed no further acceleration, the pact with Stalin gave a chance, and he believed a Jewish-led coalition made war inevitable (pp. 518-520).
- The same pattern explains the turn on the Soviet Union and the declaration of war on the United States (p. 520).
- Four novel conclusions on the early war: the wars in West and East were equally ideological; there was a compelling economic case for widening the war in 1941; the 1940-41 lag in armaments is largely a statistical illusion that hides an investment wave and a real Blitzkrieg strategy; and economic motives and genocide were intertwined in the Generalplan Ost and the Hunger Plan (pp. 520-523).
- The declaration of war on the United States sealed Germany's fate; those running the war effort all thought so (pp. 523-524).
- Backe, Sauckel and Speer were Hitler loyalists, not apolitical technocrats; Speer's miracle drew on every part of the Nazi state, including the food and labour nexus and, from 1943, the SS (p. 524).
- The purpose of the miracle was political: to claim that material inferiority need not be fatal (pp. 524-525).
- It failed strategically. In 1942 the Soviet factories prevailed; by July 1943 the war was obviously lost (p. 525).
- The 1944 surge took place amid apocalyptic violence imported into the factories; Speer and the Jaegerstab kept a telephone line to the ramp at Auschwitz (pp. 525-526).
- In 1945 more than a third of German boys born 1915-24 were dead or missing; 3.8 million of 19 million dwellings were destroyed; 14.16 million ethnic Germans were expelled, of whom at least 1.71 million died (p. 526).
- Diverting nitrogen from fertiliser to explosives in 1943-4 depressed post-war harvests; rations in 1946 fell below 1,000 calories in many cities, and one estimate puts 1946 per capita GDP at about $2,200 (p. 527).
- Both Germanies paid more in reparations after 1945 than Weimar ever did (pp. 527-528).
- Within two years the Cold War made Germany needed again; the Marshall Plan and European integration followed, in line with what Stresemann had sought (pp. 528-529).
- The catastrophe closed the classic era of European politics; post-war democracy existed within a truncated statehood (pp. 529-530).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 516) The regime shifted more resources to arms in peacetime than any capitalist state. (p. 516) It sustained high mobilisation in war.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 516-517) Interpretations that picture a regime without solid foundations, or an inefficient one, are wrong.
- **Premises**: (pp. 518-519) Arms could not be accelerated in 1939 while enemies accelerated. (p. 519) The pact removed the second front and softened blockade. (pp. 519-520) Hitler held that world Jewry made war certain.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 519-520) He had nothing to gain by waiting and chose war knowingly.
- **Premises**: (pp. 523-524) After December 1941 the forces against Germany were overwhelming, as its own experts said. (p. 525) The only window, 1942, was lost to Soviet production.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 525) Later increases in output were strategically irrelevant.
- **Premises**: (p. 528) By 1947 the Western powers needed a functioning Germany. (pp. 528-529) Integration and American alliance delivered recovery.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 528-530) Stresemann's strategy was right about Germany's place in the world economy; the cost was the end of independent great-power politics.

## Evidence offered

- The chapter is a summary and cites the body of the book (Chapters 8, 9 and 16 by number) for its claims (pp. 520, 524).
- Hitler's February 1945 remark to Bormann about Roosevelt (p. 514); his and Ribbentrop's statements to the Italians in spring 1940 (p. 520).
- Post-war figures for deaths, expulsions, housing losses, rations, GDP and reparations (pp. 526-528), from secondary sources in endnotes not read here.
- General Clay's June 1945 statement and JCS 1067 on occupation policy (p. 527).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Closes the bracket opened in ch01: Stresemann against Hitler, the Second Book, America as pivot.
- Restates the argument of ch02 and ch05 on rearmament and consumption, ch04 on business, ch09 on 1939, ch11 on France, ch12 to ch14 on Barbarossa, ch15 on December 1941, ch16 on food and genocide, ch17 to ch19 on Speer.
- The "weak dictatorship" passage answers the older debate directly and ties the postponed restructuring to the Generalplan Ost of ch14.
- Tension: the conclusion says the regime was highly effective and also that its project was bound to fail. Effectiveness is measured against Germany's means, failure against its enemies' means.
- Tension: the preface withheld "punch lines" (fm01 p. 13); the conclusion claims specific novelty in four numbered points, which helps a reader test the book against prior literature.

## Open questions

- If defeat was fixed by December 1941, how much did it matter what happened in the war economy afterwards, beyond the length and cost of the war? Tooze's answer is the length and the killing.
- The closing remark that post-war European politics has shrunk to "the tiresome squabbles of discontented affluence" (p. 530) is a judgment, not an argued claim.
