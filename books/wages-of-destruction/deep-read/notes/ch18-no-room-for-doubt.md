# ch18: No Room for Doubt (pp. 460–488)

## Question this chapter answers

What happened to the war economy and its leadership in 1943, when Allied material superiority made itself felt everywhere? Tooze's answer: Stalingrad, Tunisia and the Atlantic defeat were followed by the RAF's Battle of the Ruhr, which stopped the armaments boom in mid-1943. With defeat visible, Speer did not waver. He allied with Himmler, used police coercion to keep production and morale in line, and staked his ministry on "miracle" weapons that could not change the outcome.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adolf Hitler Panzer Programme | p. 463 | Tank drive announced 22 January 1943 with total-war rhetoric and extreme priority; tanks never exceeded 7 per cent of armaments output. |
| Battle of the Ruhr | p. 466 | RAF campaign from 5 March 1943, five months and 34,000 tons of bombs on the coal and steel heartland. Tooze calls it a turning point underrated by post-war accounts. |
| Zulieferungskrise | p. 467 | The sub-components crisis of summer 1943: shortages of parts, castings and forgings spreading from the Ruhr through the whole armaments complex. |
| Zitadelle | p. 468 | The Kursk offensive of July 1943: the last German offensive in the East, failed at the outset against better than 2:1 odds in armour. |
| Speer-Himmler axis | pp. 472-473 | The alliance formalised in 1943: SS oversight of plant security, SD checks on civilian production, joint appearance at Posen. |
| Posen, 6 October 1943 | p. 473 | Gauleiter meeting at which Speer demanded closure of civilian production and Himmler spoke openly of the murder of the Jews. |
| Technical fix | p. 478 | The hope for a wonder weapon; exaggerated expectation followed by disillusion, caused by Germany's hopeless position, not by a defective "system". |
| Mark XXI U-boat | p. 479 | Advanced submarine rushed into sectional mass production under Merker; 80 delivered by end 1944, none fit for operations. |
| A4 (V2) | p. 484 | Ballistic missile programme given top priority in July 1943; the regime's largest single arms project at 2 billion Reichsmarks. |
| Mittelwerke / Dora | pp. 486-487 | Underground rocket and jet factory in Thuringia built and run with concentration camp labour under Kammler. |

## Leading propositions

- Allied material superiority first told in North Africa: at El Alamein almost 1,000 tanks faced 123 modern German ones; the November 1942 landings projected 63,000 men across the Atlantic (pp. 460-461).
- At Stalingrad the Red Army had about 2:1 superiority on the southern front and had mastered concentration; 250,000 Wehrmacht soldiers were lost, and 290,000 more in Tunisia (pp. 461-462).
- From August 1942 Allied shipping capacity was growing despite U-boat sinkings of over 500,000 tons a month; American yards built over a million tons a month. Doenitz withdrew on 24 May 1943 (pp. 462-463).
- By end May 1943 armaments output was almost 120 per cent above its level at Speer's appointment; half of the gain was the Luftwaffe's (p. 463).
- The tank programme doubled tank output by May 1943 but distorted priorities; aircraft production was at least five times more important by value. Milch built 2,200 combat aircraft in May 1943 against 1,270 tanks (pp. 464-465).
- Steel output passed 2.7 million tons in March 1943 and plans assumed 3 million by late 1944 (pp. 465-466).
- The Ruhr raids cut steel output by 200,000 tons and left a 400,000 ton shortfall against plan. Ammunition growth fell to 20 per cent in 1943 after doubling in 1942. Aircraft output did not rise between July 1943 and March 1944 (p. 467).
- Bomber Command stopped Speer's miracle; its switch to Berlin in late 1943 was a tragic operational error that postponed the collapse by a year (pp. 467, 470).
- At Kursk Germany fielded 777,000 men and 2,451 tanks and assault guns against 1.3 million men and 5,128 vehicles; artillery odds were over 4:1 (pp. 467-468).
- Hamburg, 28 July 1943: 40,000 dead, 900,000 fled. With Mussolini's fall it convinced even committed Nazis the end was near; the SD found no senior industrialist who believed in victory (pp. 469-470).
- The regime answered with terror at home: about a hundred death sentences a week for defeatism and sabotage in 1943, including executives (pp. 470-471).
- On 29 July 1943 Speer told the Zentrale Planung that unless the raids stopped they might as well shoot themselves; Jeschonnek did, and Milch declared the war lost. Speer used the crisis to absorb the civilian economy (pp. 471-472).
- Kehrl asked Speer what the strategic purpose of the war effort now was; Speer refused to answer and threatened him with a charge of desertion (p. 472).
- At Posen Speer admitted output was rising only 3 to 5 per cent a month (in fact not at all), conceded qualitative inferiority, and invoked the SD against uncooperative firms and Gaue (pp. 473-474).
- Himmler's speech equated the clearing of the Warsaw ghetto with the comb-out of consumer industry and bound the audience in shared responsibility; Tooze judges Speer almost certainly present and in any case informed (pp. 475-478).
- Mass murder in the East was an open secret among those running the war economy (p. 477).
- The Mark XXI programme was an expensive fiasco: hull sections deviated by up to 3 centimetres, boats needed 120 days of repairs after 175 of construction. The dockyard "traditionalists" were right, and the ministry replied with coercion, including the Gestapo arrest of Deschimag's director (pp. 482-484).
- Speer's chosen miracle weapons were offensive and so strategically irrelevant; Germany needed defence (p. 485).
- The Me 262 was not delayed by Hitler's or Milch's meddling. The engine was not ready for series production before summer 1944; the post-war story was built by Messerschmitt, Heinkel and Galland (pp. 485-486).
- Speer inspected the Mittelbau tunnels on 10 December 1943, saw the dying workforce, and a week later congratulated Kammler. V2 output passed 600 a month from September 1944 (pp. 487-488).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 467) The Ruhr supplied coking coal, steel and components to all industry. (p. 467) Sustained bombing cut output just as plans required a new surge.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 467) Strategic bombing in 1943 halted armaments growth; the Allies then failed to press the advantage (p. 470).
- **Premises**: (pp. 470-471) Belief in victory had gone. (p. 472) Speer's authority rested on the miracle.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 472-473) With persuasion exhausted, Speer turned to the repressive apparatus and to Himmler.
- **Premises**: (p. 478) Germany's position was hopeless. (pp. 478-486) Promising designs were rushed into production untested and under material constraint.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 478) The failures of the wonder weapons reflect the strategic situation, not a peculiar irrationality of the Nazi technological system.

## Evidence offered

- Orders of battle for El Alamein, Stalingrad and Kursk; shipping loss and construction figures (pp. 460-463, 468).
- Steel output and allocation, the armaments index (Figure 22, not extracted), ammunition growth rates (pp. 465-467).
- Speer's statements to the Zentrale Planung (29 July 1943) and his Posen speech, quoted at length (pp. 471, 473-475).
- Surviving excerpts of Himmler's 6 October 1943 speech, compared with his speech two days earlier (pp. 475-476). Speer's absence rests on later affidavits by Rohland and the hall manager, which Tooze calls self-serving.
- Kehrl's memoir for his exchange with Speer and his reaction to Hamburg (pp. 470, 472).
- SD mood reports for summer 1943 (p. 470).
- Walther Blohm's diary and shipyard records for the Mark XXI conflict (pp. 483-484).
- Speer's post-war testimony about Kammler and his own memoir and office diary for the Mittelbau visit; his letter to Kammler (pp. 487-488).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Supplies the halt of mid-1943 announced in ch17 (p. 434) and shows the second face of Speer.
- The camp labour system of ch16 becomes the basis of the underground factories.
- The Luftwaffe's quality problem (ch13, ch17) reappears in Speer's Posen admission.
- The "technical fix" motif runs from the aircraft industry in ch04 (p. 109) to the wonder weapons here.
- The alliance of regime and industry described in ch17 frays in shipbuilding and holds in rockets and jets.
- Tension: Tooze defends German engineering against the charge of systemic dysfunction while cataloguing failed programmes; his resolution is that failure followed from time pressure and scarcity.

## Open questions

- Was Speer in the hall for Himmler's speech? Tooze thinks it far more likely than not (pp. 475-476) but it is not proven.
- Could continued attacks on the Ruhr in late 1943 have collapsed the war economy a year earlier? Tooze asserts it (p. 470); the counterfactual is not tested.
