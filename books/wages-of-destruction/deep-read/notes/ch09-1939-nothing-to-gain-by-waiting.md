# ch09: 1939: Nothing to Gain by Waiting (pp. 227–259)

## Question this chapter answers

Why did Hitler choose a general European war in September 1939? Tooze's answer: the vast arms plans made after Munich collapsed within months for want of finance and foreign exchange, and armaments output slumped in 1939 while Britain, France and America accelerated. Germany's relative strength would only fall. The pact with Stalin opened a brief chance. Given Hitler's belief that war with a Jewish-led Western coalition was inevitable, striking at once was, in his terms, logical.

## Leading propositions

- By October 1938 the Reichsbank admitted that inflation had begun; state "non-marketed" output was about 30 per cent of industrial production (pp. 227-228).
- After Munich Hitler ordered preparation for war against England, target 1942 (p. 229).
- For a few weeks in late 1938 the regime came as close as it ever did to a coherent medium-term strategy: a four-year arms plan plus an alliance system with Poland, Italy and Japan (pp. 233-234).
- The plans were unrealisable. A fleet of 21,000 aircraft exceeded what any European power fielded in the war; the fuel alone would have required purchases at twice world output (pp. 234-235).
- The Wehrmacht's plans implied about 30 billion Reichsmarks a year, 30 per cent of 1938 national income (p. 238).
- Hitler dismissed Schacht and the Reichsbank directors in January 1939 and in June removed all legal limits on money creation. Money in circulation doubled in two years (pp. 238-240).
- What stopped the arms drive was again the balance of payments: by January 1939 the Reichsbank had no reserves and export orders were falling (p. 240).
- The Wehrmacht's steel ration was cut from 530,000 to 300,000 tons a month. Machine-gun targets fell from 61,000 to 13,000, the medium tank programme was halved, and ammunition stocks covered fourteen days of heavy fighting (pp. 241-242).
- Luftwaffe plans shrank through 1939; copper allocation fell to 20 per cent of requirement (pp. 242-243).
- Prague in March 1939 produced the British guarantee to Poland, a 25 per cent American punitive tariff, and the prospect of encirclement (pp. 243-245).
- Hitler was kept closely informed of the slump in arms output and asked for forecasts to 1940 (pp. 248-250).
- Germany nonetheless had Europe's largest combat-ready army and best air force; it was ready for a short war and certain to beat Poland (pp. 250-251).
- Hitler's own economic justifications for war (22 August 1939 to the generals; March 1940 to Mussolini) should be taken seriously, not dismissed as rationalisation (pp. 251-252).
- American neutrality revision failed in July 1939, and Japan and Italy would not commit; the Soviet pact broke the encirclement and, in the words of the army's quartermaster, saved Germany (pp. 253-255).
- There was no crisis in the Third Reich in 1939; what failed was the post-Munich strategy (p. 256).
- The pact abandoned both Mein Kampf's blueprint and the post-Munich anti-Western strategy, and Stalin expected the capitalist powers to exhaust each other (pp. 256-257).
- Windows of opportunity explain timing, not why war was held inevitable. That comes from ideology: Hitler read the Anglo-American alignment, which contradicted the Second Book, as the work of world Jewry (p. 258).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 241-243) German arms output was falling in 1939 for lack of imports. (p. 247) The democracies were already outspending the Axis with America barely engaged. (p. 248) British aircraft output had caught up.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 252, 256) Germany's lead would shrink; if war was coming, sooner was better.
- **Premises**: (p. 252) Hitler knew the state of production. (pp. 251-252) He gave these reasons at the time.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 252) His stated reasoning was his real reasoning.
- **Premises**: (p. 258) Conventional strategists found the Western coalition natural and counselled delay. (p. 258) Hitler found it explicable only as a Jewish conspiracy bent on Germany's annihilation.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 258-259) Ideology converted an unfavourable balance into a reason to attack.
- **Stated limit**: Tooze concedes that the rational choice in 1939 was to step back (p. 257); his explanation is of Hitler's choice, not a defence of it.

## Evidence offered

- Reichsbank drafts and the 7 January 1939 petition; the Reichsbank-RWM exchange after the failed loan (pp. 227-228, 236-238).
- Thomas's diary entry on Munich and his 24 May 1939 lecture (pp. 229, 247).
- Brauchitsch's letters and the army procurement office's forecasts supplied to Hitler's adjutant (pp. 248-250). Tooze claims this slump is "fully revealed for the first time" here (p. 252). Figures 10 and 11, which carry the series, did not extract.
- Hitler's speeches of 30 January, 1 April and 22 August 1939, his March 1940 letter to Mussolini, Speer's recollection (pp. 240, 245, 251-252).
- Krauch's 1939 memoranda (p. 245); Soviet supply shares for 1940 (p. 255).

- The short ammunition stocks and missing plan for the West set up ch10 and ch11.
- Soviet supplies (p. 255) become the dependency examined in ch12 and ch13.
- Tension: the chapter argues both that Hitler was "essentially correct" about the arms race (p. 252) and that his premise of inevitable war was ideological fantasy (p. 258).
