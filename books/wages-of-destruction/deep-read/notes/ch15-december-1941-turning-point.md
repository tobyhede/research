# ch15: December 1941: Turning Point (pp. 378–400)

## Question this chapter answers

What happened between June and December 1941, and why does Tooze treat it as the turning point of the regime's history? His answer: Barbarossa's premise failed by late July, when the army reached the limit of its supply system with the Red Army intact. By autumn the war economy had reached an impasse in fuel, coal, steel and money. The Soviet counter-offensive of 5 December broke Army Group Centre, and Germany declared war on the United States. Insiders concluded the war could not be won by military means.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Turning point | p. 378 | The six months in which the regime came closest to realising its vision and met strategic catastrophe. |
| Taifun | p. 382 | The drive on Moscow from early October 1941; halted by late October 100 kilometres short. |
| Steel inflation | p. 388 | The pile of 12 million tons of unfillable steel entitlements, about six months' output; the physical twin of the monetary overhang. |
| LSOe pricing | p. 386 | Cost-plus-on-capital pricing of public contracts with fixed prices, which rewarded cost cutting; Tooze denies it licensed inefficiency. |
| Hauszinssteuer prepayment | p. 385 | The 1942 one-off levy of ten years of house-rent tax, raising 8 billion Reichsmarks. |
| Bankruptcy of strategy | p. 388 | Tooze's judgment of October 1941: the plan to shift resources from army to Luftwaffe after victory met an unfinished war in the East. |
| Requirements of victory | p. 391 | The Anglo-American Victory Programme: $150 billion over two years, more than Germany spent on arms in the whole war. |
| Main Committees | p. 397 | Todt's reorganised structure of five industrial committees, set up just before his death. |
| Developmental dictatorship | p. 399 | Tooze's term for the Soviet Union: a successful forced industrialiser, not a backward colonial object. |
| Anachronism | p. 399 | His verdict on Nazi imperialism: a colonial project unaware that the nineteenth-century map of power was gone. |

## Leading propositions

- The failure of Barbarossa determined that the victims of the Holocaust numbered almost 6 million and not the 11.3 million listed by Heydrich (pp. 378-379).
- On 3 July Halder thought the campaign won; by end July all three army groups had halted at the limit of supply (p. 379).
- Halder admitted in August that the "Russian colossus" had been underestimated: 200 divisions expected, 360 counted; in fact 600 by end 1941 (p. 380).
- The Soviet Union mobilised a larger share of its resources than any other combatant in 1941-2 and produced weapons, above all the T-34, to which Germany had no answer (pp. 380-381).
- Hitler doubted as early as late July and spoke to Goebbels on 18 August of a negotiated peace with Stalin (p. 381).
- Economic objectives then took priority: Hitler turned the armour south on 21 August; Kiev yielded 665,000 prisoners and the Donetz basin (pp. 381-382).
- The euphoria of early Taifun coincided with a sharp escalation of anti-Jewish policy: deportations from Germany began on 15 October (pp. 382-383).
- By October the war economy was coming apart. Fuel oil for the navies was "catastrophic"; the Wehrmacht's economists concluded Germany could not wage war with all three services at once (p. 384).
- The Reichsbank reported in September 1941 that consumer goods supply had halved and money in circulation doubled (pp. 384-385).
- The regime answered with higher corporation tax (to 55 per cent) and the house-tax prepayment, which lifted tax cover of spending above 54 per cent in 1942 and halted the inflationary spiral for a time (pp. 385-386).
- Coal was about 40 million tons short across the Grossraum; steel output had to be planned at 1.65 instead of 2 million tons a month (pp. 387-388).
- On 25 October 1941 the army's steel ration was cut to 173,000 tons a month, the lowest since before May 1938, just as it needed to re-equip (p. 388).
- Hitler then told the services to ignore rationing altogether: strategic coherence in armaments was abandoned (pp. 388-389).
- Zhukov attacked on 5 December with 1.1 million men. The winter crisis cost the Germans over 700,000 men; Moscow could have been worse than Stalingrad had Stalin not attacked along the whole front (pp. 389-391).
- Hitler's declaration of war on the United States was another calculated gamble against time: he held war with America inevitable and needed Japan committed (pp. 392-394).
- On 12 December Hitler told party leaders that the world war had come and the destruction of the Jews must follow (p. 393).
- Udet shot himself on 17 November; Fromm thought peace necessary on 24 November; on 29 November Todt and Rohland told Hitler the war could no longer be won militarily (pp. 395-397).
- Todt died in an air crash on 8 February 1942; Hitler chose Speer, a loyalist from outside the war economy (pp. 397-398).
- Fromm proposed concentrating on Army Group South and the Caucasus oil; the aim was now a base for a long war, not victory at a blow (p. 398).
- Germany set the atomic bomb aside as too long-term; Britain and America pursued it. That gap measures the difference in resources (pp. 398-399).
- What the Wehrmacht met in Russia was a successful developmental dictatorship; its own advance exposed Germany's partial modernisation (pp. 399-400).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 379) The plan needed the Red Army destroyed within 500 kilometres. (pp. 379-380) Supply halted the advance while Soviet reserves kept forming.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 380) Barbarossa rested on a misjudgment of Soviet capacity; Tooze calls the assumption a colonial presumption (p. 379).
- **Premises**: (p. 384) Armaments had been re-pointed at the Luftwaffe on the assumption of victory. (pp. 387-388) Coal and steel were short. (p. 388) The army needed more, not less.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 388) By October 1941 the war-fighting strategy as a whole was bankrupt.
- **Premises**: (p. 392) Hitler held war with America inevitable and Jewish-directed. (pp. 392-393) Japan's attack tied down Anglo-American forces.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 394) Declaring war on the United States fits the same pre-emptive logic as 1939 and 1941.
- **Premises**: (p. 399) Nazi conquest was tied to settlement and modernisation plans. (p. 399) Its means were crude beside Soviet armour and Allied science.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 399-400) Nazi imperialism was anachronistic, not atavistic; Germany lacked the time and resources for the first step.

## Evidence offered

- Halder's and Bock's diaries; Panzer Group Three's war diary (pp. 379-380, 390).
- Goebbels's diary for Hitler's August talk of peace and the 12 December address (pp. 381, 393).
- Reichsbank economics department report of 17 September 1941 (pp. 384-385); Finance Ministry tax measures (pp. 385-386).
- OKW military-economic office records on fuel, coal and steel, including Keitel's August meetings (pp. 384, 387-389).
- Casualty figures for the winter of 1941-2 (pp. 390-391).
- Rohland's and Kehrl's later accounts of the 28-29 November meetings with Todt and Hitler (pp. 395-396). These are post-war memoir testimony from participants with an interest in appearing clear-sighted; Halder's contemporaneous note on Fromm (p. 396) corroborates the mood.
- Speer on Fromm and the atomic project (p. 398).

## Open questions

- Whether Hitler decided in October 1941 on the murder of all European Jews remains unclear, Tooze says (p. 382).
- There is no record of Todt's last conversation with Hitler; the claim of a quarrel is unsubstantiated and the assassination theory is not supported by evidence (pp. 397-398).
- How real was Hitler's August 1941 interest in peace with Stalin? It rests on Goebbels's diary alone (p. 381).
