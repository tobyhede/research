# ch05: Volksgemeinschaft on a Budget (pp. 113–136)

## Question this chapter answers

How rich was Germany in the 1930s, what did the regime promise its people materially, and how did it reconcile the promise with the reality? Tooze's answer: Germany was a middling economy with a low standard of living; the "people's products" mostly failed because purchasing power was lacking; and for the leadership this confirmed that affluence required Lebensraum. Rearmament can itself be read as a form of collective consumption.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Volksgemeinschaft | p. 113 | The racial community, which the regime promised a style of life fitting its supposed racial worth. |
| Purchasing power parity comparison | p. 114 | Colin Clark's 1938 method; it put German per capita income at half the American and at least a third below the British level. |
| Fordism | p. 115 | Conveyor assembly, special-purpose machines, vertical integration, semi-skilled labour, high effort for high wages, a standardised cheap product. |
| Productivism / rationalization | p. 119 | The growth-through-productivity outlook, present in the Third Reich but not what was distinctive about its economic thinking. |
| Deproletarianization | p. 120 | Upward movement inside the blue-collar skill hierarchy through state-backed training; 30,000 unskilled male school leavers in 1939 against 200,000 in 1934. |
| Volksprodukte | p. 121 | State-backed standardised consumer goods (radio, car, apartment, refrigerator, tractor) meant to deliver a jump in living standards by political will. |
| Volksempfaenger (VE 301) | p. 122 | The people's radio, 76 Reichsmarks, launched August 1933; the one success. |
| VW saving scheme | p. 128 | DAF scheme: at least 5 Reichsmarks a week, no interest, forfeiture on withdrawal; 275 million Reichsmarks collected, no car delivered to a civilian. |
| Volkswohnung | p. 132 | No-frills urban flat of 34 to 42 square metres promoted by the Labour Ministry from 1935. |
| Guns or butter | p. 134 | The usual framing, which Tooze calls partly true and partly misleading: guns were the means to butter. |
| Collective mass-consumption | p. 134 | Tooze's suggestion for how rearmament was experienced: a public spectacle and a shared possession of industrial modernity. |

## Leading propositions

- Hitler had a conventional aim of national affluence alongside the apocalyptic one, and rejected Bolshevik frugality (p. 113).
- German poverty relative to America and Britain was deep-seated, not a Depression effect (p. 114).
- In late-twentieth-century terms German per capita income in 1935 was about $4,500, in the range of South Africa, Iran and Tunisia today (p. 114).
- America's manufacturing productivity lead was more than 2:1 generally and 4:1 or 5:1 in cars and radios (p. 116).
- Germany's shortfall against Britain came not from industry, where firms were closely matched, but from an inefficient farm sector of over 9 million workers and a tail of small workshops (p. 116).
- In 1936, at full employment, 62 per cent of taxpayers had incomes under 1,500 Reichsmarks a year; food, drink and tobacco took 43 to 50 per cent of working-class budgets (pp. 117-118).
- Matching the Detroit standard in Frankfurt or Berlin would have needed 5,380 to 6,055 Reichsmarks a year (p. 119).
- After two decades of crisis, doubt about liberal progress was widespread and not confined to the radical right; economists offered little optimism about long-run growth (p. 120).
- For Hitler growth could not be assumed; conquest of Lebensraum was the way to raise per capita income (pp. 120-121).
- The radio succeeded (penetration almost doubled 1934-42, half of households by late 1938 against 68 per cent in Britain and 84 per cent in the United States), though an open economy might have done better (pp. 122-123).
- Cars stayed a minority good: 486,001 in 1932, 1.271 million in 1938. Petrol at 39 Pfennigs a litre was a political price, doubled by taxes and compulsory alcohol admixture, and it could not be cut because of the synthetic fuel programme and revenue needs (pp. 123-125).
- Opel, owned by General Motors, drove the actual growth in car ownership (p. 125).
- The Volkswagen could not be built for 990 Reichsmarks; at 250,000 cars a year unit cost exceeded 2,000 Reichsmarks. Savers averaged 4,000 Reichsmarks income; blue-collar workers were about 5 per cent (p. 129).
- The regime ended Weimar's direct housing subsidy: the public share of housing finance fell from 42.4 per cent to 8 per cent by 1936 (pp. 131-132). Only 117,000 Volkswohnungen were started in 1935-9 against a target of 300,000 a year (p. 133).
- The failures did not shake Nazi ideology; they were what the doctrine of inadequate living space predicted (pp. 133-134).
- By 1938 military spending was 20 per cent of national income, enough to fund any housing programme (p. 134).
- Rearmament was a popular spectacle as much as a burden on living standards (p. 135).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 117-118) Most households had almost no discretionary income. (pp. 124-125, 129, 133) Each people's product cost more than its target buyers could pay, and policy priorities (fuel autarchy, rearmament finance) raised costs further.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 133) The regime could not resolve the contradiction between its aspirations and actual purchasing power.
- **Premises**: (p. 120) Germans in 1933 had no grounds, from their own experience, to expect steady growth. (p. 122) Political will was credited with ending unemployment.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 122) The people's products programme was not simply irrational; it must be understood in the terms of the time.
- **Premises**: (p. 134) The leadership saw guns as the route to butter through conquest. (pp. 135-136) Conscription, military display and weapons work gave millions a share in industrial modernity.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 134) Guns or butter is a misleading opposition. Tooze marks the consumption reading as a suggestion; the subject "has not been properly researched" (p. 135).

## Evidence offered

- Clark's 1938 income comparison and Maddison's later figures (p. 114); Table 3 did not extract.
- Reich Statistical Office income tax and household budget data, 1936 retail prices (pp. 117-118).
- The 1929 Ford/ILO study of what Detroit living standards would cost in European cities (pp. 118-119).
- Institut fuer Konjunkturforschung 1938 study of car running costs (p. 124).
- Production, sales and cost figures for the radio and VW projects; a 1942 survey of VW savers (pp. 122-123, 129).
- Hitler's speeches at the motor shows and the Second Book (pp. 123, 127, 134).
- For the consumption reading of rearmament: observers' reports on conscription, labour historians' findings, a wartime Tiger tank manual (pp. 135-136). This is thinner than the statistical evidence earlier in the chapter.

## Quotes

- "In today’s league table of economic development, the Third Reich would rank alongside South Africa, Iran and Tunisia." (p. 114)
- "What determined the actual cost of petrol in the Third Reich was politics." (p. 125)
