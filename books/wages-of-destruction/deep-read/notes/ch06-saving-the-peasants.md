# ch06: Saving the Peasants (pp. 136–163)

## Question this chapter answers

Why did land, food and the peasantry matter so much to National Socialism, and what did the regime do about them in peacetime? Tooze's answer: agrarian ideology was a response to a real social fact (a huge, poor farm population in a land-short country exposed to a global food market), not an atavism. Darré and Backe protected the middle peasantry with the hereditary farm law and replaced the food market with the Reichsnaehrstand. They could not make Germany self-sufficient, and that failure pointed to conquest in the East.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land hunger / land shortage | pp. 136, 144 | A concrete condition: in land per farmer Germany resembled Ireland, Bulgaria or Romania, not Britain or America. |
| Society in transition | p. 138 | Germany in 1933: 9.342 million people (almost 29 per cent of the workforce) in agriculture, 56.8 per cent living in communities under 20,000. |
| Blut und Boden | p. 150 | Darré's doctrine that the German race is rooted in peasant farming and dies in cities; its enemy is the "nomadic" Jew. |
| Agrarian fundamentalist | p. 142 | Tooze's label for Darré: aiming at rebirth, not regression, and convinced his racism was scientific. |
| Stage view of history | p. 143 | Backe's addition: nineteenth-century liberal capitalism was a necessary phase now to be superseded; free trade is the outdated doctrine. |
| Reichserbhofgesetz / Erbhof | p. 149 | Law of September 1933 creating hereditary farms of about 7.5 to 125 hectares: unsaleable, unmortgageable, inherited whole by one male heir of German stock. |
| Ackernahrung | p. 149 | A farm large enough to support a family, later set at about 7.5 hectares; 20 hectares became the ideal (p. 153). |
| Reichsnaehrstand (RNS) | p. 153 | Compulsory organisation of all farms and food processing from autumn 1933; sets prices and delivery quotas. Over 25 per cent of GDP and 40 per cent of the workforce (p. 154). |
| Battle for Production (Erzeugungsschlacht) | p. 156 | Campaign from late 1934 to raise domestic output and cut imported feed. |
| Political freezing of prices | p. 159 | The refusal after 1934 to let food prices rise, which created apparent shortages and covert rationing. |
| Price scissors | p. 160 | The gap between industrial and farm prices, which closed sharply in agriculture's favour after 1933. |

## Leading propositions

- European expansion overseas was driven by land hunger and remade the world food system; Nazi agrarianism should be read against that history (pp. 136-137).
- The search for territory and resources was a common European preoccupation for two centuries, not an eccentric obsession of racists (p. 137).
- Hunger was a living memory: the wartime blockade was blamed for at least 600,000 deaths, and famine in the East in the early 1920s killed perhaps 5 million (p. 138).
- Nazi radicalism lay in refusing to treat the distribution of land and resources left by earlier imperial wars as final, or to accept Germany as a medium-sized workshop economy dependent on imported food (pp. 138-139).
- Darré's organisation won the countryside for the party; he shaped the SS through its clan office, and the agrarians and the SS formed one milieu. This link explains how food and peasant policy could produce murderous policies (pp. 140-141).
- Backe was as much an ideologue as Darré; he would later cooperate with Himmler in genocide (pp. 142-143).
- In 1933, 0.2 per cent of farms (7,000 estates over 500 hectares) held almost 25 per cent of farmland; 74 per cent of farms held 19 per cent (p. 144).
- Taking 20 hectares as adequate, 88 per cent of the farm population, 12 million people or 18 per cent of all Germans, were land-poor (p. 146).
- Internal land reform could not solve this: breaking up every estate over 500 hectares would yield only 500,000 farms of 20 hectares, and all German farmland shared equally gave 13 hectares per family (pp. 146-147).
- The Nazi agrarians knew this, and their scepticism about internal settlement set them apart; Darré's 1932 report already assigned the SS to eastern colonisation after demographic "rearrangement" (pp. 147-148).
- The Erbhof law was the most distinctively Nazi economic measure of the early years. Schacht, Schmitt and the Prussian Justice Ministry opposed it; Hitler's approval decided it; the collective debt relief was dropped (pp. 150-152).
- Peasants resented the loss of testamentary freedom and credit; the law was softened in practice except at inheritance (p. 152).
- The RNS ended the free market in food (p. 153).
- Food price rises in 1933-4 caused unrest that outweighed the propaganda gain from falling unemployment, and afterwards prices were politically frozen (pp. 155-156, 159).
- Allowing for reduced imported inputs, domestic farm output rose 28 per cent between 1927 and 1936; imported protein feed was halved (pp. 157-158).
- Grain stocks fell from 3.5 million tons in early 1934 to under 700,000 by summer 1936; by 1939 they covered a year's bread supply (pp. 158-159).
- At no point were Germans threatened with real food shortage; the shortages came from surging demand at frozen prices (p. 159).
- Farm prices were at least twice world prices, and farm income rose almost 14 per cent in 1933-4 and 11.5 per cent in 1934-5 (p. 160).
- The RNS's difficulties came from the balance of payments constraint, not from Darré's ideology or incompetence (p. 161).
- RNS agronomists put the land needed for self-sufficiency at 7 to 8 million additional hectares on top of 34 million (p. 162).
- In early 1936 Darré told RNS advisers that the area to the Urals was Germany's natural settlement space and that Europe would look different within ten years (pp. 162-163).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 144) Germany was land-poor relative to its farm population. (pp. 146-147) Redistribution within Germany could not give viable farms to a larger rural population. (p. 139) Dependence on imported food meant vulnerability to blockade.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 147-148) Within Nazi premises, only new land in the East could secure the peasantry and the food supply; agrarian ideology explains the regime's militancy.
- **Premises**: (p. 156) Urban price sensitivity froze food prices. (p. 161) Wages and industrial prices were held down to protect competitiveness. (p. 161) Foreign exchange for feed was cut.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 161) A gap between consumer demand and farm output was unavoidable, whoever ran the RNS.
- **Premises**: (p. 160) Protected prices at twice world levels; closing price scissors; rising farm income.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 160) Peasant grumbling is a poor guide; farmers received unprecedented protection in exchange for control.
- **Tooze's own caution**: he calls it possibly "far-fetched" to say farm difficulties drove radicalisation, and rests the link on the fact that Hitler gave Lebensraum an agricultural meaning (p. 162).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Develops ch01's claim that farmers, not big business, were the interest most responsible for January 1933 (ch01 p. 36).
- The farm sector as the cause of low German income is stated in ch05 (p. 116).
- Schacht's quarrel with Darré over foreign exchange (p. 156) is another front of the 1934 crisis in ch03.
- Backe, the grain balance and the SS connection are the roots of the Hunger Plan and Generalplan Ost in ch14 and ch16.
- The long shadow of the 1934 price unrest (p. 156) parallels the regime's fear of a repeat of 1918.
- Tension: Tooze presents Nazi agrarianism as a rational response to real problems and also as racist fantasy. He holds both: the problem was real, the proposed solution was genocidal.
