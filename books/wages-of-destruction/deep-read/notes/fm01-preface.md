# fm01: Preface (pp. 7–13)

## Question this chapter answers

How was the Third Reich's second bid for continental domination, and the genocide that went with it, possible, and what does economic history add to an account that has for a generation been written in terms of ideology and politics? Tooze's answer in outline: the economy has to be put back at the centre, Germany has to be seen as a middling, partly modernised economy and not an economic superpower, and the United States has to be made the pivot of the story.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two-speed historiography | p. 8 | The state of the field: racial policy and social history transformed over twenty years, the economic history of the regime barely moved since the first post-1945 generation. |
| Myth of German economic superiority | pp. 9-10 | The shared assumption of historians that Germany was an economic superpower in waiting, comparable only to the United States. Tooze's starting point is to challenge it. |
| Convergence | p. 10 | The master-narrative of twentieth-century European economic history: progressive convergence on a norm set by Britain, not Germany. |
| Partially modernized society | p. 10 | Germany under Hitler: middling per capita income, modest consumption, upwards of 15 million people living from handicrafts or peasant agriculture. |
| America as pivot | p. 11 | The United States as both the motive for Hitler's aggression (a rising hegemon and a model of affluence) and the reason it failed. |
| Lebensraum / land grab in the East | p. 11 | A last European imperial land grab, to give Germany the self-sufficient base for affluence and for superpower competition with the United States. |
| Cumulative radicalization | p. 9 | The escalating process that culminated in the Holocaust; Tooze claims it can only be grasped fully through land, food and labour. |
| World Jewish conspiracy | p. 12 | The ideological lens through which Hitler read the balance of power, which made the American threat existential and gave his decisions their risk-taking quality. |

## Leading propositions

- Human will is the starting point, but people act under circumstances given from the past; Tooze takes the Marx aphorism as a licence for economic history that does not displace ideology (p. 7).
- The ideological turn permanently improved understanding of the regime, but the economy has been neglected, partly by deliberate rejection, partly through the inaccessibility of economic history, partly from a belief that nothing new could be said (p. 8).
- The book's first aim is to reposition economics at the centre of the regime's history; the second is to bring that history into line with the revised economic history of twentieth-century Europe (p. 9).
- Germany was not an economic superpower. In 1939 the combined GDP of the British and French empires exceeded that of Germany and Italy by 60 per cent; German per capita income was middling, comparable in present-day terms to Iran or South Africa (p. 10).
- The defining feature of the century was the eclipse of Europe by the United States: American output about a third larger than Germany's in 1870, roughly twice Imperial Germany's by 1914, almost four times the Third Reich's by 1943 (p. 10).
- This reframing makes Nazism both more intelligible and more plainly irrational (p. 11).
- Hitler's originality was to refuse a place in an order dominated by the affluent English-speaking countries and to mobilise popular frustration for a challenge to it, by conquest in the East (p. 11).
- The rearmament of the Third Reich was the largest peacetime transfer of resources ever undertaken by a capitalist state, yet it could not change the underlying balance of economic and military force (p. 12).
- Blitzkrieg was not a strategy laid down in advance; the effectiveness of the Panzer forces surprised the German leadership in 1940 (p. 12).
- Hitler went to war in September 1939 with no coherent plan for defeating the British Empire (p. 12).
- The risks Hitler ran cannot be rationalised by self-interest; ideology, specifically the belief in a Jewish world conspiracy centred on the United States, explains them (p. 12).
- There were several economic rationales for genocide in the minds of the leadership, connected to imperialism, forced labour and deliberate starvation (p. 13).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 10) Germany's income and consumption were middling by European standards and far below American levels. (p. 11) Germans were already immersed in American commodity culture while many lived three or four to a room. (p. 11) Hitler saw American hegemony coming and judged that Europe had only a few years to organise against it.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 11) Nazi aggression can be rationalised as an intelligible response to uneven development in global capitalism.
- **Premises**: (p. 12) Even an extraordinary mobilisation could not make Germany strong enough to beat Britain and the Soviet Union, let alone the United States. (p. 12) The alliance with Britain proposed in Mein Kampf never came about.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 12) The war was a gamble whose risks exceed any pragmatic calculation; only the ideological reading of the American threat as existential explains taking it.
- **Unstated premise**: that economic rationalisation and ideological explanation are complementary layers and not rivals. The preface asserts this (p. 12) and leaves the demonstration to the chapters.

## Evidence offered

- Comparative GDP and per capita income figures from the modern growth-accounting literature (pp. 10); no sources are named in the preface itself.
- The appeal to a generation of technical work by economic historians on European convergence (p. 10).
- Tooze states that he has re-examined archival and statistical evidence unquestioned for sixty years (p. 8). This is a claim about method, to be tested chapter by chapter.

## Quotes

- "The aim of this book is to start a long overdue process of intellectual realignment." (p. 8)
- "Economic history throws new light both on the motives for Hitler’s aggression and on the reasons why it failed, why indeed it was bound to fail." (p. 11)
- "America should provide the pivot for our understanding of the Third Reich." (p. 11)
- "The armaments programme of the Third Reich was the largest transfer of resources ever undertaken by a capitalist state in peacetime." (p. 12)
- "War was inevitable. The question was not if, but when." (p. 13)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The Stresemann alternative that frames ch01 and returns in ch20 is the foil for the claim that satellite status under America was unacceptable to Hitler (p. 12).
- The "no coherent plan" claim (p. 12) is cashed out in ch09 to ch12; the Blitzkrieg claim in ch11 and ch13.
- The promise to explain the Holocaust through land, food and labour (pp. 9, 13) is carried by ch14 and ch16.
- The Speer reassessment announced on p. 9 is ch17 to ch19.
- Tooze directs impatient readers to ch20 for a summary of key points (p. 13).
- Tension to watch: "bound to fail" (p. 11) sits beside a narrative full of contingency. How far the book is determinist about the outcome is a question for synthesis.

## Open questions

- If defeat was certain on economic fundamentals, how much explanatory work is left for military events? The preface says the 1940 victories were spectacular but inconclusive (p. 12).
- The preface names no bibliography; a full one is said to be on the author's webpage (p. 13), so secondary sources can only be checked through the endnotes.
