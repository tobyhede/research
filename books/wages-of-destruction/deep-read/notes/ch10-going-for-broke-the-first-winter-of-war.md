# ch10: Going for Broke: The First Winter of War (pp. 259–291)

## Question this chapter answers

How did the regime run its economy in the first months of war, and was this a half-hearted "peacelike" war economy? Tooze's answer: no. Cut off from most imports and facing enemies who could buy in America, Hitler staked everything on a decisive blow in 1940 and ordered resources spent without regard to later years. Output doubled between January and July 1940. The same emergency brought forced Polish labour and starvation rations in the General Government.

## Leading propositions

- Thomas, Raeder and the Luftwaffe staff all judged Germany unready for war with the West; only the army had been confident, and only about Poland (pp. 259-260).
- The Polish campaign exposed weaknesses: bomb consumption seven times production, a quarter of tanks lost or broken down, a poor showing by reserve infantry (p. 261).
- The first plan against France was a reworked Schlieffen Plan aimed at the Channel coast, with no prospect of destroying the French army (p. 261).
- In November 1939 the army leadership again came close to a coup; bad weather cancelled the 12 November attack and so probably saved the regime (pp. 261-263).
- British and French dollar assets were about $7.37 billion against a German maximum of $700 million; by summer 1940 they had over 10,000 aircraft on order in America (pp. 263-264).
- German imports fell by about 80 per cent. Within six months raw material imports were under a third of the 1932 level (p. 264).
- Hitler's wish for a quick victory came from the belief that Germany would lose a long war, not from concern for the home front. Funk, the one minister who argued for moderation, lost his powers (pp. 265-266).
- The long war strategy would have ruled out offensive action and played into Allied hands (pp. 266-267).
- Hitler dropped the Z Plan within hours of war. The navy never exceeded 15 per cent of arms spending; the Luftwaffe took at least 40 per cent for the rest of the war (p. 269).
- Aircraft and ammunition together took over two-thirds of armaments resources in the first ten months, 70 per cent in June 1940. The war economy did not lack focus (p. 271).
- The ammunition programme fits a slogging advance to the Channel, not a Blitzkrieg concept (p. 271).
- Armaments output doubled between January and July 1940, the steepest rise of the whole war, faster than anything under Speer (p. 275).
- The surge was driven by steel and copper allocations and began in February 1940, before Todt's ministry existed. Todt and industry took the credit (pp. 276, 279).
- The military share of national output rose from 20 per cent to over a third in the first year. Per capita civilian consumption fell 11 per cent, and 18 per cent by 1941 (p. 280).
- The Wehrmacht's steel share reached 55 per cent (885,000 tons a month) in early 1940, then 1.1 million tons by running down ore stocks on Hitler's express decision to spend reserves "without regard to the future" (pp. 282-283).
- German women's labour participation in 1939 (over half of those aged 15 to 60) was higher than Britain's or America's at the end of the war. The claim that the regime failed to mobilise women is wrong (p. 284).
- The workforce fell from 39.4 million to about 36 million in a year as 4 million men were drafted (pp. 283-284).
- Backe demanded a million Polish workers for agriculture; voluntary recruitment brought 200,000, so conscription of Poles aged 14 to 25 was introduced in April 1940, backed by the murder of about 4,000 Polish notables and 3,000 "criminals" (pp. 287-289).
- Rations in the General Government were set by racial and functional rank: 609 calories for urban Poles and 503 for Jews in early 1940 against over 2,600 for Germans (p. 290).
- As late as spring 1940 Hitler had no coherent strategy for victory; the plan that worked emerged late and largely by accident (p. 291).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 263-264) The Allies could draw on America; Germany was blockaded. (p. 267) A defensive long war could not be won.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 267-268) An all-out offensive in 1940 was the only course with any chance, and it required spending reserves.
- **Premises**: (p. 264) Imports fell 80 per cent. (p. 275) Arms output doubled. (pp. 280-283) Consumption, housing and non-military steel were cut hard.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 264, 280) The early war economy was one of draconian mobilisation, not business as usual.
- **Premises**: (p. 279) Output took off in February. (p. 279) Lead times in ammunition were months. (p. 279) The committee model predated Todt.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 279) The "Todt miracle" was a political myth; inputs explain outputs. This is a template for the Speer argument in ch17.

## Evidence offered

- Thomas's office diary and files recording Keitel's, Goering's and Todt's transmissions of Hitler's wishes (pp. 259, 267-268, 283). Tooze notes that much historiography leans on the "embittered" Thomas's memoirs (p. 271); he uses the contemporary files.
- Reich Statistical Office import volumes (Figure 12), armaments index (Figure 14), savings bank flows (Figure 15), rail data (Figure 13): none extracted.
- Steel allocation by user; workforce statistics by sex; housing completions (pp. 281-284).
- Comparative female participation rates for Germany and Britain (p. 284).
- Frank's statements on feeding Poles and Jews; League of Nations estimates of nutrition in the General Government (p. 290).

## Quotes

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Continues ch09: the time argument becomes explicit after September 1939.
- Argues against the Blitzkrieg-economy thesis (limited mobilisation for short wars) from the economic side; ch11 does so from the military side.
- The Todt episode prefigures the Speer myth (ch17).
- Polish labour and hunger policy are the first steps of the labour, food and genocide complex in ch14 and ch16; Backe is the link.
- The fear of 1918 (pp. 286, 289) drives food policy throughout.
- Tension: Hitler's all-out gamble worked, yet Tooze insists it lacked a strategy until February 1940. Success is credited to contingency.
