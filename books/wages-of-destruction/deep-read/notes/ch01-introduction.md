# ch01: Introduction (pp. 13–40)

## Question this chapter answers

What was the alternative to Hitler's programme in Weimar Germany, and how was it pushed aside between 1928 and January 1933? Tooze's answer: Stresemann's "Atlanticist" strategy of economic revisionism under American auspices was a real, working option until the Depression destroyed American power to hold the ring. What replaced it was a nationalist agenda of rearmament, debt repudiation and agrarian protection, already in place before Hitler; work creation was not the dividing line.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic revisionism | p. 15 | Stresemann's policy: revise Versailles through Germany's economic weight, not military force. |
| Atlanticist strategy | p. 23 | Stresemann and Schacht's bet on American financial involvement: borrow from America so that American creditors become the force pushing for reparations revision. |
| Merry-go-round | p. 18 | Germans borrow from Americans to pay Britain and France, who pay their war debts to America. |
| Reparations Agent | p. 17 | Parker Gilbert's office under the Dawes Plan, with power to halt transfers that endangered the currency; it put reparations behind private claims. Removed by the Young Plan (p. 23). |
| Second Book | p. 15 | Hitler's unpublished 1928 manuscript; adds the American threat to the eastern Lebensraum argument of Mein Kampf. |
| Lebensraum | p. 19 | Contiguous land in the East, taken by conquest, with the inhabitants removed; land, not people or markets. |
| Fordism requires Lebensraum | p. 21 | Tooze's gloss on Hitler: American mass production rests on a huge internal market and resource base, so matching it needs territory. |
| Fulfilment | p. 26 | Compliance with reparations obligations; the bedrock of respectable politics until 1932. |
| Deflation consensus | p. 31 | The backing Bruening had for austerity in his first eighteen months. |
| Exchange controls | p. 28 | The 15 July 1931 system: private foreign currency nationalised and rationed by the Reichsbank, which thereby gained control of all imports. |
| Standstill Agreement | p. 29 | August 1931 moratorium on Germany's short-term foreign credits. |
| Triple priority | p. 32 | Rearmament, repudiation of foreign debts, saving agriculture: what united the nationalist right in January 1933. |
| Umbau Plan | p. 33 | Schleicher's November 1932 plan for a 21-division standing army with 147,000 professionals: an outright Treaty breach. |

## Leading propositions

- Weimar voters faced a real choice between peaceful pursuit of prosperity and militant nationalism; an account of Hitler's economy must start from the alternative (pp. 13-14).
- Stresemann believed the world economy was the one sphere in which Germany was indispensable, and the route to the United States as counterweight to France and Britain (pp. 16-17). He remained a full-blooded nationalist; his method was more complex, not his aims milder (p. 17).
- Under Dawes, capital inflows from October 1925 to end 1928 were large enough for Germany to pay reparations without a trade surplus (p. 18). The cynical version of the strategy was to borrow so much from America that reparations could not be transferred (p. 18).
- Hitler held that history is decided by struggle for limited means of sustenance, not by labour and trade; export-led growth would recreate the coalition of 1914 (p. 19).
- In the Second Book the United States becomes a threat: its living standard is the yardstick Europeans apply, and its industrial lead comes from the size of its internal market and resources (pp. 20-21).
- Hitler's wish for a British alliance came from the American threat as well as from the eastern programme (p. 21).
- The deeper difference is in the view of history: Stresemann thought economic development had a predictable direction; for Hitler history offered no guarantees and 1918 could be the start of a collapse like that of ancient civilisations (pp. 21-22).
- In May 1928 the Nazis took 2.5 per cent and 12 seats; the SPD rose from 131 to 153 seats (pp. 22-23).
- The Young Plan disappointed (annuity cut only to just over 2 billion against a hoped-for 1.5 billion) and removed transfer protection; with Smoot-Hawley it discredited the Atlanticist strategy (pp. 23-24).
- Schacht is the "missing link" between economic revisionism and unilateral militarism: he broke with the Mueller government in December 1929 and stood beside Hitler at Bad Harzburg in 1931 (pp. 24-26, 31).
- Bruening and Luther's hands were forced in economic policy in 1930; under the gold standard and the Young Plan deflation was the only option (p. 26). Bruening's freedom lay in foreign policy, and he used it badly: battle cruisers, the Austro-German customs union, bilateral deals in the south-east, all aimed at France, then the financially strongest European power (p. 27).
- Bruening's 6 June 1931 demand to end reparations triggered the currency crisis, not the Austrian bank failure (pp. 27-28).
- Germany did not devalue after sterling left gold, chiefly because its foreign debt was in foreign currency and Washington wanted the debt serviced behind exchange controls (pp. 29-30).
- Deflation hurt more than inflation because debts stayed fixed while incomes fell; by winter 1931-2 the major banks were under state control (pp. 30-31).
- The real break between Weimar and the Third Reich was not work creation, which the Nazi party adopted only in spring 1932 and dropped as a priority in December 1933, but rearmament, debt repudiation and agricultural protection (pp. 31-32).
- After Lausanne (July 1932) ended reparations, Germany no longer needed American goodwill; it still owed 19 billion Reichsmarks abroad, at least 8.3 billion to the United States, and servicing it required a real export surplus of about 1 billion a year (pp. 34-35).
- In so far as economic interests brought Hitler to power, the group chiefly responsible was the farmers, not big business (p. 36).
- Hitler's appointment was not predetermined: the Nazi vote fell from 37.2 to 33 per cent between July and November 1932, and a cyclical recovery had begun (pp. 37-38).
- The decisive economic choices of 1933-4 concerned foreign debt, the currency and rearmament, where there can be no pretence of political innocence (p. 39).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 18) American lenders acquired a large stake in Germany. (p. 18) Reparations ranked behind private claims under the Reparations Agent. (p. 18) In 1928 Benjamin Strong, not the Germans, pressed for renegotiation.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 18) The Atlanticist strategy was working in the late 1920s.
- **Premises**: (p. 23) Hoover would not link war debts and reparations. (p. 24) American long-term lending fell from 1928 and the tariff made dollars harder to earn. (p. 31) The collapse of the American economy and Britain's exit from gold removed the assumption of an interdependent world economy.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 31) Nationalist visions that discounted global financial ties gained plausibility; this is a change in circumstances, not in German character.
- **Premises**: (p. 32) Work creation divided the January 1933 coalition; Hugenberg and Schacht opposed credit-financed measures. (p. 32) Rearmament, default and agrarian protection united it.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 32, 39) Treating work creation as the key to Nazi economic policy is an anachronism of the Keynesian debate and depoliticises the regime's economic history.
- **Counterfactual premise**: (pp. 37-39) had Hitler been kept out a few months longer, recovery and the Nazi party's decline might have prevented the dictatorship; but (p. 40) any likely German government would still have disrupted Geneva and London, and the outcome depended on other powers.

## Evidence offered

- Election results 1928-32 (pp. 22, 26, 37); reparations and debt figures (pp. 17, 23, 34); Reichsbank reserve movements in June 1931 (p. 28); trade balance 1928-31 with reference to Appendix Table A1 (p. 26).
- Hitler's Second Book, quoted at length (pp. 20-22), and Stresemann's speeches (pp. 15-16, 24). These give the two men's own statements of intent.
- Contemporary business-cycle commentary (Stolper's Der Deutsche Volkswirt, the Reichskreditgesellschaft, the Berlin institute, The Economist) for the claim that recovery had begun by late 1932 (p. 38).
- Table 1 (foreign debt position, spring 1931) did not extract as text (p. 18).
- Interpretation rests on recent secondary work on Bruening's constraints; Tooze notes the long debate and sides with the view that policy was forced in 1930 (p. 26).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Stresemann's logic returns as the victor in ch20's closing pages; the introduction and conclusion bracket the book.
- The exchange-control apparatus of July 1931 (p. 28) is the instrument the Third Reich inherits; it underlies the trade and debt politics of ch03.
- The demotion of work creation (pp. 31-32, 39) is argued in detail in ch02.
- The agrarian lobby's role (p. 36) prepares ch06.
- Tension: Bruening is absolved on economic policy (p. 26) but blamed for the foreign-policy turn that caused the 1931 crisis (pp. 27-28). The two judgments depend on separating domains that the chapter elsewhere insists are linked.
