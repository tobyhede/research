# ch17: Albert Speer: 'Miracle Man' (pp. 430–460)

## Question this chapter answers

Was there an "armaments miracle" under Albert Speer after February 1942, and was Speer an unpolitical technocrat? Tooze's answer: output really rose, but for ordinary reasons: earlier reorganisation and investment, more labour, food and money supplied by others, a huge reallocation of steel to ammunition, and quality sacrificed for quantity. The miracle was above all propaganda that kept the regime fighting by silencing the conclusion that the war was lost. In 1942 Germany was out-produced by the Soviet Union.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speer myth | p. 430 | Two linked claims: that Speer was unpolitical, and that he worked an armaments miracle. |
| Armaments miracle | p. 430 | The rise in the armaments index after February 1942, presented as a rebirth; for Tooze its main function was political. |
| Zentrale Planung | p. 436 | Central planning committee of Speer, Milch and Koerner allocating raw materials; 62 meetings, 52 of them by end 1943; the war cabinet of the economy. |
| Speer-Milch partnership | p. 436 | Speer controlled about 45 per cent of armaments until mid-1943; the Luftwaffe (35 to 40 per cent) stayed under Milch until spring 1944. |
| Main Committees and Rings | pp. 437-438 | Todt's committees for final products, run by Saur, plus Speer's new Rings for materials and components under Schieber; 249 bodies by end 1942. |
| Selbstverantwortung | p. 439 | "Self-responsibility of industry": the ministry sets targets, firms organise delivery. |
| Standard (fixed) price system | pp. 440-441 | Single prices based on a good-to-average producer, decided November 1941; Speer coupled it to generous treatment of profits. |
| Ration inflation | p. 445 | The excess of steel entitlements over steel; ended in 1942 by cancelling the backlog and capping issue at 90 per cent of output. |
| RVE | p. 445 | Reichsvereinigung Eisen, the steel organisation of 1 June 1942 under Roechling. |
| Learning curve | p. 454 | The predictable fall in unit cost as cumulative output of one aircraft model rises. |
| Soviet armaments miracle | p. 459 | The real production feat of 1942: the Soviet Union out-produced Germany in every weapon class. |

## Leading propositions

- Speer was a party member from 1931, the stage manager of the regime's rallies and one of the few men Hitler trusted. His claim to be unpolitical was absurd (pp. 430-431).
- His crucial service was to shut down the reasoning that led Fromm, Thomas and Todt from Germany's capacity to the need for a political end to the war (pp. 431-432).
- He made production "tell a story": newsreels, medals for workers, a propaganda committee with Goebbels, indices based on low points of 1941 and no absolute figures (pp. 432-433).
- The output rise was real until summer 1944 but not miraculous. Its causes: reorganisation begun before Speer, ruthless mobilisation, earlier investments maturing, and quantity bought at the cost of quality (pp. 433-434).
- The boom stopped abruptly in summer 1943 (p. 434).
- Only the first year, February 1942 to early 1943, had a plausible strategic rationale: equip Army Group South to finish the Soviet Union before Anglo-American weight told (pp. 434-435).
- Of the output increase to mid-1943 only half came from Speer's sectors; 40 per cent came from the Luftwaffe (p. 436).
- Saur, a party engineer and a bully, ran the committees; Hitler named him Speer's successor in his will (pp. 436-437).
- Industry rallied to the regime after Moscow: profits had been at record levels in 1940, and Soviet victory was the thing business feared most (pp. 437-438).
- Every one of the top hundred industrial firms of 1938 held at least one committee or ring chairmanship (p. 439).
- Speer's story that pre-1942 pricing rewarded high costs is a travesty; profit had been calculated on capital, with fixed prices and incentives to cut costs. The reform's substance was a standard price and a promise to leave profits alone (pp. 440-442).
- Others supplied the preconditions: the 1942 fiscal levies, food from the occupied territories, and 970,000 foreign workers against 280,000 lost to the draft. Speer's sector took 420,000 of 513,000 added Wehrmacht workers; the tank workforce rose almost 60 per cent (pp. 442-443).
- Thirty of the Zentrale Planung's sixty-two meetings were about steel and eleven about coal (p. 443).
- Ammunition was half of the increase in armaments in Speer's first eight months. Its steel quota went from 25,000 to 350,000 tons a month; it took over half the army's steel in late 1942 (pp. 444-445).
- Roechling told the steel industry in June 1942 that the private economy's existence depended on its performance in a "battle of the systems". Tooze reads the Speer system as an alliance of the regime and industrial capital for common survival against Stalinism (pp. 445-446).
- On 11 August 1942 Hitler told Pleiger that without more coking coal for steel the war was lost; the October coal crisis nearly broke the system and was met by cutting household coal (pp. 447-449).
- Steel reached a wartime record of 2.1 million tons a month in the old Reich in early 1943; by February 1943 the armaments index was double its level at Speer's appointment (pp. 449-450).
- Ammunition output tracks the steel allocated to it almost perfectly from 1939 to 1943, before and after Speer (p. 450).
- Rationalisation did matter in Milch's sector: aircraft output more than doubled in a year with little more labour and no more aluminium. Milch had first broken the aircraft firms' autonomy in 1941 (pp. 451-453).
- The price was obsolescence. Milch froze designs and built almost 24,000 Me 109 G fighters that became death traps (pp. 454-456).
- In 1942 the Soviet Union, with a 25 per cent smaller economy than before the invasion, out-produced Germany 3:1 in small arms and artillery, 4:1 in tanks and 2:1 in combat aircraft, at a cost of starvation at home. It was not sustainable, but by 1944 it no longer mattered (pp. 459-460).
- German and British armaments output were similar in 1942; this is a story of Soviet success, not German failure (pp. 459-460).
- Fromm asked Hitler for a negotiated peace in September 1942; Speer knew and stayed silent (pp. 458-459).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 436) Half the gain lay outside Speer's remit. (pp. 442-443) Labour, food and finance came from Sauckel, Backe and the Finance Ministry. (p. 450) Ammunition follows steel.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 433-434, 450-451) Mobilisation of inputs, not a new efficiency, explains most of Speer's rise; the pre-Speer economy was not the inefficient sink he described.
- **Premises**: (pp. 431-432) Insiders had concluded the war was unwinnable. (pp. 432-433) Production statistics were the one kind of news the regime still controlled.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 432) The miracle's main work was ideological: it let the war continue.
- **Premises**: (pp. 453-454) Mass production needs frozen designs. (pp. 455-456) Germany's frozen designs were ageing.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 455) Quantity was bought with quality; the cost appeared when American escorted bombers arrived in late 1943.
- **Premises**: (p. 459) Soviet output exceeded German in every class in 1942. (p. 460) 1942 was the last year a decision in the East was possible.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 460) Speer and Milch lost the battle of the factories when it counted.

## Evidence offered

- SD reports on audience reaction to armaments newsreels; Speer's 5 June 1943 Sportpalast figures and insiders' remarks on them (pp. 432-433).
- Workforce and steel allocation data by sector; the correlation of ammunition output with steel (Figure 20, not extracted; Appendix Table A6 cited) (pp. 442-445, 450).
- Zentrale Planung minutes, including the 23 October 1942 session and Speer's own words on self-responsibility (pp. 448-449).
- Kehrl's account of the 11 August 1942 meeting with Hitler (p. 448): memoir evidence.
- Roechling's June 1942 speech to steel industrialists (pp. 445-446).
- Speer Ministry telephone book for the list of committee chairs (p. 439).
- Messerschmitt-Regensburg hours per aircraft (Figure 21, not extracted); an American test pilot's judgment of the Me 109 G (pp. 454-456).
- Comparative armaments output for Germany, Britain, the Soviet Union and America in 1942 (pp. 459-460).

## Open questions

- How much of the post-1942 gain in labour productivity was rationalisation as against fuller use of plant once steel arrived? Tooze argues the latter for ammunition (p. 450) and leaves other sectors less quantified.
- Tooze's account relies on his reworked statistics; Figures 20 and 21 and Table A6 could not be read.
- Was the Soviet effort's unsustainability (p. 460) known to the Germans at the time?
