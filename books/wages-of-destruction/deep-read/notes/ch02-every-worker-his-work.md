# ch02: 'Every Worker his Work' (pp. 40–62)

## Question this chapter answers

What drove the German recovery of 1933-5, and what were the regime's real economic priorities in its first eighteen months? Tooze's answer: civilian work creation was inherited, short-lived and mostly propaganda. The decisions that mattered were the debt moratorium and the 35 billion Reichsmark rearmament programme, both of June 1933. The recovery was state-driven, and by 1934 the state's spending was military.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Productive credit creation | p. 44 | Financing public spending with newly created money; the disputed question was whether this could raise real output or only prices. With 6 million unemployed and industry under 50 per cent capacity, the balance was not hard to strike (p. 45). |
| Work creation bills | p. 45 | Interest-bearing IOUs paid to contractors, guaranteed by state-affiliated banks and discountable at the Reichsbank. Devised under Schleicher in December 1932. |
| Reinhardt programme | p. 44 | The 1 billion Reichsmark credit-financed work creation package approved 28 May 1933; a second, smaller programme of subsidies followed in September 1933 (p. 48). |
| Battle for Work (Arbeitsschlacht) | p. 45 | The propaganda campaign against unemployment, launched in East Prussia under Gauleiter Koch. |
| Special measures | p. 46 | Euphemism for military infrastructure (strategic roads, airfields, barracks) paid from work creation funds; 230 million Reichsmarks by 1934. |
| Moratorium | p. 51 | Unilateral suspension from 30 June 1933 of foreign-currency transfer on long-term debts; debtors paid Reichsmarks into Reichsbank accounts. |
| Clearing agreement | p. 56 | Bilateral deal (Dutch, Swiss) in which a creditor country takes German exports in return for continued debt service. |
| Mefo bills | p. 53 | Off-budget rearmament IOUs issued in the name of Mefo GmbH, a shell backed by Vereinigte Stahlwerke, Krupp, Siemens, Deutsche Industrie Werke and GHH; discountable at the Reichsbank. Large-scale issue from April 1934. |
| 35 billion programme | pp. 52-53 | Schacht's June 1933 commitment to military spending over eight years, about 4.4 billion a year, 5 to 10 per cent of GDP. |
| Natural recovery | p. 60 | The cyclical upswing in private investment visible in 1933-4 independent of policy. |
| Non-marketed production | p. 62 | Output for the state, mostly military; a quarter of industry by 1935 on contemporary estimates. |

## Leading propositions

- Within days of taking office Hitler told the generals and the cabinet that rearmament had absolute priority and that Lebensraum in the East remained the aim (pp. 40-41).
- The first work creation and rearmament spending was Schleicher's 600 million Reichsmarks, unspent in January 1933; 190 million of the Reich's 200 million share went to the military (pp. 41-42).
- The March 1933 election showed the Nazi party had reached its electoral limit short of a majority; the Enabling Law and violence did the rest, and the unions were destroyed on 2 May (p. 42).
- Schacht's affinity with the right was over foreign policy, not work creation (p. 43).
- East Prussia's success was stage-managed: a province with 1.89 per cent of the unemployed received a disproportionate share of funds, and the jobless were conscripted into camps (pp. 45-46).
- Earth-moving did little for the urban centres of unemployment (p. 46).
- The autobahns were not a work creation measure and had negligible effect on unemployment: 1,000 workers in 1933, 38,000 a year after Todt's appointment. Todt's rationale was military mobility (pp. 47-48).
- Germany could not recover without imports, and so without exports; real import volume in 1928 had been 50 per cent above 1933 (p. 50).
- Reichsbank reserves fell from over 800 million to 400 million Reichsmarks between January and summer 1933, about one month of minimal imports (p. 51).
- The moratorium of 8 June 1933 was the regime's first overtly aggressive foreign policy act (p. 51), and the World Economic Conference could not answer it because the United States, Britain and France were divided (pp. 51-52).
- The rearmament package was probably agreed at the same cabinet meeting of 8 June 1933 (p. 52). It was almost three times all civilian work creation measures of 1932-3 combined (p. 54).
- Default and rearmament belong together: Germany gave up both the American debts and the implicit American security guarantee and re-entered continental military competition (p. 54).
- The army programme of December 1933 (21 divisions and 300,000 men by end 1937, 63 divisions in war) pre-programmed conscription and the remilitarisation of the Rhineland (pp. 55-56).
- No new money was allocated to national work creation after 6 December 1933 (p. 59).
- At their peak in March 1934 work creation schemes accounted for about 30 per cent of the fall in registered unemployment (p. 60).
- In 1934 military spending was at least 4 billion Reichsmarks, over half of central government spending on goods and services; 73 per cent in 1935 (p. 59).
- Household consumption contracted in the first half of 1933 and contributed little in 1934; real wages fell (pp. 60-61).
- By 1935 real GDP was back at about the 1928 level, private consumption 7 per cent below and private investment 22 per cent below pre-Depression levels, state spending 70 per cent above 1928 (p. 62).
- Nazi policy cannot, in a strict counterfactual sense, claim to have caused the recovery (p. 62).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 59) No new civilian work creation money after December 1933. (p. 60) Schemes employed 1,075,000 at the March 1934 peak against a 2.6 million fall in unemployment. (p. 60) Numbers on schemes then fell to about 700,000.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 60) The 1934 Battle for Work added little or nothing to the continuing fall in unemployment.
- **Premises**: (p. 60) Private investment, mainly stock-building, was the largest contributor in 1933. (p. 62) The 1926-7 rebound was faster than any year of the Third Reich, and the American recovery under a different policy mix was comparable.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 62) A similar recovery is imaginable under a different regime.
- **Premises**: (p. 61) Falling consumption offset rising investment, so private demand was under half of the growth in aggregate demand in 1933 and 1934. (p. 61) Reich civilian spending partly repackaged squeezed local government spending.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 61-62) The recovery as it happened was public-sector driven, centralised, and from 1934 dominated by the military.
- **Unstated premise**: that the national accounts reconstruction in Table 2 is reliable enough to separate public from private contributions. Tooze concedes the consumption figure is inferred as a residual (p. 60).

## Evidence offered

- Hitler's own statements of 3 and 9 February 1933 to the generals and the cabinet committee (pp. 40-41).
- A retrospective Wehrmacht memorandum of 1938 for the 35 billion figure; the date of the agreement is inferred as most probable, not documented (p. 52).
- Official labour market figures for work creation employment and unemployment (p. 60); Figure 1 did not extract.
- Ritschl's national accounts reconstruction, the source for Table 2 (p. 61); the table did not extract, so the percentages in the prose are all that could be checked.
- Local studies (Northeim, Hanover, Hamburg) and a draft Propaganda Ministry programme for 21 March 1934 reproduced in full (pp. 48-49, 58-59).
- Reichsbank monthly returns, read by creditors as evidence that Schacht was running down reserves deliberately (p. 51). This is the creditors' view.

## Quotes

- "All other tasks must cede precedence to the task of rearmament" (p. 41)
- "aggressive foreign policy move by Hitler’s government" (p. 52)
- "In every respect except propaganda, the civilian work creation measures of 1933 were dwarfed by the decisions taken in relation to rearmament and foreign debt." (p. 54)
- "From December 1933 onwards, the clock was ticking towards confrontation with France." (p. 56)
- "In this strict counterfactual sense, Nazi economic policy cannot claim to have ‘caused’ the German economic recovery." (p. 61)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Delivers the argument promised in ch01 (pp. 31-32, 39) against the Keynesian reading of the Nazi recovery.
- The import and foreign-exchange constraint (pp. 50-51) is the subject of ch03 and recurs as the binding limit on rearmament in ch07 to ch09.
- The unequal treatment of creditors through clearing (p. 56) anticipates the bilateral trade system of ch03.
- Todt's road organisation is named as a seedbed of the later control system (p. 48); see ch10 and ch17.
- Tension: Tooze denies that policy caused the recovery yet says the recovery was driven primarily by the public sector (pp. 61-62). The two fit only on the counterfactual reading: some recovery would have happened anyway, but this one was the state's.
