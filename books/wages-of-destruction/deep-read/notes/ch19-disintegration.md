# ch19: Disintegration (pp. 488–513)

## Question this chapter answers

How did the war economy end, and what should be made of the last "miracle" of 1944? Tooze's answer: the surge in aircraft output under the Jaegerstab rested on earlier Air Ministry preparations, Speer's control of priority resources, and unrestrained coercion, including Jewish slave labour. It changed nothing: Allied output was three to four times larger. The economy then came apart from inside through inflation and from outside through the bombing of the Ruhr's transport links, while Speer pressed for resistance to the end.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Big Week | p. 489 | 20-25 February 1944: American raids on aircraft factories with long-range escorts; the start of unsustainable Luftwaffe attrition. |
| Jaegerstab | p. 490 | Fighter Staff formed February 1944 joining Speer's ministry, the Air Ministry and the SS under Milch and Saur, with absolute priority and no legal restraint. |
| Panzer priority / slush funds | p. 496 | Speer's reserved steel from "secret sources" and his power to confer top priority, which explain the robustness of favoured programmes. |
| Big lie | p. 499 | Tooze's phrase for Speer's August 1944 Posen statistics, which added the first week of August to July's output. |
| Staatswirtschaft | p. 502 | A full state economy run by "coercion or idealism", which Kehrl's planning office warned Germany was sliding into as money lost value. |
| Drive to substance (Drang zur Substanz) | p. 505 | Firms' flight from money into stocks, plant, shares and foreign havens as inflation loomed. |
| Silent financing | p. 503 | The savings-recycling system that kept inflation down until mid-1943 and then failed. |
| Transport offensive | p. 509 | The autumn 1944 bombing of canals, bridges and marshalling yards that cut the Ruhr off from the rest of Germany. |
| Nero order | p. 512 | Hitler's scorched-earth order of March 1945; Speer softened its execution only after their last meeting. |

## Leading propositions

- In early 1944 the Luftwaffe lost a third to a half of its fighters each month; its whole complement of fighter pilots was killed or disabled in five months (pp. 489-490).
- Aircraft output rose from 1,323 in February to 3,538 in September 1944. The armaments index went from 230 in February to 330 in July (100 at Speer's appointment); two-thirds of that rise was the Jaegerstab's (pp. 490-491).
- This late burst is the basis of Speer's reputation. In January 1944 output was 130 per cent above February 1942 and had not risen since July 1943 (pp. 490-491).
- Allied interrogators took the Speer-Saur story at face value and used Saur as a chief source (p. 491).
- The Jaegerstab worked by a 72-hour week, summary courts martial, and explicit licence to ignore law; Milch threatened to hang prisoners of war in their factories (pp. 491-492).
- The Luftwaffe used 36,000 camp inmates in early 1944 with 90,000 planned; 35 per cent of Messerschmitt-Regensburg's output came from SS subcontractors by summer 1944 (p. 493).
- The Jaegerstab broke the ban on Jewish labour in armaments. Of 509,000 Jews deported from Hungary, over 120,000 survived as forced labourers; Speer attended the meeting of 26 May 1944 at which the supply from Auschwitz was discussed (pp. 493-494).
- An Air Ministry analysis showed the surge owed most to pre-Jaegerstab measures: 560,000 workers added in 1943, aero-engine expansion, and a fighter programme of autumn 1943 that Speer and Saur had then blocked and later adopted almost unchanged (pp. 494-495).
- Speer himself admitted that capacity had been moved "on the quiet" from armour into aircraft (p. 496).
- At Linz and Berchtesgaden in June 1944 Speer had Hitler promise business a return of private initiative after victory and warn of the "merciless extirpation" of German industry in defeat (p. 497).
- Bagration destroyed Army Group Centre: 450,000 men lost and a Soviet advance of almost 600 kilometres in six weeks (p. 498).
- From 1 August 1944 the whole armaments effort was for the first time under one authority. Speer backed Goebbels and Himmler before and after 20 July (pp. 498-499).
- Armaments output fell from July 1944 and plunged in 1945; aircraft collapsed first (pp. 499-500).
- In 1944 Germany made 34,100 combat aircraft against the Allies' 127,300 (3.7:1) and 18,300 tanks against 54,100. Nothing Speer did changed the Wehrmacht's predicament; but this was not failure, since combined Allied GDP in 1941 was 4.36 times Germany's (pp. 500-501).
- The occupied territories supplied 9.3 per cent of armaments in 1943; their main contribution was labour, one in three armaments workers by 1944 (p. 501).
- The war economy was also disintegrating from within. Speer's ministry had ignored money; in July 1944 Kehrl's office warned of anarchy (p. 502).
- Monetary stability to mid-1943 was a "crucial lubricant" of the armaments miracle. In 1943 domestic war spending took 60 per cent of net national product, the highest of any combatant. Savings deposits fell in summer 1943; banknotes rose over 80 per cent between September 1944 and April 1945 (pp. 503-505).
- As money lost meaning, incentives gave way to rations in kind and police coercion (p. 505).
- Hitler vetoed tax increases in 1943 and in September 1944. Not taxing did not spare Germans the real cost of the war (pp. 506-507).
- Loss of the Ukrainian ores (February 1944) and Romanian oil set time limits; the immediate cause of collapse was bombing. Over a million tons fell between June 1944 and spring 1945 (pp. 507-508).
- Area bombing of cities correlated only loosely with lost production. Cutting the Ruhr's rail and water links did the work: by 11 November 1944 the Ruhr was sealed off, and steel output there was down 66 per cent by January 1945 (pp. 508-509).
- Speer's second memorandum of 18 March 1945, recently found, urged defence of the zone between Rhine and Weichsel to the last man for eight more weeks (pp. 510-511).
- The Wehrmacht lost 1.8 million dead in 1944 and 1.4 million in the first five months of 1945 (p. 511).
- Wagenfuehr compiled the statistics on which the standard account still rests while the collapse was under way (pp. 512-513).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 494) Aircraft take six months to build. (pp. 494-495) Workers and engine capacity were added in 1943. (p. 496) Speer redirected reserved resources in February 1944.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 495-496) The 1944 surge was no miracle of rationalisation; it followed from earlier inputs, priority and coercion.
- **Premises**: (pp. 500-501) Allied economic potential was over four times Germany's. (p. 500) Allied output in 1944 exceeded German by 2 to 4 times in every class.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 500-501) Germany met exactly the material superiority its strategists had always feared; Speer's effort was neither a failure nor able to matter.
- **Premises**: (pp. 503-504) War spending outran taxes and voluntary saving. (pp. 504-505) Households and firms fled money.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 502, 505) Blaming collapse only on external shocks repeats Speer's own story; internal monetary disintegration was under way and drove the spiral of coercion.
- **Premises**: (p. 508) City raids disrupted but did not stop production. (p. 509) Transport raids isolated the coal and steel base.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 509) Transport, not area bombing, ended industrial production.

## Evidence offered

- Kehrl's planning office armaments index; aircraft output by month (pp. 490-491).
- Milch's 25 March 1944 speech and remarks on foreign workers, quoted at length; Jaegerstab minutes of 26 May and June 1944 (pp. 491-494). These were prosecution evidence at Nuremberg.
- The Air Ministry's own summer 1944 analysis of the surge. Tooze notes it had an axe to grind and uses it against Saur's account (pp. 494-496).
- Speer's June 1944 remark to journalists and the Speer Ministry's confidential diary on reserved steel (p. 496).
- Speer's notes for Hitler's 26 June 1944 speech (p. 497).
- Comparative production and GDP data (Table 17, not extracted) (pp. 500-501).
- Kehrl's July 1944 memorandum on purchasing power; Finance Ministry revenue shares; savings and banknote series (pp. 502-505).
- Bomb tonnages, Ruhr coal and steel shipments, Speer's 11 November 1944 report to Hitler (pp. 508-509).
- Speer's memoranda of 15 and 18 March 1945; Goebbels's diary and Speer's "unreliable memoirs" for the last meeting with Hitler (pp. 510-512).

## Quotes

- "It was this sudden and late burst in aircraft production to which the Speer Ministry owes its legendary reputation." (p. 490)
- "Even in 1944 there were no miracles of rationalization." (p. 496)
- "In short, nothing that Albert Speer and his colleagues had done since 1942 had made any difference to the Wehrmacht’s fundamental predicament." (p. 500)
- "The functioning monetary system was a crucial lubricant for the armaments miracle." (p. 504)
- "As the ghastly reality of the Nazi war economy was finally being liquidated, the writing of its history had already begun." (p. 513)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Third instance of the pattern from ch10 (Todt 1940) and ch17 (Speer 1942-3): a new authority takes credit for results prepared by others.
- The halt of mid-1943 (ch18) is the baseline for the 1944 surge.
- The obsolete Me 109 (ch17 pp. 455-456) explains the fighter losses of early 1944.
- The monetary thread runs from Mefo bills (ch02) through the 1938-9 inflation warnings (ch08, ch09), silent financing (ch10) and the 1941-2 consolidation (ch15) to collapse here.
- Wagenfuehr's statistics (p. 512) are the source of the "peacetime economy at war" thesis Tooze attacks in ch13.
- The ratios of economic potential restate the preface's "bound to fail" (fm01 p. 11).
- Tension: Tooze denies "failure" (p. 500) while documenting waste, coercion and deception. He means failure relative to what was materially possible.

## Open questions

- A precise accounting of the 1944 surge is impossible, Tooze says, because of the confusion of that year (p. 495).
- How much did the late-1943 choice of Berlin over the Ruhr, and the spring 1944 diversion to Normandy, prolong the war? The chapter implies much; it does not measure it.
- There is no authentic record of the Speer-Hitler meeting of 29-30 March 1945 (p. 512).
- Table 17 could not be read.
