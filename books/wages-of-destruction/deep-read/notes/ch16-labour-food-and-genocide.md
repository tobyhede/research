# ch16: Labour, Food and Genocide (pp. 400–430)

## Question this chapter answers

After the winter crisis of 1941-2 Germany was desperately short of labour, yet it murdered millions of potential workers. How do the forced labour programme and the genocide relate? Tooze's answer: the usual picture of ideology against economic need, softened by compromise, is true as far as it goes. It leaves out food. The food crisis of 1942 gave the regime an economic motive for killing, and the murder of Polish Jews in summer 1942 was tied directly to extracting grain from the General Government to restore German rations.

## Leading propositions

- The Wehrmacht lost on average almost 60,000 men killed a month in the East between June 1941 and May 1944 (p. 400).
- More German women could not have closed the gap: the 1943 registration found 1.5 million potential workers, half part-time. In 1943 women were 34 per cent of war workers in Germany, 33.1 in Britain, 25.4 in America (pp. 400-401).
- Sauckel was a serious industrial politician, not a buffoon; Thuringia was an armaments hub (p. 402).
- Between January 1942 and June 1943 he delivered 2.8 million foreign workers, about 34,000 a week. The foreign workforce peaked at 7.907 million in autumn 1944, over 20 per cent of all workers and over a third in armaments (pp. 402-403).
- The response to the labour shortage was rational in the basic sense; nobody in Berlin questioned press-ganging Poles and Soviets even in 1944 (pp. 403-404).
- The regime mistreated the workers it imported: mass deaths of Soviet prisoners continued in Germany, and Ostarbeiter fled in tens of thousands in 1942 (pp. 405-406).
- The peak of the Holocaust in 1942 followed the manpower crisis by months. By German standards the Holocaust destroyed at least 2.4 million potential workers; with other deaths in forced labour after January 1942, at least 4.8 million, nearer 7 million including prisoners killed in 1941 (pp. 406-408).
- The SS were not blind to economics: women, children and the old were killed first, and productive remnants kept in ghettos such as Łódź until 1944. Of 1.1 million Jews deported to Auschwitz, 900,000 were killed on arrival and 200,000 kept for labour (pp. 409-410).
- Himmler wanted Jewish labour only for the construction sites of the Generalplan Ost; after Stalingrad he lost interest in keeping any alive (p. 410).
- Auschwitz remained a labour camp throughout; 400,000 people were registered as inmates, half of them Jews (pp. 410-411).
- German industry learned to manage coerced labour through beatings, camps and food. Ley and Pleiger told mine managers in October 1942 to use force: "Below ground it is dark and Berlin is a long way away" (pp. 412-413).
- Camp labour was irrational for the war economy as a whole and often a godsend for the individual employer, because the SS replaced worn-out workers (p. 415).
- From winter 1942-3 the SS raised camp rations to cut attrition; mortality fell in 1943 (p. 416).
- At the Loibl tunnel inmate productivity was 40 per cent below German labour yet still more profitable; the Reich clawed back part through a price cut of 3.515 per cent (pp. 416-417).
- At Krupp in November 1942 Soviet prisoners worked at 42 per cent of German output; by mid-1943 foreign workers' productivity had risen markedly. Employing them was the "entry ticket to the war economy" (pp. 418-419).
- The Hunger Plan predates any concrete plan for the murder of Europe's Jews and gave a starkly economic incentive for mass killing (pp. 419-420).
- German grain stocks neared exhaustion by end 1941. The ration cut of April 1942 was "devastating" for morale according to the SD (pp. 420-422).
- The Wehrmacht's economists argued that feeding 200 workers badly was worse than feeding 100 properly (p. 421).
- Darré was retired and Backe promoted. Backe told General Government officials on 23 June 1942 that the Jews would be gone within the year, and predicated grain demands on that. Himmler's orders of 18-19 July joined the killing of Polish Jews, the Zamość clearance and a ruthless harvest drive (pp. 423-425).
- On 6 August 1942 Goering told occupation chiefs that others would starve before Germans. Feeding of 1.2 million Jews in the General Government was to stop at once; by end 1942 about 300,000 Polish Jews were alive (pp. 426-427).
- Occupied Europe's grain deliveries more than doubled to over 5 million tons in 1942-3; German and foreign workers' rations were raised on 19 October 1942 (p. 428).
- Sauckel, Backe and Speer were all appointed between February and April 1942. The men who made Speer's "miracle" possible were among the regime's most ruthless ideologues (pp. 429-430).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 400-401) No domestic labour reserve existed. (p. 403) German productivity was the highest in Europe.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 403-404) Importing millions of foreign workers was economically rational, whatever its brutality.
- **Premises**: (p. 408) Sauckel's supply made Jewish workers seem dispensable. (p. 409) The SS kept the fit alive for a time.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 411, 419) Ideology and economy were reconciled by segmentation and compromise. Tooze accepts this and then says it is incomplete.
- **Premises**: (pp. 420-421) Food was short and foreign workers were unproductive because unfed. (pp. 424-425) Removing Jews from the General Government's food chain and black market freed grain. (p. 428) Rations then rose.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 428-429) There was a "perverse functional connection" between the extermination of Polish Jewry and the feeding of the workforce in Germany.
- **Limit stated by Tooze**: ideology was decisive in the last instance for the Judaeocide; no other reason explains such thoroughness (p. 419).

## Evidence offered

- Labour statistics for foreign workers and prisoners; a Labour Ministry comparison of women's war work (pp. 400-403).
- Tooze's own tally of worker deaths by category, built from estimates with acknowledged gaps (pp. 407-408).
- A Krupp manager's verbatim record of the Kaiserhof meeting of October 1942 (p. 413).
- Productivity studies at Krupp (November 1942) and later; Spoerer's cost accounts for the Loibl tunnel (Table 16, not extracted) (pp. 416-419).
- SD reports on the April 1942 ration cut; the OKW economic office memorandum on calories and labour (pp. 421-422).
- Minutes of Backe's 23 June 1942 meeting and of Goering's 6 August 1942 conference. One page of the latter, on the Jews, is missing from every surviving copy; Tooze infers deliberate removal (pp. 425-427).
- Goebbels's diary; General Government records of Frank's response (pp. 409, 424, 427).
- Food delivery statistics by territory for 1940-41 and 1942-3 (p. 428).

- Tension: Tooze says ideology was decisive for the Judaeocide (p. 419) and also that food supplied an economic motive that produces "coherence" (p. 420). The reader has to decide how much the food motive explains timing as against the decision itself.

## Open questions

- Would the Polish Jews have been killed on the same timetable without the 1942 food crisis? Tooze shows the linkage in July-August 1942; he does not claim food caused the decision.
- The gap of "hundreds of thousands" between those deported from the East and those registered as workers in Germany is unexplained (p. 407).
