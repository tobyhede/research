# ch04: Partners: The Regime and German Business (pp. 85–113)

## Question this chapter answers

What was the relationship between big business and Hitler's regime in the peacetime years? Tooze's answer: business paid for the destruction of democracy and the left and got the domestic settlement it wanted, but lost the liberal international order it preferred and lost power to the state. The state then found willing collaborators sector by sector, because it offered profits, protected markets and the chance to develop the technologies of autarchy and rearmament.

## Leading propositions

- At the 20 February meeting Hitler promised an end to democracy and the crushing of the left, not anti-Semitism or conquest; business paid for that, and the donations mattered because the party was short of money (pp. 86-87).
- Business leaders were not ideological Nazis, but they were willing partners in destroying political pluralism (p. 87).
- The wage freeze, the end of unions and tolerated cartelisation made the profit outlook very favourable, though real wage costs had not fallen much in the Depression (p. 88).
- In 1923 business accepted an unwelcome domestic settlement for the sake of an attractive international one; in 1933 the positions were reversed (p. 89).
- Big business had little good to expect from the Hitler-Schacht-Hugenberg government on trade and debt, and Hitler did not need its agreement: the Depression had left business powerless to resist intervention from the right (pp. 90-91).
- The controls of 1933-5 were unprecedented in peacetime and grew out of balance of payments management (p. 91).
- Import controls removed foreign competition; consumer prices rose almost 6 per cent between spring 1933 and August 1934, leading to a state price-supervision system under Goerdeler (p. 92).
- The Berlin Great Banks were among the commercial losers: their assets rose 15 per cent between 1932 and 1939 while output more than doubled and savings bank assets rose 102 per cent. They were never less influential than in 1933-45 (pp. 93-94).
- Never again did big business influence German government as directly as between 1914 and 1929 (p. 96).
- Business was not a passive object. Profits financed recovery from the Depression and then an unprecedented investment boom, and the regime needed industry's technological and organisational capacity (p. 97).
- IG Farben did not need Hitler; its commitment to costly synthesis technology, stranded by the oil glut of 1930, made it dependent on state protection and so the regime's closest industrial collaborator (pp. 98-99).
- Brabag shows open coercion of capital: reluctant coal firms financed a 350 million Reichsmark company and never saw a dividend (pp. 101-102).
- Heavy industry had no single position. Krupp and Thyssen lost out in the new organisations; Vestag under Voegler and Poensgen did well. Coal and crude steel output grew sluggishly from fear of over-capacity, but high-quality electric steel grew sevenfold between 1929 and 1939 (pp. 102-106).
- The aircraft industry belongs at the centre of the regime's industrial history: from 3,200 employees in 1932 to at least a quarter of a million within ten years, created by state initiative, funding and direction, beginning with the forced expropriation of Hugo Junkers in October 1933 (pp. 106-107).
- Private capital would not carry the risk of the aircraft sector; Vestag declined to buy Junkers and Siemens sold its aero-engine business (p. 108).
- Foreign direct investors (about $450 million from the United States) profited to the degree they collaborated, but could not repatriate capital or profits and so reinvested (pp. 112-113).
- In virtually every setting the political authorities, who held the initiative, found active collaborators in business (p. 113).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 90) The Depression broke banks and major firms and left the Reich holding controlling stakes. (p. 90) The gold standard and free trade collapsed. (p. 91) Intervention now came from the right, with the left destroyed.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 91, 96) The balance of power shifted permanently from business to the state.
- **Premises**: (p. 93) Rising demand, no foreign competition, rising prices, static wages. (p. 93) Dividends capped.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 93, 97) Firms accumulated large reserves and invested them in the regime's priorities; control and profit went together.
- **Premises**: (p. 96) Devaluation was made non-negotiable. (p. 96) Germans were used to seeing their payments problem as structural. (p. 97) The home market was booming while world trade stagnated.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 96-97) There was little business opposition to controls until 1936-7.
- **Unstated premise**: that the case studies (chemicals, steel, aircraft, textiles) are representative of German industry as a whole. Tooze himself calls the IG Farben alliance historically unique (p. 97).

## Evidence offered

- The surviving record of the 20 February meeting and Schacht's donation list (pp. 85-87); Tooze notes what the record does and does not show.
- Company and sector histories for IG Farben, Vestag, Krupp, Junkers and the aircraft firms; employment and output figures (pp. 98-108).
- Bank balance-sheet growth 1932-9 (p. 94); cartel counts (p. 92); profit data in Figure 6, which did not extract (p. 93).
- An RWM official's recollection of the Schacht years for the bureaucracy's sense of power (p. 96); memoirs of Rohland (p. 104). Both are self-presentations.
- American wartime estimates of direct investment in Germany (p. 112).

## Quotes

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The control system described here is the New Plan apparatus of ch03 seen from the firm's side.
- The 1936-7 anxieties of business when the world economy revived (p. 97) lead into ch07.
- Aircraft industry and the unresolved bomber question (pp. 109-110) return in ch08, ch12 and ch13; synthetic fuel and rubber in ch07.
- Keppler, Kehrl, Krauch, Koppenberg, Rohland and Voegler recur as the managers of the Four Year Plan and the Speer system (ch07, ch17).
- Tension: business is called powerless (p. 91) and also still powerful in the sense of capacity to get things done (p. 97). Tooze resolves it by distinguishing influence over policy from indispensability in execution.
