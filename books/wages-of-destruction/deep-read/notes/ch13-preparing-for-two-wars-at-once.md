# ch13: Preparing for Two Wars at Once (pp. 335–360)

## Question this chapter answers

Did Germany lose in the East because it failed to mobilise its economy in 1940-41, as Galbraith, Speer and Kehrl claimed? Tooze's answer: no. Mobilisation was already high and rose further. The effort was deliberately split: the army was equipped for a short armoured campaign against the Soviet Union, while investment and the Luftwaffe were pointed at the coming air war with Britain and America. This was the one real Blitzkrieg strategy, and its weakness was the assumption that the Red Army would collapse within 500 kilometres of the border.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under-mobilization thesis | p. 336 | The view (Galbraith 1945, from Speer's staff) of a "peacetime economy at war" before 1942. Tooze calls it a myth. |
| Blitzkrieg strategy (1940-41) | p. 337 | A synthesis of campaign plan, technology and armaments programme premised on quick victory; adopted only after France, for Barbarossa. Its point was to split the war effort, not to spare civilians. |
| Ruestungsprogramm B | p. 338 | The army's programme for Barbarossa, August 1940 to April 1941: Panzer divisions doubled from 10 to 20, medium tanks doubled, more infantry firepower. |
| Main Committee for Tanks | p. 339 | Todt's committee under Rohland with Saur as political driver; a durable structure of the war economy. |
| Armaments holiday | p. 342 | Winter 1940-41 release of veterans to factories to make the weapons they would use; to be followed by a "war holiday" for industry during the campaign. |
| Unabkoemmlich (UK) | p. 342 | Exempt from the draft as indispensable to war production: 5.516 million men in mid-1941. |
| Investment boom | p. 345 | The unprecedented wartime expansion of industrial capital in chemicals, aircraft engines, aluminium and tanks, aimed at later years. |
| MONTAN | p. 345 | The army's industrial investment holding; 70 to 80 per cent of its wartime investment went into explosives. |
| IG Auschwitz | pp. 346-347 | The Monowitz chemicals complex chosen in winter 1940-41: budget 776 million Reichsmarks, the largest single Four Year Plan investment. |
| Dnieper-Dvina line | p. 353 | About 500 kilometres from the frontier, the limit of truck-borne supply and so the line on which the Red Army had to be destroyed. |
| Poor army | p. 354 | The Wehrmacht of 1941: 33 motorised divisions of 130, 600,000 to 750,000 horses. Poverty reflected Germany's incomplete development, not neglect. |

## Leading propositions

- It was the Red Army that destroyed the Wehrmacht; by ordering the attack Hitler brought on his own destruction (p. 335).
- Barbarossa was a means to consolidate Germany for the confrontation with the Western powers, so the war effort could not be directed only at the Red Army or only at immediate output (pp. 336-337).
- The home front was not under-mobilised: labour was fully used, consumer production already cut and never restored (p. 337).
- Hitler's civilian priorities were food and protection from air attack, both of which argued for attacking the Soviet Union and Britain (p. 338).
- The attack of 22 June 1941, with 3,050,000 men, was the largest military operation in history; the army grew from 143 to 180 divisions (pp. 338-339).
- Between October 1940 and April 1941 output of weapons and vehicles rose 54 per cent year on year, aircraft 40 per cent, U-boats more than threefold. The fall in ammunition output (from 36 per cent of armaments to under 20 per cent) was deliberate: 21.9 million light howitzer rounds were in stock (pp. 340-341).
- Steel taken from the army went to exports, which rose 25 per cent, to hold allies and neutrals (p. 341).
- The Wehrmacht grew to 7.3 million. Of fit men aged 20 to 30, 85 per cent were in uniform by summer 1941; there was no manpower reserve (p. 342).
- The claim of collapsing labour productivity in 1939-41 rests on bad statistics; revised series show output and workforce moving together (pp. 343-344).
- The surge in aircraft output in 1942, credited to Speer, followed from the Luftwaffe workforce and capacity expansion of 1941 (p. 344).
- The army planned 36 Panzer divisions and nearly 40,000 tanks for post-Barbarossa operations against the British Empire, and actually invested for it; later tank output exceeded those figures (p. 345).
- Krauch's projects received 2.5 billion Reichsmarks in 1940-41. IG Auschwitz was not a pure "investment ruin": by end 1944 it made 15 per cent of German methanol. Its construction killed at least 30,000 inmates (pp. 347-348).
- The Luftwaffe faced both wars and the American threat first. Its problem in 1940-41 was quality: the Ju 88, Me 110, Me 210 and He 177 all disappointed (pp. 348-349).
- Aero-engine expansion begun in autumn 1940 (Daimler-Benz Genshagen from 350 to 1,200 engines a month) made later fighter output possible; the giant Ostmark plant was a failure (pp. 351-352).
- In June 1941 Milch said Anglo-American output had passed the Axis and would be double by end 1942; he set a target of 3,000 aircraft a month. Krauch's 3 million tons of air fuel assumed Caucasus oil (pp. 352-353).
- Germany had 83.76 million people against at least 170 million Soviets. Truck supply reached 300 kilometres, stretched to 500; only three main rail lines served ten armies (pp. 353-354).
- German per capita GDP was about two and a half times the Soviet level; attacking the apparently weakest link with Germany's best weapon was not irrational (pp. 355-356).
- Doubts existed: Bock, a war game, Marcks's assessment, Halder's diary note that the purpose was unclear. Hitler put economic objectives (the Ukraine, the Baltic) before Moscow (pp. 356-358).
- Thomas reversed his pessimistic assessment in February 1941 to fit Hitler's view, promising 70 per cent of Soviet industry and 4 million tons of Ukrainian grain (pp. 358-359).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 340-341) Weapons output rose sharply while ammunition was cut on purpose. (pp. 343-344) Productivity did not collapse. (pp. 345-352) Large resources went into capacity for 1942 and after.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 338, 344) The 1940-41 record shows strategic design and further mobilisation, not indolence. Speer's later "miracle" drew on these investments.
- **Premises**: (p. 342) All prime manpower was committed. (pp. 353-354) Logistics could not sustain operations beyond 500 kilometres. (p. 353) The enemy had twice the population and a reserve system.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 355, 360) Everything rested on destroying the Red Army in the first weeks; if that failed, strategic disaster was foreseeable in February 1941.
- **Premises**: (p. 353) Air fuel plans presupposed the Caucasus. (pp. 358-359) Economic justifications were produced to order.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 353) Conquest was written into industrial plans as a precondition, the "perverse logic of Barbarossa".

## Evidence offered

- Galbraith's 1945 Fortune article and post-war interrogations of Speer's staff, identified as the origin of the thesis Tooze opposes (p. 336).
- Armaments output by category, army steel rations, manpower census of May 1941 (pp. 340-342); Appendix Tables A3 and A4 are cited but did not extract.
- Tooze's own recalculated armaments index and workforce estimate (Figure 17, not extracted) (pp. 343-344). The key productivity claim therefore cannot be checked from the text.
- MONTAN balance sheet (Table 13, not extracted); Four Year Plan records; US Strategic Bombing Survey on Auschwitz and Heydebreck (pp. 345-348).
- Reich Air Ministry investment authorisations; firm-level outcomes at Genshagen and the Ostmark works (pp. 351-352).
- Halder's diary; Bock; the Marcks study; OKW memoranda on Backe and Thomas (pp. 356-359).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Applies to 1940-41 the argument made for 1939-40 in ch10: politics was messy, outputs show design.
- Sets up the Speer chapter (ch17): the "miracle" has earlier causes.
- Backe's remark about the Ukraine as the only surplus region (p. 359) is the seed of the Hunger Plan in ch14.
- IG Auschwitz links the investment boom to the camp system treated in ch16.
- The poor-army point continues ch05 (p. 136) and ch07 (p. 171).
- Tension: Tooze calls the choice to attack the Soviet Union not irrational (p. 356) while showing that insiders foresaw disaster if the first blow failed (p. 360).

## Open questions

- How reliable is the claim that 5.18 billion Reichsmarks went into Luftwaffe industry expansion? Tooze himself calls the figure implausibly large (p. 352).
- What exactly Backe told Hitler in January 1941 was unclear even to insiders (p. 359).
