# ch11: Victory in the West – Sieg im Westen (pp. 291–313)

## Question this chapter answers

Does the victory over France in 1940 show that will and skill beat material odds, and so undermine an economic account of the war? And what did Germany gain? Tooze's answer: there was no Blitzkrieg strategy behind rearmament, and Germany had no material superiority. The victory came from an improvised operational plan that concentrated force at one point at very high risk, helped by geography. It was not repeatable. It gave Germany control of Western Europe's resources through booty, clearing deficits and occupation costs, but Britain stayed in the war and there was no plan to defeat it.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blitzkrieg thesis / strategic synthesis | p. 294 | The retrospective claim that Germany deliberately built a limited, tank-and-air force for short wars as part of a coherent strategy. Tooze rejects it. |
| Sickle cut (Manstein plan) | p. 296 | The Ardennes encirclement adopted in late February 1940 after the capture of staff maps discredited the first plan. |
| Schwerpunkt | p. 300 | Concentration of force at the decisive point: 45 divisions against 18 in the Ardennes, nearly 3:1 locally while outnumbered overall. |
| One-shot affair | p. 301 | Tooze's judgment of the plan: every Panzer formation committed on day one with no reserve. |
| Voluntarism | p. 297 | The error of treating the material balance as irrelevant because élan won in 1940; also the regime's own reading of the victory. |
| Grossraum | p. 305 | A large unified economic space under German leadership, now imagined as including Western Europe. |
| Centralized clearing | p. 307 | The August 1940 system routing European trade payments through Berlin; in practice an unlimited German overdraft with occupied countries. |
| Occupation costs | p. 310 | Regular payments exacted from Poland and the Western states in place of reparations; 20 million Reichsmarks a day from France. |
| Kontinentale Oel | p. 311 | State-party-industry hybrid built on French oil holdings in Romania taken in lieu of occupation costs. |

## Leading propositions

- The encirclement trapped 1.7 million Allied soldiers; Germany lost 49,000 dead and missing, France 120,000 killed in six weeks (p. 293).
- If the victory showed that élan beats material, it would challenge any economic analysis of the war (pp. 293-294).
- Germany had 2,439 tanks against 4,200 Allied (3,254 French), mostly inferior in gun and armour, and 3,578 combat aircraft against 4,469 (pp. 294-295).
- Rearmament had no clear strategic rationale; the 1936 and 1938 programmes aimed at a large, partly mechanised army, a strategic air force and a fleet. The 1939-40 priority was ammunition, inspired by 1914 (p. 295).
- The army had no plan to attack France before September 1939; the plan that won was adopted too late to affect armaments (pp. 295-296).
- The Blitzkrieg myth suited both sides: the Allies as an excuse, the Germans as proof of racial and leadership superiority. The victory fixed a superiority complex in German military planning (pp. 296-297).
- Material still mattered: the 1939-40 mobilisation nearly tripled the stock of medium tanks to 1,456 by 10 May (p. 298).
- Manstein's plan applied the Napoleonic principle of superior force at the decisive point; it confirms that numbers decide (pp. 298-299).
- The Luftwaffe paid heavily: 347 aircraft lost on 10 May, 30 per cent written off by the end of May (pp. 299-300).
- Panzer Group Kleist moved 1,222 tanks and 39,373 vehicles down four roads; petrol stocks covered five months of mobile war; most of the army walked with horse-drawn supply (pp. 300-301).
- Success depended on the short distance to the Channel and a dense road network. The 1940 gamble resembles Barbarossa: no reserves, everything on the opening phase (pp. 301-302).
- Swedish ore was 83 per cent of iron ore imports, hence Norway; the oil pact of 27 May 1940 secured 200,000 to 300,000 tons of Romanian oil a month (pp. 302-303).
- The German bloc potentially held 290 million people and about 30 per cent of world GDP, more than the United States or the British Empire, if pre-war activity could be kept up (pp. 304-305).
- Booty from France alone was valued at 7.7 billion Reichsmarks; 4,260 locomotives and 140,000 wagons came from the Western railways, and 81,000 tons of copper (p. 305).
- Clearing debts reached almost 30 billion Reichsmarks by end 1944 (France 8.5 billion); occupation costs exceeded even that (pp. 307-311).
- Germany kept exporting at a high level to prop up allies and neutrals; in 1943 its exports relative to 1938 were three times Britain's (p. 308).
- German private capital made few inroads in the West. A country running a huge current account deficit cannot buy up its neighbours; state-party hybrids like the Reichswerke gained most (pp. 309-311).
- Britain decided by end May 1940 to fight on, counting on America. On 12 July Hitler turned armaments to the navy and Luftwaffe and by August ordered preparations against the Soviet Union (pp. 311-312).
- Hitler had started a war with Britain with no coherent plan for defeating it (p. 313).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 294-295) Germany had fewer and worse tanks and fewer aircraft. (p. 295) Armaments priorities did not match the battle fought. (p. 296) The winning plan was adopted in February 1940.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 296) The victory was an improvisation, not the product of a strategic synthesis.
- **Premises**: (pp. 298-299) Local 3:1 superiority was achieved by stripping other sectors. (pp. 299-301) This required committing everything at once on a narrow front with tight logistics. (p. 301) Geography made the distances manageable.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 301-302) The result obeys conventional military logic and could not be repeated against an enemy with more material, better leadership and more space.
- **Premises**: (p. 309) Buying foreign assets requires exporting capital. (p. 310) Germany was importing on credit.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 310) The route to control of Europe's economy was levies and state seizure, not private investment.

## Evidence offered

- Order-of-battle figures for tanks, aircraft and divisions by sector (pp. 294-299), drawn from recent operational history (Tooze cites the revisionist work on the Blitzkrieg legend without naming it in the text).
- The film Sieg im Westen and Swiss censorship instructions as evidence of how the victory was presented (pp. 296-297).
- French post-war enumeration of goods seized; Reichsbank clearing balances at end 1944; French calculation of occupation costs (pp. 305-310). French figures come from the victim's accounting.
- Schlotterer's July 1940 speech to businessmen and Abs's remarks to bankers (pp. 307, 309).
- Gestapo mood reports and American Gallup polls for late 1940 (pp. 312-313).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Completes the anti-Blitzkrieg argument begun in ch07 (the horse-drawn army) and ch10 (ammunition priority).
- The superiority complex born here (p. 297) explains the planning errors of Barbarossa in ch14.
- Whether the bloc's economic potential could be realised (p. 305) is the question of ch12 and ch13.
- The preface's claim of "no coherent plan" against Britain (fm01 p. 12) is restated as the chapter's conclusion.
- Tension: Tooze criticises voluntarism yet his own explanation leans on Manstein's "brilliant conception" (p. 298). He reconciles them by calling the plan a way of producing local material superiority.
