# ch07: 1936: Four Years to War (pp. 163–196)

## Question this chapter answers

What role did economics play in the turn towards war in 1936-7? Tooze's answer: the acceleration of rearmament reopened the balance of payments problem and forced a choice between returning to the world economy (devaluation, restraint in arms, accommodation with the West) and pressing on. Hitler chose war readiness within four years. The Four Year Plan and steel rationing followed, but 1937 brought a stall in armaments, and Hitler's response was to raise international tension to force mobilisation.

## Leading propositions

- After 1936 an arrangement with the Western powers was on offer that would have left the regime's internal power intact, and most Germans would have accepted it (p. 165).
- Hitler refused, reading the Popular Fronts as a Communist and so Jewish threat; from summer 1936 he wanted Germany made ready for war (pp. 165-166).
- Of the growth in national output between 1935 and 1938, 47 per cent came directly from military spending and 67 per cent from military spending plus investment; private consumption supplied 25 per cent (pp. 166-167).
- Army targets rose from 21 divisions (December 1933) to 36 (March 1935), with 48 tank battalions added in December 1935; on 18 November 1935 Blomberg told the services to ignore financial limits (pp. 167-168).
- Schacht, who had promised the military everything in 1934, now warned of economic limits; the Reichsbank expected a currency shortfall of 400 million Reichsmarks against reserves of 88 million (pp. 168-169).
- Fromm's budget gave under 5 per cent to tanks and motor vehicles against 32 per cent for guns and ammunition; the army planned on 630,700 horses. Rearmament was not tailored to a motorised Blitzkrieg (p. 171).
- Fromm's question made war a logical consequence of the programme: the army had to be used or the effort scaled back (p. 172).
- Goering's seizure of private foreign assets raised 473 million Reichsmarks (p. 173).
- Reichsbank economists held that devaluation and rearmament were mutually exclusive (p. 176).
- Hitler's memorandum rejected exports and ignored devaluation; it threatened industry and proposed making Jews collectively liable for economic damage (pp. 177-179).
- Schacht tried to devalue alongside the franc in late September 1936 and was stopped by Funk's appeal to Hitler (p. 180).
- The steel shortage, caused by a production cut of November 1936 to save ore and scrap, mattered more to the working economy than the Four Year Plan announcement (pp. 184-185).
- Under rationing the Wehrmacht's steel was frozen at its 1936 level: 195,000 tons a month for the army against 270,500 requested. Aircraft output fell between April 1937 and summer 1938 (pp. 185-186).
- Exports had priority in 1937 (505,000 tons of steel a month, as much as the Wehrmacht and Four Year Plan together), and export earnings rose to 530 million Reichsmarks a month on the back of a 25 per cent rise in world trade (pp. 186-188).
- 1937 was the only pre-war year in which military spending did not rise significantly (p. 194).
- Goering broke the Ruhr's united front with police-state methods; Schacht left the RWM (pp. 190-191). The Reichswerke grew mainly by acquisition and became important chiefly in coal (pp. 191-192).
- International escalation opened the door to domestic mobilisation, not the reverse (pp. 194-195).
- From November 1937 no significant change in steel allocation was made without Hitler's personal approval (p. 196).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 172) Equipment for a 102-division army built in four years cannot be kept idle without waste or conversion. (p. 179) Hitler's memorandum set the same four-year horizon.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 172, 179) From 1936 war was the planned outcome of rearmament, not one option among several.
- **Premises**: (pp. 173-175) The liberal route required arms limitation and Western goodwill. (p. 177) Hitler held the Bolshevik-Jewish threat to be existential.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 179-180) The choice against devaluation was ideological and strategic, not technical.
- **Premises**: (p. 186) Balance of payments priority stalled arms in 1937. (p. 193) Hitler registered the slippage, moving his horizon to 1943-5. (p. 194) He could change what the generals took as given.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 194-195) He used foreign-policy tension as the lever to push mobilisation higher.
- **Unstated premise**: that the 1936 Western offer was real and durable. Tooze himself judges that Goerdeler overstated its price (pp. 174-175).

## Evidence offered

- The Fromm memorandum and army budget (pp. 170-172); Hitler's memorandum quoted at length (pp. 177-179); the Hossbach notes (p. 193).
- Goerdeler's memorandum and Goering's marginalia on it; Reichsbank department papers (pp. 173-176). These show the losing side's arguments from the inside.
- Goebbels's diary for the September 1936 devaluation episode (p. 180).
- Steel allocation figures by claimant, export earnings, aircraft output (pp. 185-188).
- Schnitzler's pre-trial affidavit on IG Farben's investment culture (pp. 183-184): a defendant's account, used with that caveat implied.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Schacht reverses role: the financier of rearmament in ch02 and ch03 becomes its brake and is removed.
- The devaluation question of 1934 (ch03) returns and is closed for good.
- IG Farben's stable partnership (pp. 183-184) continues the story from ch04; heavy industry's troubled one contrasts with it.
- The 1937 stall sets up the acceleration of 1938 in ch08.
- The horse-drawn army (p. 171) supports the anti-Blitzkrieg argument of ch11.
- Tension: the chapter says war was the logical consequence of 1936 planning and also that Hitler's decision would depend on unpredictable international events (p. 193).
