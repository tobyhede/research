# ch12: Britain and America: Hitler's Strategic Dilemma (pp. 313–335)

## Question this chapter answers

Why, after defeating France, did Germany not stand on the defensive and consolidate, and why did Hitler turn on the Soviet Union? Tooze's answer: Germany could not defeat Britain by sea or air; Britain could draw on American industry, which began mobilising in mid-1940; and occupied Western Europe was short of oil, coal and food and could not match that. Only Soviet resources could sustain a long war, and getting them by trade meant dependence on Stalin. Conquest in 1941, before America could intervene in 1942, seemed the way out.

## Leading propositions

- Stalin told Cripps in July 1940 that German hegemony was physically impossible for lack of sea power; Tooze agrees (p. 313).
- After Norway the German surface fleet was negligible: one heavy cruiser, two light cruisers and four destroyers to cover an invasion against a Home Fleet of 5 battleships, 11 cruisers and 30 destroyers nearby (p. 314).
- Only 20 U-boats were built between September 1939 and June 1940; boats in the Atlantic fell to 22 by February 1941. The U-boat war was too slow for Hitler's purpose and the most likely to bring America in (pp. 315-316).
- The Battle of Britain was one-sided: the Luftwaffe had lost 30 per cent of its strength in France and lacked heavy bombers and escorts. Neither side had the means for air supremacy over the Channel (pp. 316-317).
- While Britain fought, America had a channel for its industrial power (p. 318).
- American mobilisation began in summer 1940, not after Pearl Harbor: the aircraft programme, the Two Oceans Navy, the draft. By 1941 America, at peace, made almost as many weapons as Germany or Britain (pp. 318-319).
- A secret Anglo-American meeting on 23 July 1940 planned capacity for 72,000 aircraft a year. American output went from 6,019 military aircraft in 1940 to 19,433 in 1941, almost 48,000 in 1942 and 85,898 in 1943. German output was 10,826 in 1940 and about 12,000 in 1941 (pp. 321-322).
- Hitler and the Wehrmacht took the American threat seriously; on 17 December 1940 Hitler said all continental problems had to be solved in 1941 because America could intervene decisively in 1942 (pp. 322-323).
- German assessments of America were suffused with anti-Semitism (p. 322).
- Occupied Europe yielded little direct armament: by end 1941 Germany had 78 aircraft from France and the Netherlands against Britain's 5,012 from America; 2,517 from France in the whole war (p. 324).
- Germany's fuel flow was at most 8.9 million tons a year for itself and its dependants; France was cut to 8 per cent of its pre-war petrol. Britain imported 10.2 million tons of oil in 1942 and over 20 million in 1944 (pp. 325-326).
- French coal output fell 18 per cent in 1940 and never recovered; Ruhr output peaked in 1939 and was falling in 1941; steel potential of 46 million tons could not be reached without 15 million more tons of coal (pp. 327-329).
- The 1940 harvest failed across Europe (French grain under half of 1938); rations in Belgium and France were about 1,300 calories (pp. 330-331).
- No Western European country occupied in 1940 grew in the next five years; the larger economies collapsed (p. 331).
- Only Soviet grain, coal, ores and oil could sustain the bloc. Alliance with Stalin was possible but meant growing dependence, shown when Moscow demanded hydrogenation and Buna plants in payment (pp. 331-333).
- In late 1940 Goering gave deliveries to the Soviet Union equal priority with Wehrmacht orders (p. 333).
- Stalin's doubling of grain deliveries in January 1941 only confirmed Hitler's view that the Ukraine should be seized (pp. 333-334).
- Hitler reasoned on 31 July 1940 that knocking out Russia would remove Britain's hope and free Japan against America (p. 334).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 314-317) No naval or air route to defeating Britain. (pp. 318-322) American production would tilt the air war from late 1941. (pp. 324-331) Occupied Europe was an economic liability in energy and food.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 335) A defensive posture meant a long war Germany would lose; it was a second-best.
- **Premises**: (p. 331) Only Soviet resources could close the gap. (pp. 332-333) Trade made Germany a dependant. (p. 335) The army had just shown it could win decisive victories.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 334-335) Conquest of the Soviet Union in 1941 appeared both necessary and feasible. Tooze adds that real arms-race pressure and ideological fantasy fused in Barbarossa (p. 335).
- **Hindsight concession**: Tooze grants that, since the Red Army destroyed the Wehrmacht, a defensive strategy would have served Germany better (p. 335).

## Evidence offered

- Naval orders of battle and U-boat construction and sinking figures (pp. 314-316).
- American, British and German aircraft production series, 1940-43 (pp. 321-322); the American expansion is inferred from output timing to have begun by autumn 1940 (p. 321).
- Harrison's comparison of domestic and external mobilisation (Table 11, not extracted) (p. 320).
- Oil stocks and imports for Germany and Britain; British intelligence disbelief at how small German stocks were (p. 326).
- Coal and steel data for France and the Ruhr; the Winkhaus report on steel capacity (pp. 327-329). Table 12 and Figure 16 did not extract.
- Halder's diary, the Wehrmacht economic office's October 1940 assessment, Hitler's statements of 31 July and 17 December 1940 and 9 January 1941 (pp. 323, 333-334).
- Gestapo reports on German preoccupation with America (p. 323).

## Quotes

- "For all Churchill’s rhetoric of blood, sweat and tears, Britain never matched Germany in its mobilization of domestic resources." (p. 320)
- "Whilst output in both Germany and Britain increased substantially over the course of the war and whilst output in the United States rocketed, Germany’s European empire was a basket case." (p. 331)

- Tension: Barbarossa is presented as rational given the constraints and also as a fusion with ideological fantasy. Tooze does not separate how much each contributed.
