## Classification

- **Kind**: theoretical (explains what happened and why)
- **Genre**: history; economic history of a political regime, with a strong revisionist thesis argued against earlier economic historians and against the "ideology only" school

## The whole-book question

How did a middling European economy make its bid for continental and world power, why did Hitler take the gambles he took, and why was the bid bound to fail?

## Unity statement

Germany in the 1930s was a partly modernised, import-dependent economy facing an American hegemon, and Hitler answered that predicament with a programme of conquest read through racial ideology. The regime mobilised its economy more thoroughly than any capitalist state had in peacetime, but it could not change the balance of resources, so each step (1939, 1940, 1941) was a gamble against time and against an ever larger coalition. The same shortages of land, food and labour that drove the gambles also shaped the regime's programmes of mass murder, and the war economy of 1942–5 under Speer was a politically driven effort to prolong a war already lost.

## High-value chapters for this reading

Ranked against the stated reading purpose: "a durable, checkable understanding of the book's argument, for later question-answering and cross-book synthesis"

1. ch20 — the author's own statement of what is new; read first as an index to the rest.
2. ch09 — the fullest version of the central explanatory move (economic pressure plus ideology explains the decision for war).
3. ch07 — the 1936 fork in the road and the mechanism by which foreign-policy escalation drove mobilisation.
4. ch16 — the food, labour and genocide argument, the book's most distinctive contribution on the Holocaust.
5. ch12 and ch13 — why Barbarossa, and the rebuttal of the under-mobilisation thesis.
6. ch17 — the Speer reassessment.
7. ch03 — the balance of payments constraint that recurs everywhere.
8. ch01 — the Stresemann alternative that frames the whole.

## Reading strategy for history

- The historian's thesis is explicit: Tooze argues against (a) the Keynesian reading of the Nazi recovery, (b) the Blitzkrieg-economy thesis, (c) the Galbraith-Speer-Wagenfuehr account of an inefficient war economy rescued in 1942, and (d) accounts that treat ideology and economics as rival explanations. Each note records which of these a chapter is aimed at.
- Sources lean on German state and military records (Reichsbank, the Wehrmacht's military-economic office under Thomas, Four Year Plan, Zentrale Planung), diaries (Halder, Goebbels, Bock), and post-war memoirs and affidavits (Speer, Kehrl, Rohland, Schnitzler). The memoirs are self-serving and Tooze says so in places; notes flag where a claim rests on them.
- A second history of the same events is the natural check. For cross-book work, the obvious comparisons are with accounts centred on ideology and with the older economic histories Tooze criticises.
