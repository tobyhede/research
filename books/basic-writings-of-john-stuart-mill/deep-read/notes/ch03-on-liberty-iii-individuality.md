# ch03: On Liberty III: Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being (pp. 53–69)

Scope note: chapter text pp. 53-68; Mill's three footnotes (Humboldt reference, Sterling's Essays, lunacy trials) are printed at pp. 68-69.

## Question this chapter answers

Do the reasons for free opinion also require that people be free to act on their opinions and live in their own way, at their own risk?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| individuality | p. 54 | A person's own character, and not the traditions or customs of others, being the rule of conduct in things that do not primarily concern others. Later said to be 'the same thing with development' (p. 60). |
| experiments of living | p. 53 | Different modes of life tried in practice so that their worth can be proved; the conduct analogue of differing opinions. |
| spontaneity | p. 54 | Acting from one's own impulses and judgment. Mill complains that it is not seen as having intrinsic worth. |
| custom | p. 55 | Presumptive evidence of what others' experience has taught, with a claim to deference; but conformity to custom merely as custom exercises no faculty. Its 'despotism' is the standing hindrance to advancement (p. 65). |
| character | p. 57 | Having desires and impulses that are one's own, the expression of one's own nature as developed by one's own culture. An 'energetic character' adds strength under a strong will. |
| Calvinistic theory | p. 58 | The view that self-will is the one great offence and obedience the whole of human good. |
| genius / originality | pp. 60-61 | Originality in thought and action, not only in art. Possible only in an atmosphere of freedom. |
| collective mediocrity | p. 62 | The mass whose opinions pass for public opinion: the whole white population in America, the middle class in England. |
| eccentricity | p. 63 | Non-conformity as such, which in an age of tyrannous opinion is itself a service. |
| progressive principle | p. 65 | The love of liberty or of improvement, antagonist of custom. Liberty is its only unfailing source. |

Shift to note: 'nature' is used approvingly here (human nature as a tree, p. 56; 'not following their own nature', p. 58), whereas in The Subjection of Women Mill denies that anything can be known of women's nature (ch06). Here nature means a person's own developed bent, not a fixed kind.

## Leading propositions

- Actions cannot be as free as opinions; even an opinion loses immunity when its expression is a positive instigation to a mischievous act (p. 53).
- In things that do not primarily concern others, individuality should assert itself; where custom is the rule, a principal ingredient of happiness and the chief ingredient of progress is missing (p. 54).
- The obstacle is not disagreement about means but indifference to the end: spontaneity is not recognised as having intrinsic worth (p. 54).
- The faculties of perception, judgment, feeling and moral preference are exercised only in making a choice; whoever acts from custom makes none (p. 55).
- It matters what manner of men people are, not only what they do (p. 56).
- Strong desires and impulses are raw material for good; men act ill because their consciences are weak, not because their desires are strong (p. 56).
- Society has now got the better of individuality; the present danger is deficiency of personal impulse, to the point that people have no inclination except for what is customary (pp. 57-58).
- Restraint for the sake of others' rights develops the social part of a person; restraint by others' mere displeasure develops nothing valuable (p. 59).
- Whatever crushes individuality is despotism, whatever it calls itself (p. 60).
- Developed individuals are useful to the undeveloped: they originate new practices and keep old ones from becoming mechanical (p. 60).
- The tendency of the age is to make mediocrity the ascendant power; the corrective is the pronounced individuality of exceptional people, who may claim only freedom to point the way and never power to compel (p. 62).
- A person's own mode of laying out his existence is best because it is his own, given tolerable common sense and experience; this holds for everyone and not only for the gifted (p. 63).
- People differ in their sources of pleasure and conditions of development, so without diversity of lives they get neither their share of happiness nor their full stature (pp. 63-64).
- Liberty is the only unfailing and permanent source of improvement, because it makes each individual a possible centre of improvement (p. 65).
- Europe owes its progress to plurality of paths and is losing it through assimilation of circumstances and the ascendancy of public opinion (pp. 67-68).
- The stand for individuality must be made early, because people who no longer see diversity cannot conceive it (p. 68).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 53) mankind are fallible, their truths are half-truths, and diversity is a good until all sides of truth can be seen + (p. 53) these principles apply to modes of action as much as to opinions.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 53) there should be different experiments of living, short of injury to others. This is the transfer of ch02's argument to conduct.
- **Premises**: (p. 55) human faculties improve only by use + (p. 55) they are used only in choosing + (p. 56) the first work of human life is the perfecting of man himself.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 56) choosing one's own plan of life is a component of human worth, apart from whether the plan chosen is the best. Unstated premise: the developed exercise of the distinctively human faculties is part of well-being and not merely a means to pleasure. Mill asserts this as the standard ('what more or better can be said', p. 60) and does not derive it from pleasure in this chapter.
- **Premises**: (p. 60) few people's experiments would improve on established practice + (p. 61) those few need a soil of freedom, and cannot be picked out in advance.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 60-61) those who do not want liberty for themselves still gain from allowing it to others. This is the instrumental argument, offered to those unmoved by the first.
- **Premises**: (p. 63) people differ as much in their requirements for a life as in the shape of their feet + (p. 63) a mode of life that helps one hinders another.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 63-64) one pattern of life cannot give each a fair share of happiness. This is the argument closest to plain utilitarian reasoning.
- **Premises**: (pp. 65-66) nations stop progressing when they cease to have individuality (the East, China) + (p. 67) Europe progressed through diversity of character and culture.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 67) unless individuality asserts itself against public opinion Europe will become another China. The historical premises are asserted with one sentence of evidence each.

## Evidence offered

- Humboldt's Sphere and Duties of Government on the development of human powers, with its two requisites, freedom and variety of situations (p. 54, p. 67).
- The corn-dealer example: the same opinion is protected in the press and punishable before an excited mob (p. 53).
- The contrast of machine and tree; automatons doing all human work (p. 56).
- Knox, Alcibiades and Pericles as types (p. 59).
- China as a talented nation made stationary by excellent customs uniformly impressed (pp. 66-67); Tocqueville on the growing resemblance of Frenchmen (p. 67).
- Lunacy commissions used against the eccentric (p. 64 and Mill's footnote, pp. 68-69).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Two different defences run side by side: individuality as part of well-being in itself (pp. 54-60) and individuality as useful to progress and to others (pp. 60-68). Mill marks the join (p. 60). The first is what 'utility in the largest sense' (ch01, p. 16) has to mean if the essay is utilitarian; Utilitarianism II's higher pleasures (ch11) is the nearest supporting doctrine.
- The praise of exceptional individuals and the contempt for 'collective mediocrity' (p. 62) stand beside the insistence that everyone, not only the superior, has a claim to live in his own way (p. 63). Mill rejects hero-worship explicitly (p. 62).
- 'The man, and still more the woman' who departs from custom is censured (p. 64); the Chinese lady's foot as the image of compressed character (p. 64). Both anticipate The Subjection of Women, where women's character is said to be an artificial product of such compression (ch06).
- Opinion is again the main enemy (p. 62, p. 68), as in ch01 (p. 11) and ch02 (p. 32).
- The limit 'within the limits imposed by the rights and interests of others' (p. 59) restates the harm principle in terms of rights, which ch04 develops.
- The view of the East and China as stationary repeats the civilizational ranking behind the 'barbarians' exception in ch01 (p. 16). I cannot verify the historical claims from the text; they are asserted, not argued.

## Open questions

- Is individuality valued because it produces happiness, or is it part of what happiness is? The chapter says both (p. 54) and never chooses.
- If eccentricity is a service merely as non-conformity (p. 63), does the value of a way of life depend on its being chosen or on its being different?
- What 'tolerable amount of common sense and experience' (p. 63) is required before one's own mode is best, and who decides?
