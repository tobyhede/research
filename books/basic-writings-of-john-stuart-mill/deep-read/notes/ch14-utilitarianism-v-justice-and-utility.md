## Question this chapter answers

If utility is the criterion of right, why does justice feel like something absolute and different in kind from expediency, and what is justice?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sentiment of justice | pp. 226-227 | The powerful feeling attached to the idea of justice. Its origin is a separate question from its binding force. |
| legal right / moral right | pp. 228-229 | What belongs to a person by law; what he ought to have even where the law denies it. Injustice is taking or withholding either. |
| desert | p. 229 | Good for doing right, evil for doing wrong; in particular, good from those one has benefited and evil from those one has harmed. |
| impartiality | p. 230 | Being influenced only by the considerations that ought to govern the case in hand. |
| equality | p. 230 | Thought by many to be the essence of justice; in practice each person holds it to be required except where he thinks expediency requires inequality. |
| wrong / duty | pp. 232-233 | Conduct for which a person ought to be punished, by law, opinion or conscience. Duty is what may be exacted, as one exacts a debt. This marks morality off from expediency. |
| perfect and imperfect obligation | p. 233 | Perfect: duties with a correlative right in some person. Imperfect: obligatory acts whose occasions are left to choice, such as charity. |
| justice | p. 234 | The part of morality in which some assignable person can claim the act as his moral right. Later: a name for certain classes of moral rules that concern the essentials of human well-being most nearly (p. 242). |
| a right | p. 237 | Something society ought to defend me in the possession of, by law or by education and opinion. The reason it ought is general utility. |
| security | p. 237 | The interest in not being deprived of everything by whoever is stronger. The most vital of all interests, on which the value of every other good depends. |
| the Expedient | p. 226 | Here used neutrally for what promotes general utility; justice is a branch of it with a special sentiment. |

Shift to note: 'expediency' in ch11 was a term of reproach for narrow or short-term advantage (p. 209). Here 'general expediency' is simply utility (p. 227). 'Harm' and 'hurt' appear as the core of injustice (pp. 242-243), with wrongful interference in another's freedom expressly included.

## Leading propositions

- That a feeling is natural does not legitimate all its promptings; the feeling of justice might be an instinct and still need control by reason (pp. 226-227).
- People readily take a subjective feeling not otherwise explained to be a revelation of objective reality (p. 227).
- Survey of what is called unjust: violating legal rights; violating moral rights; giving people what they do not deserve; breaking faith; partiality; and, allied to it, inequality (pp. 228-230).
- On equality, each person's notion of justice follows his notion of utility (p. 230).
- The primitive element in the idea of justice is conformity to law, later to law as it ought to be (pp. 231-232).
- The idea of penal sanction belongs to all wrong, not only to injustice: we call an act wrong when we think the person ought to be punished for it in some way (pp. 232-233).
- Justice is distinguished from the rest of morality by a correlative right in an assignable person (pp. 233-234).
- No one has a moral right to our generosity or beneficence (p. 234).
- The sentiment of justice has two ingredients: the desire to punish one who has done harm, and the belief that some definite individual has been harmed (pp. 234-235).
- The desire to punish grows from self-defence and sympathy. In itself it has nothing moral in it; what is moral is its subordination to the social sympathies (p. 235).
- Kant's principle, to have any meaning, must be read as a rule all rational beings might adopt with benefit to their collective interest (p. 236).
- To have a right is to have something society ought to defend me in the possession of, and the reason is general utility (p. 237).
- The peculiar strength of the feeling comes from the interest at stake, security, which no one can do without; the difference in degree becomes a difference in kind (pp. 237-238).
- If justice were an independent standard known by introspection, it would not be so ambiguous: on punishment, on wages and on taxation, rival maxims of justice conflict and only social utility can decide (pp. 238-242).
- Justice grounded on utility is the chief and most sacred part of morality (p. 242).
- The rules that forbid people to hurt one another, including wrongful interference with each other's freedom, are more vital to well-being than any other maxims (p. 242).
- Disappointment of expectation is a real hurt, which is why breach of promise and of friendship are grave wrongs and why good for good belongs to justice (p. 243).
- Impartiality and equality follow from the principle of utility itself: one person's happiness, equal in degree with allowance for kind, counts for exactly as much as another's (p. 244).
- All persons have a right to equality of treatment except where some recognised social expediency requires otherwise (p. 245).
- Social improvement has been a series of transitions in which accepted institutions came to be seen as injustice: slavery, serfdom, and in time the aristocracies of colour, race and sex (p. 245).
- A maxim of justice may be overruled in a particular case, as in stealing food or medicine to save a life; we then say the act is not unjust in that case (p. 245).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 228-230) the things called unjust are various + (p. 234) what they share is a wrong done to an assignable person who has a right + (p. 235) the feeling is resentment extended by sympathy and intelligence to hurts that touch society.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 236) the sentiment needs no special origin; it is the natural feeling of retaliation, moralised.
- **Premises**: (p. 237) a right is a valid claim to society's protection + (p. 237) the ground of the claim is utility + (p. 237) the utility in question is security, on which all other goods depend.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 238) the claim rightly takes on a character of absoluteness. Rights are not outside utility; they are its most important rules. Unstated premise: the rules that best serve security are fixed enough that individuals can rely on them against case-by-case calculation. Mill's talk of 'more absolute obligation' (p. 242) implies it; the life-saving exception (p. 245) limits it.
- **Premises**: (pp. 238-241) accepted maxims of justice conflict on punishment, on remuneration and on taxation + (p. 239) each disputant is unanswerable from his own maxim.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 241) justice cannot be an independent self-evident standard; an external standard is needed, and utility is the one available. This is the ch10 argument against intuitionism (p. 193) applied to its strongest case.
- **Premises**: (p. 244) the greatest happiness principle is empty unless equal happiness counts equally whoever feels it.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 244-245) everyone has an equal claim to happiness and so to the means of it, limited only by the conditions of life and the general interest, and inequalities need positive justification. Unstated premise: equal weight for equal happiness in the calculation implies a presumption of equal treatment in distribution. The first is arithmetic (footnote, p. 246); the second is a substantive rule and Mill moves from one to the other in a sentence.

## Evidence offered

- The etymology of words for just in Latin, Greek, German and French, pointing to law or authoritative custom (p. 231).
- Slave countries, where equal protection of such rights as slaves have is thought just while the absence of rights is not thought unjust (p. 230).
- Three views of punishment: for the offender's good only, for others' protection only, and Owen's view that it is never just because character is made by circumstances (pp. 238-239).
- The social contract and the maxim volenti non fit injuria as devices to turn these difficulties (pp. 239-240).
- The co-operative association: should skill earn more? (pp. 240-241). Taxation: proportional, graduated or equal sums? (pp. 241-242).
- The person earning three hundred a year by competition against the holder of stock yielding three hundred a year (p. 237).
- Stealing or kidnapping a doctor to save a life (p. 245).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- This chapter supplies what On Liberty lacks: an account of how a utilitarian can speak of rights. On Liberty ch04 limits enforceable conduct to injury of interests that 'ought to be considered as rights' (p. 69); here a right is what society ought to protect on grounds of utility (p. 237), and the protected interest is security, with freedom from wrongful interference named as part of it (p. 242). The harm principle can be read as one of these rules of justice.
- The test of wrongness, that the person ought to be punished by law, opinion or conscience (pp. 232-233), marks the same line On Liberty draws between conduct society may penalise and self-regarding faults that are 'not properly immoralities' (ch04, p. 72).
- The sentence on the 'aristocracies of colour, race, and sex' (p. 245) states the thesis of The Subjection of Women as an application of utilitarian justice, and the remark that people wonder how past inequalities were ever tolerated (p. 245) matches ch06's account of custom.
- The life-saving exception (p. 245) shows that rules of justice can be overridden by utility in the particular case; this is the main textual obstacle to a strict rule-utilitarian reading and to treating On Liberty's principle as 'absolute' (ch01, p. 15).
- The reading of Kant (p. 236) repeats ch10 (p. 194). It is asserted in a few lines.
- The distinction between perfect and imperfect obligation (p. 233) leaves beneficence unenforceable as a right, yet On Liberty allows compulsion to save a life or protect the defenceless (ch01, p. 16). Either such acts are owed to assignable persons, or enforcement reaches beyond justice.

## Open questions

- If 'recognised social expediency' can justify unequal treatment (p. 245), and each person's idea of justice follows his idea of expediency (p. 230), what stops a majority from finding the subjection of a group expedient? Mill's answer is historical optimism (p. 245), not a criterion.
- How is 'with the proper allowance made for kind' (p. 244) to be applied when counting each person's happiness equally? It imports ch11's qualities of pleasure into the distributive principle.
- Does the account explain the sentiment of justice, or justify it? Mill says both that the feeling does and that it 'ought to exist' in these cases (p. 246), but the argument given is genealogical.
