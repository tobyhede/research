# ch06: The Subjection of Women I (pp. 104–128)

Scope note: chapter text pp. 104-128; one footnote by Mill identifying the motto from Mme. de Staël's Delphine (p. 128).

## Question this chapter answers

Does the universal custom of women's legal subordination to men create any presumption in its favour, and can anything be inferred from the supposed nature of the sexes?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| legal subordination of one sex to the other | p. 104 | The principle regulating existing relations between the sexes. Called wrong in itself and a chief hindrance to human improvement. |
| perfect equality | p. 104 | The replacement principle: no power or privilege on one side, no disability on the other. |
| burthen of proof | p. 105 | Ordinarily on the affirmative, and in practical matters on those who are against liberty and for partiality. Mill says it is wrongly reversed in this case. |
| custom and the general feeling | p. 106 | What Mill agrees to treat as conclusive against him unless shown to owe its existence to causes other than its soundness. |
| instinct | p. 106 | The name given to whatever we find in ourselves without a traceable rational foundation; its worship is the characteristic prejudice of the nineteenth century. |
| law of the strongest / law of force | p. 108 | The rule by which regard for a class was measured by what it could enforce. The only source of the inequality of rights between men and women. |
| natural | p. 113 | As used by defenders of any domination it generally means customary. 'Unnatural generally means only uncustomary' (p. 114). |
| the nature of women | p. 121 | As now observed, an eminently artificial thing, the result of forced repression in some directions and unnatural stimulation in others. Unknowable under present relations. |
| laws of the formation of character | p. 123 | The psychology of how circumstances shape character, which Mill says is needed, and lacking, for any claim about natural differences. |
| the modern world's principle | p. 117 | Human beings are no longer born to their place in life but are free to use their faculties to achieve the lot they prefer. |
| Hobson's choice | p. 127 | Marriage or nothing: the position women are left in when all other doors are closed. |

Shift to note: 'nature' is used in three ways. Defenders' sense, a fixed endowment of each sex, which Mill says cannot be known (p. 121). The debunked sense, where natural means customary (p. 114). And Mill's own positive use: what women by nature cannot do it is superfluous to forbid, and giving their nature 'free play' will settle the matter (p. 126). He denies knowledge of women's nature and still relies on there being one.

## Leading propositions

- The legal subordination of one sex to the other is wrong in itself and ought to be replaced by perfect equality (p. 104).
- An opinion rooted in feeling gains stability from being defeated in argument, since its holders infer a deeper ground the arguments do not reach (p. 104).
- The presumption is in favour of freedom and impartiality, so the burden of proof lies on those who would restrict women; in this case it is wrongly thrown on the other side (p. 105).
- Mill accepts the unfavourable terms: custom may stand unless he shows it arose from causes other than its soundness (p. 106).
- The present system rests on theory only, since no other has been tried; and it was never adopted by deliberation (p. 107).
- It arose because women were found in bondage to men through inferior strength, and law converted the physical fact into a legal right (p. 107).
- The present dependence of women is the primitive slavery continuing through successive mitigations; it has not lost the taint of its origin (p. 108).
- Institutions that put right on the side of might are durable; those holding power from force rarely lose it until the physical power passes to the other side (pp. 108-109).
- The power of men over women was bound to outlast other forms of unjust authority: it is shared by the whole male sex, exercised over those nearest, and each subject lives under her master's eye with no means of combining (pp. 112-113).
- Every domination has appeared natural to those who held it: Aristotle on slavery, American slaveholders, absolute monarchy, feudal nobility (pp. 113-114).
- The claim that women consent is false in fact, since many protest, and misleading, since no subject class begins by complaining of the power itself and complaint invites worse usage (pp. 115-116).
- Men want not only women's obedience but their sentiments, so the whole force of education has been turned to make submission the ideal of feminine character and part of sexual attractiveness (pp. 116-117).
- The mark of the modern world is that people are not born to their place; freedom of individual choice is known to be the only thing that puts each task in the hands of those best fit for it (pp. 117-118).
- Legal exclusions based on general presumptions wrong the exceptional individual and cost society, while the excluded unfit would have been kept out by ordinary motives anyway (p. 119).
- The disabilities of women are the solitary case, royalty aside, in which birth fixes a person's position for life (pp. 119-120).
- This raises a prima facie presumption against the system (p. 120).
- Experience cannot have decided between two courses when only one has been tried; and every step of improvement has been accompanied by a rise in the position of women (p. 121).
- No one knows or can know the nature of the two sexes as long as they have been seen only in their present relation (p. 121).
- Only a difference that could not possibly be artificial could be inferred to be natural, and no one has the knowledge of character formation needed to say (p. 123).
- Men know little even of the women nearest them, because authority on one side and subordination on the other prevent confidence; thorough knowledge exists only between equals (p. 124).
- Women have told little, and that little is mostly suborned (pp. 123, 125).
- No such knowledge is needed: the question rests with women themselves, and there is no way to find what anyone can do but by trying (p. 126).
- What women cannot do by nature need not be forbidden; what they do less well, competition will exclude them from (p. 126).
- The real fear of opponents is that women given other options will refuse marriage on unequal terms (pp. 127-128).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 107) a general practice supports a presumption only if adopted as a means to good ends on the evidence of experience + (p. 107) the subordination of women was never compared with alternatives and arose from force.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 108) its existence creates no presumption in its favour. This is a debunking argument: it removes a reason for belief without showing the practice bad. Mill says so twice (p. 112, p. 113).
- **Premises**: (pp. 113-114) every past domination was called natural by its beneficiaries + (p. 114) feelings of what is natural track what is customary, as shown by attitudes to queens, soldiers and members of parliament.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 114) the feeling that women's subjection is natural is no evidence that it is.
- **Premises**: (p. 118) individuals are better judges of their own capacities than law is + (p. 119) no general presumption of unfitness is infallible + (p. 119) competition excludes the unfit without law.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 119) birth should not fix anyone's occupation; the principle that has been applied to class and trade applies to sex. Unstated premise: the conditions of competition are themselves fair to women, which Mill elsewhere denies (education, opinion). The argument is for removing legal bars, not for anything further.
- **Premises**: (p. 121) women have been seen only under subjection + (p. 122) their character has been cultivated for the benefit of their masters + (p. 123) a natural difference could be shown only as a residue after every artificial cause is deducted.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 123) no one is entitled to a positive opinion on natural differences of mind and character between the sexes. This is an argument from ignorance used correctly: it blocks the opponent's premise and asserts nothing about equality of capacity.
- **Premises**: (p. 126) if women are naturally unfit for something, freedom will not make them do it + (p. 126) if they are fit, exclusion is a loss.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 126) legal exclusion is either superfluous or harmful.

## Evidence offered

- The history of slavery and serfdom: the Church's thousand-year failure to stop the powerful from using force until they were met by force (pp. 110-111).
- British slave-holding within living memory; American slavery ended three or four years before the time of writing (p. 111).
- Aristotle's natural slaves and the Southern slaveholders' doctrine (p. 113).
- Reactions to a queen in distant countries; women in feudal war and politics; Spartan women and Plato (p. 114).
- Women's petitions to Parliament for the suffrage, the suffrage society, and movements in the United States, France, Italy, Switzerland and Russia (p. 115).
- Wives who dare not use the laws made for their protection, since they are returned to the power of the man who injured them (pp. 115-116).
- The blacksmith, who needs no law requiring strong arms (p. 118).
- The Irish cottier, the French and the Turks as cases where circumstance is mistaken for nature (p. 122).
- The impressment of sailors and the slaveholder's argument, as parallels to compelling women into marriage (p. 127).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The burden-of-proof rule, a presumption for freedom and impartiality with restraint needing positive reasons of justice or policy (p. 105), is On Liberty's position restated as a rule of evidence, and matches Utilitarianism V's presumption of equal treatment unless expediency requires otherwise (ch14, p. 245).
- The decision is to rest on 'an enlightened estimate of tendencies and consequences' for humanity without distinction of sex (p. 121): the standard is utilitarian. Yet the opening calls the subordination 'wrong in itself' (p. 104). ch14's account of justice as the most binding class of utilities is what reconciles the two.
- Custom mistaken for nature (p. 114) is ch01's 'second nature ... continually mistaken for the first' (p. 12). The education of women to have no will of their own (p. 116) is ch03's picture of people who 'have no nature to follow' (p. 58), applied to half the population by design.
- 'There are no means of finding what either one person or many can do, but by trying' (p. 126) is the experiments-of-living argument of ch03 (p. 53).
- The agnosticism about natural differences (p. 123) is not kept up in ch08, where Mill offers conjectures about women's practical bent and nervous temperament. He flags here that conjectures are all that can be made (p. 123).
- On Liberty let Mormon women's consent to polygamy stand, while noting that custom makes marriage 'the one thing needful' (ch04, p. 84). Here the same fact is used to deny that women's acceptance is voluntary (pp. 116-117).
- Mill's free-market optimism (pp. 118, 126) assumes competition sorts by fitness. He does not consider that opinion and unequal preparation would continue to exclude after legal bars fall, though his own account of education (p. 116) gives the reason to expect it.

## Open questions

- If nothing can be known of women's nature (p. 121), what entitles Mill to say that freedom lets that nature have 'free play' and that competition will assign women to what they are 'most fit' for (p. 126)?
- The origin of an institution does not settle its present value, as Mill grants (p. 112). How much of the chapter's force depends on readers forgetting that?
- Does the argument reach social and economic pressure, or only legal disabilities? The thesis sentence names legal subordination (p. 104); the analysis of education and opinion (pp. 116-117) goes much further.
