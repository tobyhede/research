# ch07: The Subjection of Women II (pp. 128–146)

Scope note: chapter text pp. 128-146; no footnotes by Mill. The legal position described is that of England at the time of writing; the editor's notes (ap02) record later changes.

## Question this chapter answers

What does the law of marriage make of the wife, why is the husband's legal power indefensible, and what should replace it?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| bond-servant | p. 129 | The wife's legal status: bound to lifelong obedience, able to act only by her husband's permission, and unable to hold property. |
| one person in law | p. 130 | The legal fiction uniting husband and wife, used to infer that what is hers is his and never the converse. |
| settlement / separate use | p. 129 | Contracts by which the rich withdraw a wife's inherited property from the husband's control. They protect the capital without giving her the use of it. |
| legal separation | p. 131 | The court decree allowing a wife to live apart and keep her earnings. Distinguished from divorce with liberty to remarry, which Mill declines to discuss. |
| despotism in the family | p. 132 | The husband's legal power, compared point for point with political despotism. |
| the shrewish sanction | p. 135 | The wife's power to retaliate by making life disagreeable. It works best against the gentlest husbands. |
| power and freedom | p. 136 | Distinguished: a wife's influence over her husband gives her what she has no right to but does not let her assert her rights. |
| partnership | p. 137 | Mill's model for marriage: a voluntary association in which powers are divided by agreement and not fixed by law. |
| morality of submission, of chivalry, of justice | p. 141 | Three historical stages of moral life. The third rests on equal and sympathetic association. |
| school of despotism / school of the virtues of freedom | pp. 141-142 | What the family is now, and what it would be if justly constituted. |
| self-worship | p. 140 | The habit, taught to men by their position, of treating their own will as law for another rational being. |

Shift to note: 'slavery' is used literally of the wife's legal position (pp. 129-131) and Mill insists he describes law and not usual treatment (p. 131). 'Power' is consistently opposed to 'freedom' and 'rights' (p. 136), as in On Liberty ch05 (p. 95).

## Leading propositions

- Society has made marriage women's appointed destination and secured it by foul means and not fair (p. 128).
- In law the wife is the bond-servant of her husband: she vows obedience, can act only by his permission, and can acquire no property that does not become his (p. 129).
- In respect of property her position under English common law is worse than that of slaves under many codes (p. 129).
- A wife cannot refuse her husband 'the last familiarity', which even a female slave in Christian countries has an admitted right to refuse her master (p. 130).
- The children are by law the husband's; she can do nothing in relation to them except by delegation (p. 130).
- She cannot withdraw: if she leaves she takes nothing, and he can compel her return; legal separation is given only for desertion or extreme cruelty (pp. 130-131).
- For those allowed nothing but servitude, free choice of servitude is the only alleviation; Mill does not say she ought to have it and sets the question of divorce aside (p. 131).
- This is the legal position, not the actual treatment; the laws are worse than the people who execute them (p. 131).
- Mitigation in practice is no defence of despotism in the family, any more than of political despotism (p. 132).
- Laws and institutions must be adapted to bad men, not good ones; marriage gives absolute power to every adult male, down to the basest (pp. 133-134).
- There can be no real check on brutality while the victim is left in the power of the offender; conviction for violence should entitle the wife to divorce or at least separation (pp. 133-134).
- The relation of superiors to dependents is the nursery of wilfulness and selfishness; the family is, for its head, a school of those vices (pp. 134-135).
- A wife's influence is not a compensation for the loss of freedom and is as often used for bad ends as good, since she is taught she has no business with matters outside the family (pp. 136-137).
- It is not true that in every voluntary association one party must be absolute master, still less that the law must say which: business partnership shows otherwise (p. 137).
- The natural arrangement is a division of powers by consent, each supreme in their own department, with changes of principle requiring both (pp. 137-138).
- The equality of married persons before the law is the only way to make the relation just and the only way to make daily life a school of moral cultivation (p. 140).
- The only school of genuine moral sentiment is society between equals (p. 140).
- The true virtue of human beings is fitness to live together as equals (p. 141).
- A love of freedom held by a man who is absolute master of those nearest him is only a feeling for his own dignity (p. 142).
- The principle asked for would make all marriages what the best already are (p. 142).
- St. Paul's injunction to wives carries no more authority for the institution than his injunction to slaves does for slavery (p. 143).
- Whatever would be the husband's or wife's if unmarried should remain under that person's exclusive control (p. 144).
- Where the family lives on earnings, the common division, the man earning and the wife managing the household, seems in general the most suitable; in a just state of things it is not desirable that the wife contribute by labour to the family income (pp. 144-145).
- A woman who marries may be understood to choose household management and the raising of a family as the first call on her exertions, though nothing should prevent exceptional faculties from following their vocation (pp. 145-146).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (pp. 129-131) the wife owes obedience, holds no property, has no rights over her children, cannot refuse her husband's body and cannot leave + (p. 130) no slave is a slave at all hours or in all these respects.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 130-131) in law the wife's position is slavery, and in some respects worse. The argument is from the content of the law and is checkable against it; I cannot verify the statements of English law from this text alone, and rely on the editor's notes (ap02) for their accuracy.
- **Premises**: (p. 133) institutions must be judged by what they permit bad men to do + (p. 133) the power of a husband is given to every man without test of fitness + (pp. 134-135) power over dependents brings out selfishness even in ordinary men.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 132) the usual kindness of husbands is no more a defence of the law than the mildness of some kings is a defence of absolutism. The argument applies to the family the standard liberal case against unchecked political power, as Mill says (p. 134).
- **Premises**: (p. 137) partnerships work without a legally designated master + (p. 138) in practice married people already settle matters by qualifications and compromise + (p. 138) a court that always found for one side would give that side no motive to compromise.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 138-139) the husband's legal supremacy is not what makes marriages workable; it only makes every concession revocable.
- **Premises**: (pp. 140-141) moral sentiments are formed by the relations people live in + (p. 141) existing moralities were formed by relations of command and obedience + (pp. 141-142) the family is where character is first and most constantly formed.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 142) until the family is a relation of equals, mankind's moral training will not fit the equal society toward which everything else is tending. Unstated premise: the dispositions learned at home carry over to public life. This is the chapter's link between private justice and general utility.

## Evidence offered

- Legal history: marriage by force or sale; the husband's ancient power of life and death; 'petty treason' punished by burning (pp. 128-129).
- The Roman slave's peculium; Uncle Tom's cabin as a private life that a wife lacks (pp. 129-130).
- Serjeant Talfourd's Act on custody (p. 130).
- Wives and slaves faithful under the Roman proscriptions; gratitude toward those who refrain from using power (p. 132).
- Louis XVI compared with Philippe le Bel, Nadir Shah and Caligula (p. 132).
- Business partnership (p. 137).
- Married women's property provisions in constitutions of American states (p. 144).
- The observation that brutal men feel a contempt for their own wife that they feel for no one else (p. 143). Offered as something an acute observer may check.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- On Liberty ch05 said the State must control any power it lets one person hold over another and named the 'almost despotic power of husbands over wives' (p. 94). This chapter is that sentence worked out.
- The claim that society between equals is the school of moral sentiment (p. 140) is the claim of Utilitarianism III that equal society generates the feeling of unity that sanctions utilitarian morality (ch12, pp. 218-219). The 'morality of justice' (p. 141) is ch14's justice as the most binding class of social utilities.
- Mill sets divorce aside twice (p. 131, p. 145) although his argument that there is no check while the victim remains in the offender's power (p. 133) points straight at it. On Liberty ch05 treats marriage as a releasable engagement qualified by obligations to children (pp. 93-94).
- The endorsement of the customary division of labour (pp. 144-145) sits awkwardly with ch06's argument that nothing can be known about what women are fit for and that choice should be left free (pp. 123, 126). Mill frames it as what a woman 'may in general be understood' to choose by marrying, compares it to a man's choice of profession, and leaves it to opinion and not law (pp. 145-146). The comparison overlooks that a man choosing a profession does not thereby give up an independent income, which Mill himself has just called essential to dignity (p. 145).
- He concedes that the breadwinner will have 'a more potential voice' and attributes this to the general conditions of society and not to marriage law (p. 138). Legal equality therefore leaves an economic inequality in place that his own division of labour produces.
- The remarks on Islam and Brahminism as religions of stationary or declining peoples (pp. 143-144) repeat the civilizational ranking of On Liberty ch03 (pp. 65-66). Asserted, not argued.
- 'Self-worship' taught to men by privilege (p. 140) is developed in ch09 as a main benefit of reform.

## Open questions

- If conviction for violence should bring separation (p. 134) and people unfit for equal association are 'a proper subject for the law of divorce' (p. 140), what is Mill's view of divorce? The text withholds it.
- How is a wife who has chosen household management to keep the 'power of earning' that protects her (p. 145)?
- Mill says a great majority of upper-class English couples already live as equals (p. 142). On what evidence? None is given, and it conflicts with the claim that most men know nothing of their wives' minds (ch06, p. 124).
