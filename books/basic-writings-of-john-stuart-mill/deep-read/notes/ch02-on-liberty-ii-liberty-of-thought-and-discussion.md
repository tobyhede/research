# ch02: On Liberty II: Of the Liberty of Thought and Discussion (pp. 19–53)

Scope note: the chapter text runs pp. 19-51. Mill's own footnotes, marked with asterisks and daggers in the text, are printed at the end of the chapter (pp. 51-53); numbered superscripts point to the editor's notes (ap01).

## Question this chapter answers

May society, by law or by opinion, ever rightly silence the expression of an opinion?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| assumption of infallibility | p. 20 | Not feeling sure of a doctrine, but undertaking to decide the question for others without letting them hear the other side (p. 26). |
| corrigibility | p. 23 | The quality that makes human judgment worth anything: errors can be put right by discussion and experience. Reliance on judgment is warranted only while the means of correcting it are at hand. |
| rational assurance | p. 22 | The only certainty open to a fallible being: an opinion that has stood against complete liberty of contradiction (pp. 22, 24). |
| dead dogma / living truth | p. 35 | A true opinion held without discussion is held as a prejudice; a living truth is held with its grounds and felt in conduct. |
| social stigma / social intolerance | p. 32 | The penalty of opinion, as efficacious as law for anyone who depends on the good will of others. It roots out no opinions but makes men disguise them. |
| negative logic | p. 43 | Criticism that exposes weakness without establishing positive truth; poor as a result, invaluable as a means. |
| half-truths / one-sidedness | p. 44 | Popular opinions are usually part of the truth; heretical opinions usually carry the suppressed part. |
| Christian morality (so called) | pp. 46-47 | Better named theological morality: built by the early Church, a reaction against paganism, negative and passive, a doctrine of obedience. Distinguished from the sayings of Christ (p. 48). |
| mental well-being of mankind | p. 49 | The good on which all other well-being depends, and the stated ground of the four conclusions. |
| morality of public discussion | p. 51 | Candour, stating opponents' views honestly, and judging advocacy by its manner and not by its side. Enforced by opinion only, never by law. |

## Leading propositions

- The power to coerce opinion is illegitimate even when government and people are at one; it is as noxious, or more so, when used in accordance with public opinion (p. 20).
- All mankind minus one would have no more right to silence the one than he would to silence them (p. 20).
- Silencing an opinion robs the human race, and those who dissent from it more than those who hold it (p. 20).
- First branch: the suppressed opinion may be true, and to refuse it a hearing is to assume infallibility (p. 20).
- Acting on an opinion is justified only because the opinion has been left open to refutation; presuming it true for action differs from assuming it true in order to forbid refutation (p. 22).
- Usefulness cannot be substituted for truth as the ground of protection, because the usefulness of an opinion is itself a matter of opinion and its truth is part of its utility (p. 25).
- The men who condemned Socrates and Christ, and Marcus Aurelius who persecuted Christians, were not bad men; conscientious persecution is the dangerous kind (pp. 26-29).
- It is false that truth always triumphs over persecution; persecution has usually succeeded (pp. 29-30).
- England still has legal penalties for opinion, but the effective penalty is social stigma, and its cost is the moral courage of the human mind (pp. 30-33).
- The ban on heresy harms the orthodox most, since it cramps the mental development of those who dare not think (p. 33).
- Freedom of thought is needed even more for average minds than for great thinkers (p. 34).
- Second branch: a true opinion not fully, frequently and fearlessly discussed is held as a dead dogma (p. 35).
- On any subject where difference is possible, knowing only one's own side is not knowledge; one must hear opponents from those who believe them (p. 36).
- Without discussion the meaning of a doctrine is lost, not only its grounds; Christian maxims are the example (pp. 38-41).
- Mill does not say unanimity is bad: as mankind improve, the number of undisputed truths will grow, but the loss of contest is a drawback that teachers should make up by contrivance (p. 42).
- Third and commonest case: conflicting doctrines share the truth, and the nonconforming one supplies what the received one omits (p. 44).
- On open practical questions the minority opinion has the better claim to be encouraged (p. 45).
- Free discussion does not cure sectarianism in partisans; it works on the calmer bystander (p. 49).
- The four grounds are recapitulated: the silenced opinion may be true; it may hold a portion of truth; an uncontested truth is held as a prejudice; and its meaning is lost or enfeebled (pp. 49-50).
- Temperate manner cannot be made a legal condition of free expression; opinion should condemn bad advocacy on whichever side it appears (pp. 50-51).
- Instigation to tyrannicide may be punished only where an overt act followed and a probable connection can be shown (p. 52, Mill's footnote).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 20) if the silenced opinion is right, mankind lose the chance to exchange error for truth + (p. 20) if it is wrong, they lose the livelier impression of truth produced by its collision with error + (p. 20) we can never be sure it is false.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 20) suppression is an evil on either hypothesis. The chapter is the working out of this dilemma, later extended by a third case (p. 44).
- **Premises**: (p. 23) human judgment is valuable only because it is corrigible + (p. 23) correction needs discussion as well as experience + (p. 24) even the best-warranted beliefs rest only on a standing invitation to disprove them.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 22) liberty to contradict is the condition that justifies acting on an opinion at all. This answers the objection (pp. 21-22) that governments must act on their best judgment. Unstated premise: the gain in reliability outweighs any harm done by false opinions while they circulate. Mill gives no accounting of that harm.
- **Premises**: (p. 25) to judge an opinion noxious requires the same infallibility as to judge it false + (p. 25) truth is part of utility.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 25) the appeal to social usefulness only shifts the assumption of infallibility from one point to another.
- **Premises**: (p. 36) on disputed subjects truth is a balance between conflicting reasons + (p. 36) arguments must be met in their most plausible form, from those who hold them.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 37) an unopposed believer does not know the doctrine he professes; if opponents do not exist they must be imagined.
- **Premises**: (p. 44) one-sidedness is the rule in the human mind + (p. 45) truth in practical life is a reconciling of opposites, reached through a struggle between combatants.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 46) only through diversity of opinion do all sides of the truth get fair play.

## Evidence offered

- The devil's advocate at a canonization; Newtonian philosophy as trusted because open to question (p. 24).
- Socrates, condemned for impiety and immorality (pp. 26-27); the crucifixion, and Saint Paul among the persecutors (p. 27); Marcus Aurelius (pp. 27-29).
- The Reformation put down some twenty times before Luther; Protestantism rooted out in Spain, Italy, Flanders and the Austrian empire (p. 30).
- The 1857 cases: Pooley imprisoned for words about Christianity; jurymen rejected and a witness denied justice for unbelief (pp. 30-31, footnotes p. 52). The oath rule admits atheists who lie and rejects only those who tell the truth (p. 31).
- Three periods of mental freedom: after the Reformation, the later eighteenth century, and Germany in the time of Goethe and Fichte (p. 34).
- How Christians hold the precepts of the New Testament, contrasted with the early Christians (pp. 40-41).
- Rousseau's paradoxes against eighteenth-century self-satisfaction (p. 45); parties of order and of progress (p. 45).
- Mill's footnote on the Sepoy insurrection and an Under-Secretary's speech limiting toleration to Christians (pp. 52-53).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The argument never uses the harm principle of ch01. It rests on the interest of mankind in truth and in 'mental well-being' (p. 49). ch01 said expression belongs to conduct that concerns others (p. 17); the only limit ch02 itself gives is instigation, in a footnote (p. 52), and ch03 opens with the corn-dealer case (p. 53).
- Mill grants that truth has no inherent power against persecution (p. 30) and that discussion heightens sectarianism (p. 49), so the case for free discussion is not that truth reliably wins. It is that suppression removes the only means of correction.
- The claim that consolidation of opinion is 'inevitable and indispensable' (p. 42) sits uneasily with the claim that diversity is needed until mankind are far more advanced (p. 44). Mill's resolution is a contrived substitute for real opponents (pp. 42-43).
- The stress on social stigma (p. 32) is the application to opinion of the social tyranny of ch01 (p. 11). Yet Mill wants opinion to police the manner of discussion (p. 51), so opinion is both the danger and the remedy.
- The treatment of Christian morality as passive obedience (p. 47) returns in ch03 as the 'Calvinistic theory', and in The Subjection of Women as the ethic of submission taught to women.
- The 'judicious utilitarianism of Aristotle' (p. 26) and truth as 'part of utility' (p. 25) show the utilitarian frame at work without being argued for; see ch11.

## Open questions

- Does the argument cover expression that is not opinion on a disputable matter, such as false statements of fact about a person? The examples are all doctrines of religion, morals and politics.
- If social stigma is as efficacious as law (p. 32), what may individuals do to show disapproval of an opinion without joining in the tyranny? ch04 gives Mill's answer for conduct.
- Mill concedes mathematics is different because all the argument is on one side (p. 35). Where does the boundary fall for well-established empirical findings?
