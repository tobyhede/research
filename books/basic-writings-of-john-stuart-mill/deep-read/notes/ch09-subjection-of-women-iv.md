# ch09: The Subjection of Women IV (pp. 172–192)

Scope note: chapter text pp. 172-192; no footnotes by Mill. Two Greek words are missing from this EPUB at p. 177 and appear as empty brackets; the sentence is not quoted here.

## Question this chapter answers

What good would come of freeing women: would mankind be better off?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| abstract right | p. 172 | The ground the objector supposes Mill to stand on. Mill answers by listing benefits, that is on the ground of utility. |
| law of servitude in marriage | p. 172 | The husband's legal power. A contradiction of the principles of the modern world and the only actual bondage known to the law (p. 173). |
| self-worship | p. 173 | The unearned sense of superiority men acquire from being born male; said to be the root of all selfish propensities. |
| principle of the modern movement | p. 175 | Conduct alone entitles to respect; merit and not birth is the only rightful claim to power and authority. |
| chivalry | pp. 177-178 | The moral ideal produced by women's influence in a society of individual prowess: warlike virtue joined to gentleness toward the defenceless. Now superseded by justice and prudence backed by law. |
| Mrs. Grundy | p. 182 | Conventional social opinion, to which a married man has 'given hostages'. |
| ideal of marriage | pp. 186-187 | A union of two persons of cultivated faculties, alike in opinions and purposes, with similarity of powers and reciprocal superiority, each leading and being led. |
| rational freedom | p. 187 | A life governed by one's own sense of duty and by restraints one's own conscience can accept, as against subjection to another's will. |
| liberty and power | pp. 188-189 | Antagonists. A mind denied liberty seeks power over others; the desire of power ceases to deprave only where each can do without it. |
| worthy outlet for the active faculties | p. 189 | A pursuit one relishes. Its absence is, after disease, indigence and guilt, the thing most fatal to enjoyment of life. |

Shift to note: happiness here is not presented as a sum of pleasures. Freedom is 'the first and strongest want of human nature' after food and raiment (p. 187), personal independence is 'an element of happiness' (p. 187), and restraint on freedom dries up 'the principal fountain of human happiness' (p. 192). This is the constitutive view of ch13 (things desired for themselves are parts of happiness) applied to liberty.

## Leading propositions

- For marriage the question of benefit hardly arises: the evils of subjecting individual women to individual men are too terrible to overlook, and abuse cannot be checked while the power remains (p. 172).
- Marriage is the only actual bondage known to English law (p. 173).
- First benefit of equality: the most universal of human relations would be regulated by justice (p. 173).
- The present relation is the source of male self-worship; a boy learns that by birth alone he is the superior of half the human race (p. 173).
- The family so constituted trains men in arrogance, which breaks out toward all who must tolerate it (pp. 174-175).
- While the right of the strong rules at the heart of society, justice will never possess men's inmost sentiments (p. 175).
- Second benefit: doubling the mass of mental faculties available for the higher service of humanity, with the added stimulus of competition to men (pp. 175-176).
- The mere removal of the barrier would expand women's faculties and moral sentiments, whether or not they took part in public affairs (p. 176).
- Third benefit: women's influence on opinion, always considerable, would become more beneficial (p. 177).
- That influence has softened manners and stimulated courage, producing chivalry; modern life must rest on justice and prudence and on penal sanctions, not on the generosity of the strong (pp. 177-178).
- As things are, women's influence favours the softer virtues but is 'somewhat lower' in justice and seldom supports disinterested public conduct (p. 179).
- In philanthropy women's influence is often mischievous, because an education of sentiment makes them look to immediate effects on persons and not to remote effects on classes (p. 180).
- A wife is the auxiliary of common public opinion and holds her husband to mediocrity; this is the natural effect of her position, since consideration is the only return society makes her (pp. 181-182).
- Difference of education makes real union of thoughts in marriage nearly impossible; unlikeness may attract but likeness retains (p. 183).
- Where the wife is inferior in cultivation the husband deteriorates, and the more so the happier the marriage (pp. 185-186).
- The ideal of marriage is between equals in cultivation, and every other notion of it is a relic of primitive barbarism (pp. 186-187).
- The moral regeneration of mankind will begin only when the most fundamental social relation is placed under equal justice (p. 187).
- The most direct benefit is the gain in private happiness to women themselves: the difference between subjection to the will of others and rational freedom (p. 187).
- Men judge the value of independence one way for themselves and another for others (pp. 187-188).
- Denied liberty, an active mind seeks power; hence the meddling, and the passion for dress and display, charged against women (pp. 188-189).
- Many women have no worthy outlet for their faculties, especially the unmarried and those whose children are grown; the feeling of a wasted life produces much unhappiness (pp. 189-191).
- Every restraint on the freedom of conduct of a fellow creature, other than holding him responsible for evil actually caused, dries up the principal fountain of human happiness (pp. 191-192).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 173) character is formed by the relations in which people grow up + (pp. 173-174) boys grow up believing themselves superior by birth + (p. 175) society then has to curb the propensities it has built.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 175) equality in the family would remove a main source of selfishness and injustice in men. Unstated premise: no other source would replace it. Mill's claim that all selfish propensities 'have their source and root' here (p. 173) is far stronger than the argument needs or supports.
- **Premises**: (p. 175) ability is everywhere below demand + (p. 175) half of all talent is at present unused for public purposes.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 175) admission of women would roughly double the supply. Unstated premise: ability is distributed alike in both sexes, which ch06 said cannot be known (p. 123). The argument needs only that a large amount is wasted.
- **Premises**: (p. 182) a wife's social consideration depends on her husband's conformity + (p. 182) she has paid for it with lifelong self-sacrifice and has no share in his purposes.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 181-183) she will press him toward conventional opinion, and society is kept in 'mediocrity of respectability'. The remedy is her education and independence, not her exclusion.
- **Premises**: (p. 187) freedom is among the first wants of human nature + (p. 188) every man knows this in his own case and when emerging from boyhood + (p. 188) women feel the same.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 187) emancipation is a direct and very large gain in happiness to half the species. This is an appeal to the reader's own experience, like the proof in ch13 (p. 224).
- **Premises**: (pp. 188-189) where liberty is denied, the desire for power takes its place.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 189) the faults charged against women are products of their subjection, and respect for liberty is the cure for the love of power generally.

## Evidence offered

- The hereditary king, the feudal lord and Figaro's remark about taking the trouble to be born (p. 174).
- The conversion of the Anglo-Saxons and Franks, begun by the wives of Ethelbert and Clovis (p. 177).
- Chivalry as a historical case of moral influence (pp. 177-178).
- Women who administer charities, who see the demoralising effect of alms better than male political economists (p. 180).
- The Dissenter or radical whose wife regrets the social cost of his opinions (pp. 182-183).
- Priests' influence over wives in Catholic countries (p. 183).
- Men who retire from business and decline, as a parallel to women left without occupation (p. 189).
- The feeling of release on emerging from boyhood (p. 188).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- This is where the three works meet. The objector asks why make a revolution 'in the name of an abstract right' (p. 172); Mill answers with consequences, as On Liberty forgoes abstract right for utility (ch01, p. 16). The closing sentence states the harm principle (restraint only by responsibility for evil actually caused) and grounds it in happiness (pp. 191-192).
- Liberty as an ingredient of happiness (p. 187) is the reading of 'utility in the largest sense' that On Liberty ch03 needs, and it uses Utilitarianism IV's device of parts of happiness (ch13, pp. 222-223). The 'sense of dignity' of Utilitarianism II (ch11, p. 198) reappears as 'the sentiment of personal dignity' (p. 189).
- 'The love of power and the love of liberty are in eternal antagonism' (p. 189) completes On Liberty ch05's remark that people value liberty much less than power (p. 95).
- The wife as enforcer of conventional opinion (pp. 181-183) gives On Liberty's tyranny of opinion (ch01, p. 11; ch03, p. 63) a mechanism inside the household. The 'mediocrity of respectability' (p. 183) is ch03's collective mediocrity (p. 62).
- The moral psychology of equality (pp. 173-175) is Utilitarianism III's: society between equals teaches regard for the interests of all (ch12, p. 218).
- The criticism of women's charity for undermining self-help (p. 180) shows Mill's political economy and repeats On Liberty's distrust of doing for people what they can do for themselves (ch05, p. 99).
- The chapter is frankly unflattering about women as they now are: lower in justice (p. 179), a 'dead weight' on a superior husband (p. 181), 'hopelessly inferior' in cultivation (p. 186). Mill attributes all of it to education and position, consistently with ch06 and ch08, but the argument that men are harmed by inferior wives is addressed to men's self-interest and sits beside the argument from justice without being reconciled with it.
- The marriage ideal (pp. 186-187) is plainly autobiographical, as the dedication of On Liberty suggests (fm01, p. 8); Schneewind's claim that Subjection answers the charge of self-centred individualism rests on this passage (fm01, p. 6).

## Open questions

- If the benefits failed to appear, would the subjection cease to be wrong? ch06 called it 'wrong in itself' (p. 104). The chapter never says which ground is basic.
- Mill says women with families have a sufficient outlet in them (p. 189). Is that consistent with the claim that every person needs the free disposal of their own faculties (p. 189)?
- The prediction that equal marriage will begin 'the moral regeneration of mankind' (p. 187) is a very large empirical claim. What evidence could bear on it?
