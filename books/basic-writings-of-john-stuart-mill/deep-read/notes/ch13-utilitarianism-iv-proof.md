# ch13: Utilitarianism IV: Of What Sort of Proof the Principle of Utility Is Susceptible (pp. 220–226)

Scope note: chapter text pp. 220-226; no footnotes by Mill. The numbered superscripts point to long editorial notes in ap03, where the standard objections to this chapter are reported.

## Question this chapter answers

What considerations can show that happiness is desirable, and the only thing desirable, as an end?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| desirable | p. 221 | What is good as an end or as a means. Questions about ends are questions about what is desirable. |
| proof | p. 220 | Not proof by reasoning, which no first principle admits, but an appeal to the faculties that judge of fact: the senses and internal consciousness (pp. 220-221). |
| happiness as a concrete whole | p. 223 | Not an abstract idea or a collective something to which particular pleasures are means. Its ingredients are each desired in themselves and are parts of the end. |
| part of happiness | p. 222 | The status of anything desired for its own sake, including things first desired as means (virtue, money, power, fame) that association has made into ends. |
| virtue | pp. 221-222 | Originally valued for its conduciveness to pleasure and protection from pain. Capable of becoming, to the individual, a good in itself, and so part of his happiness. |
| desire | p. 224 | Inseparable from finding the idea of a thing pleasant. Desiring a thing and thinking of it as pleasant are two names for one psychological fact. |
| will | p. 225 | The active phenomenon, distinct from desire, a state of passive sensibility. Originally an offshoot of desire that habit can detach from it. |
| habit | p. 225 | What gives certainty to feeling and conduct. A will to do right that has become habitual is a means to good, not intrinsically a good (p. 226). |

Shift to note: 'happiness' in ch11 was pleasure and absence of pain (p. 196). Here it becomes a whole made of parts, and anything a person desires for its own sake counts as a part of his happiness (pp. 222-223). The term has widened enough to absorb virtue.

## Leading propositions

- First principles of knowledge and of conduct alike cannot be proved by reasoning; the question is which faculty takes cognizance of ends (pp. 220-221).
- The utilitarian doctrine is that happiness is desirable, and the only thing desirable, as an end (p. 221).
- The sole evidence that anything is desirable is that people do actually desire it, as the only proof that an object is visible is that people see it (p. 221).
- Each person desires his own happiness; so each person's happiness is a good to that person, and the general happiness a good to the aggregate of all persons (p. 221).
- This shows happiness is one of the ends of conduct. To show it is the sole end, it must be shown that people never desire anything else (p. 221).
- People do desire virtue, and utilitarians hold that it should be desired disinterestedly, for itself (pp. 221-222).
- The mind is not in a right state, not in the state most conducive to general happiness, unless it loves virtue as desirable in itself (p. 222).
- This is no departure from the happiness principle, because the ingredients of happiness are various and each is desirable in itself (p. 222).
- Money, power and fame are first desired as means and come by association to be desired for themselves, as parts of the person's happiness (pp. 222-223).
- Virtue is a good of this kind, with the difference that the love of it makes a person a blessing to others, so utility requires its cultivation to the greatest possible strength (pp. 223-224).
- There is in reality nothing desired except happiness (p. 224).
- Whether this is so is a question of fact, to be settled by self-observation and observation of others (p. 224).
- To desire anything except in proportion as the idea of it is pleasant is a physical and metaphysical impossibility (pp. 224-225).
- Will differs from desire and can persist by habit without thought of pleasure; but will is produced by desire in the first place and the habit of willing rightly is a means to good, not a good in itself (pp. 225-226).
- If the psychological doctrine is true the principle of utility is proved; whether it is true is left to the reader (p. 226).

## Arguments

The chapter is one argument in three steps.

- **Step 1. Premises**: (p. 221) the only evidence that a thing is desirable is that it is desired + (p. 221) each person desires his own happiness.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 221) each person's happiness is a good to that person. Unstated premise: 'desirable' is established by desire in the way 'visible' is established by sight. The analogy holds only if desirable means capable of being desired; the conclusion needs it to mean worthy of being desired. Mill's defence is that no other evidence is available for an ultimate end (p. 221), which makes this a claim about evidence and not a definition.
- **Step 2. Premises**: (p. 221) each person's happiness is a good to that person.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 221) the general happiness is a good to the aggregate of all persons. Unstated premise: goods to separate persons sum to a good for the collection, and that collective good gives each person a reason. The text gives one clause to this step. ch12's social feelings and ch14's equal claim of everyone to happiness (p. 245) are where support would have to come from.
- **Step 3. Premises**: (pp. 222-223) things desired for their own sake are desired as parts of happiness + (p. 224) desiring a thing and finding it pleasant are the same fact.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 224) nothing is desired but happiness, so happiness is the sole end and the criterion of morality. Unstated premise: 'part of happiness' is not being defined as whatever is desired for itself; if it is, the conclusion is true by definition and tells us nothing about pleasure. Mill rests the step on the empirical claim at pp. 224-225.
- **Reply to the objection from will. Premises**: (p. 225) will begins as desire and is detached by habit + (p. 226) what results from habit carries no presumption of being intrinsically good.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 226) the virtuous person who acts without thought of pleasure does not show that something other than pleasure is good in itself.

## Evidence offered

- The analogy of visible and audible (p. 221).
- The love of money as the clear case of a means become an end, with nothing originally more desirable about it than about 'any heap of glittering pebbles' (p. 222).
- Power and fame as partial parallels (p. 223).
- The appeal to 'practised self-consciousness and self-observation' (p. 224). No observations are reported; the reader is asked to make them.
- People with habits of hurtful indulgence, who will from habit against their deliberate preference (p. 225).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- ch10 said ultimate ends admit no proof in the ordinary sense, only considerations that can determine the intellect (p. 195). This chapter keeps that framing at the start (p. 220) and the end (p. 226), yet the middle uses the language of proof without qualification (p. 224).
- The treatment of virtue as a part of happiness is the same move On Liberty needs for individuality: something valued for itself that is nonetheless counted within utility 'in the largest sense' (ch01, p. 16; ch03, p. 54). If the move works for virtue it works for self-development, and the essay's utilitarianism is consistent. If it empties 'happiness' of content, both books lose their foundation together.
- The statement that people desire nothing except in proportion as the idea is pleasant (p. 225) is hard to square with ch11's competent judges, who prefer a higher pleasure 'even though knowing it to be attended with a greater amount of discontent' (p. 198).
- Associationist psychology does heavy work here (pp. 222-223) and in ch12 (p. 217). There Mill warned that wholly artificial associations dissolve under analysis. Whether the love of virtue survives the analysis offered in this chapter, which traces it to pleasure and protection from pain (p. 223), is the question Mill raises against others and does not put to himself.

## Open questions

- Is the claim on pp. 224-225 empirical, as Mill says ('a question of fact and experience'), or a matter of definition, as 'two different modes of naming the same psychological fact' suggests? It cannot easily be both.
- What would count as evidence against the claim that nothing is desired but happiness, once anything desired for itself is called a part of happiness?
- Does the step from each to the aggregate (p. 221) claim that every person has reason to promote the general happiness, or only that general happiness is a good in some impersonal sense?
