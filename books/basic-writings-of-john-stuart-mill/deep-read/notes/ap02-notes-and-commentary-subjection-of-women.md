# ap02: Notes and Commentary: The Subjection of Women (pp. 257–268)

Scope note: the author is the editor, Dale E. Miller. For each of the four chapters there is an interpretive summary followed by numbered notes keyed to the superscripts in Mill's text. The opening of the Chapter I commentary also gives the editor's case for reading the book now and a biographical account of Mill and Harriet Taylor. This note records the interpretive and factual claims that go beyond identifications.

## Question this unit answers

Why read The Subjection of Women now that the legal rights it argues for exist, and what context and criticism does its argument need?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| coercive social pressure | p. 258 | Non-legal pressure that keeps people from using freedoms the law grants. The editor ties it to On Liberty. |
| socialization | p. 258 | The editor's modern term for the education into a gender role that Mill describes. Dominance is now sustained by socialization more than force (p. 259). |
| intuitionism (its conservatism) | p. 260 | The doctrine that what seems self-evidently right is right. The editor says it fosters complacency with the status quo. |
| a priori (Mill's loose use) | p. 260 | Mill means only that we start on the side of liberty and change our minds for compelling reasons. |
| shrewish sanction | p. 263 | Mill's term, glossed: a wife's power to make her husband miserable. |
| family selfishness | p. 263 | The editor's label for putting the family's good ahead of justice and society. |
| regent | p. 265 | One who holds real power on behalf of a monarch; used in the notes on Mill's examples of women rulers. |

## Leading propositions

On reading the book now (Chapter I commentary):

- Mill argues for removing limits on women's rights to own property, get an advanced education, choose any occupation and take part in politics (p. 257).
- The book still matters for five reasons: social pressure can do what law no longer does; Mill's reasoning implies women should be encouraged to use their rights, and girls may be socialised so thoroughly that they never form the desire; it bears on current debates such as subsidised day care and abortion; some people still deny women anything like equal rights; and its account of how power warps character applies beyond the sexes (p. 258).
- Mill's description of the effects of political participation on character, developed in Considerations on Representative Government, has been influential in democratic theory (p. 258).

On Mill and Harriet Taylor:

- They met in 1830, when she was married; the relationship caused scandal; her husband died in 1849 and they married in 1851; she died in 1858 (pp. 258-259).
- Mill's praise of her was, in Alexander Bain's words, such as 'outraged all reasonable credibility'; others did not rate her abilities so highly (p. 259).
- Her influence on his work is very hard to ascertain. She published 'Enfranchisement of Women'. Mill believed in sexual equality before he met her, but probably attended to it more because of her and saw her as a living refutation of claims of women's inferiority (p. 259).

On the chapters:

- Chapter I: the subjection is a product of force and not of expediency; the principle of the rule of the strongest survives longest here; the modern consensus for individual discretion creates a strong presumption against the restrictions; and nature cannot be told apart from socialization (pp. 259-260).
- Mill's opening remark about feelings entrenched against argument illustrates his chief complaint against intuitionism, its conservatism (p. 260).
- Aristotle accepted natural slavery only in a qualified sense (p. 261).
- Plato in Republic V holds that there is no occupation from which women are excluded by nature, though he thinks men as a group superior in most (p. 261).
- The suffrage petition Mill mentions was organised in 1866 by the Kensington Society, whose members included Helen Taylor (p. 261).
- On Mill's remark that a man's opinions of women show what his wife is like: 'one might say that the same is true of Mill himself' (p. 261).
- Mill's remark about fathers who do not know their sons almost certainly reflects his own relation to James Mill (pp. 261-262).
- Chapter II: because of restrictions on earning, most women had little choice but to marry, and marriage gave the husband complete power (p. 262).
- The law worsens the characters of both: men's selfishness is strengthened; some women become too unselfish, some become household tyrants, and many develop family selfishness (pp. 262-263).
- Legal background: the Infant Custody Act of 1839 gave mothers custody of children under seven on conditions; only in 1852 did courts recognise that wives could leave intolerable husbands; until 1857 divorce required an act of Parliament; after 1857 a husband needed to show only infidelity, a wife infidelity plus desertion or cruelty (p. 263).
- 'Later feminists have justly criticized Mill' for assuming the woman will be primarily responsible for the care of children (p. 264).
- Chapter III: Mill disagreed vehemently with his father, whose article 'Government' held that women need not vote because fathers or husbands would protect their interests (p. 264).
- In 1867 Mill moved an amendment to the Second Reform Bill to enfranchise women on the same terms as men; British women gained the vote in 1918 and equal terms in 1928; American women in 1920 (pp. 264-265).
- The editor summarises Mill as holding that women tend to be less capable of speculative thought and perhaps more gifted at practical thought, while saying it is impossible to know how far this reflects nature (p. 265).
- One of the two women Mill says have written usefully on political economy is Harriet Martineau; the other is probably Harriet Taylor Mill (p. 266).
- Chapter IV: benefits to women are wider opportunities, an outlet for those without family duties, and the feeling of being in charge of their own lives. Benefits to society are a doubled pool of talent, better influence of women on public affairs, and better marriages (pp. 267-268).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 258) rights can be removed by social pressure as well as by law + (p. 258) girls may be socialised so thoroughly that they never want non-traditional lives.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 258) legal equality does not make the book obsolete. This extends Mill's argument in the direction ch06's analysis of education points (ch06, pp. 116-117), further than Mill's own stated thesis about legal subordination.
- **Premises**: (p. 260) long-familiar institutions seem self-evidently right + (p. 260) intuitionism treats what seems self-evident as true.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 260) intuitionism is conservative, and Mill's opposition to it is of a piece with his reforming politics. The editor uses this to link Subjection to Utilitarianism I.
- **Premises**: (p. 264) Mill assumes the wife will be primarily responsible for children.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 264) later feminist criticism of Mill on this point is just. The editor states the verdict without giving the argument; ch07 (pp. 144-146) supplies the passage it refers to.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The editor's summary of Chapter III states as Mill's view that women 'tend to be less capable of speculative thought than men' (p. 265). Mill's text is more guarded: he speaks of women 'as they now are' and denies knowledge of natural differences (ch08, pp. 152, 160). The summary keeps the disclaimer but leads with the generalisation.
- The editor reads Mill's remark that the 'shrewish sanction' yields counter-tyranny as a claim about what such women would do with equal power (p. 263). Mill says the weapon belongs to those who would misuse power (ch07, p. 135) and that legal subordination makes such characters more frequent (ch07, p. 140).
- The legal notes confirm the general accuracy of Mill's description of marriage law in ch07 and date it: several of the worst features had begun to be reformed between 1839 and 1857, before he wrote.
- The link drawn between intuitionism and conservatism (p. 260) ties ch06 to ch10 and to On Liberty ch01 (p. 12).
- The biographical account bears on ch09's ideal of marriage (pp. 186-187), on ch08's 'my own case' (p. 165), and on the dedication (fm01, p. 8). The editor's caution about Harriet's abilities and influence is a reason not to treat those passages as evidence.
- James Mill's argument against women's suffrage (p. 264) is the position ch08 answers when it says women need the vote as a guarantee of just consideration (p. 149).

## Open questions

- The editor asserts that Mill's reasoning implies women should be 'encouraged' to exercise their rights (p. 258). Mill says only that the matter should be left to women's own choice and to opinion rightly directed (ch06, p. 126; ch07, p. 146). Is the editor's reading an inference or an addition?
- The commentary does not discuss the tension between Mill's agnosticism about women's nature and his conjectures in Chapter III, which is the main internal difficulty of the book.
- A parenthetical remark about the Mills' private life (p. 258) is speculation by the editor and has no bearing on the argument; I do not rely on it.
