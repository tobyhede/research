# ch01: On Liberty I: Introductory (pp. 8–19)

Scope note: superscript numbers in the text point to the editor's notes (ap01). The chapter has no footnotes by Mill.

## Question this chapter answers

What are the nature and limits of the power that society may legitimately exercise over the individual, by law or by opinion?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civil, or Social Liberty | p. 8 | The subject of the essay: the limits of society's legitimate power over the individual. Explicitly not freedom of the will. |
| liberty (old sense) | p. 8 | Protection against the tyranny of political rulers, won through recognised immunities and constitutional checks (pp. 8-9). |
| tyranny of the majority | p. 11 | Oppression of part of the people by the most numerous or most active part. At first feared as acting through public authorities. |
| social tyranny | p. 11 | Society executing its own mandates through opinion and feeling. Mill calls it more formidable than many kinds of political oppression because it leaves fewer means of escape. |
| custom | p. 12 | The source of the illusion that prevailing rules of conduct are self-evident. A second nature continually mistaken for the first. |
| the principle (the 'one very simple principle') | p. 15 | The sole end that warrants interference with anyone's liberty of action is self-protection; power may be used against a member of a civilized community against his will only to prevent harm to others. |
| harm to others | p. 15 | Used interchangeably in this chapter with 'evil to some one else' (p. 15), an act 'hurtful to others' (p. 16), and conduct that 'concerns the interest of other people' (p. 16). Not defined. |
| self-regarding sphere | p. 17 | Conduct affecting only the agent, or others only with their free, voluntary and undeceived consent. 'Only himself' means directly and in the first instance. |
| utility in the largest sense | p. 16 | The ultimate appeal on all ethical questions, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being. |
| maturity of faculties | p. 15 | The condition for the principle applying. Excludes children and 'backward states of society'. |
| compulsion and control | p. 15 | Covers both legal penalties and the moral coercion of public opinion. |

Shift to note: liberty moves from protection against rulers (p. 8), to limits on the majority's government (p. 11), to limits on opinion itself (p. 11), to a positive formula, pursuing our own good in our own way (p. 18).

## Leading propositions

- Popular government does not remove the need to limit power, because the people who exercise power are not the same as those over whom it is exercised (p. 10).
- The will of the people means in practice the will of the majority or of those who pass for it, who may wish to oppress a part (pp. 10-11).
- Society can tyrannise without the magistrate, through prevailing opinion and feeling, and protection is needed against that too (p. 11).
- Where to place the limit between individual independence and social control is a question on which nearly everything remains to be done (p. 11).
- Rules of conduct in fact rest on the likings and dislikings of society or its powerful part, often on class interest, and people give no reasons for them (pp. 12-13).
- Only in religious belief has the individual's right against society been defended on principle, and even there toleration is admitted with tacit reserves (pp. 13-14).
- There is no recognised principle for testing government interference, so it is as often wrongly invoked as wrongly condemned (pp. 14-15).
- The harm principle: prevention of harm to others is the only purpose for which power may be exercised over a member of a civilized community against his will; his own good is not a sufficient warrant (p. 15).
- A person's own good is a reason for remonstrating, reasoning, persuading or entreating, not for compelling (p. 15).
- The principle applies only to people in the maturity of their faculties. It excludes children and societies not yet capable of being improved by free and equal discussion, for which despotism is legitimate if it aims at and achieves improvement (pp. 15-16).
- Mill forgoes any appeal to abstract right independent of utility (p. 16).
- A person may be compelled to perform some positive acts for others, such as giving evidence, sharing in common defence and saving a life; inaction can cause evil, though liability for it is the exception (pp. 16-17).
- Even where conduct affects others there are often good reasons of expediency for not enforcing responsibility (p. 17).
- The region of liberty has three parts: conscience, thought and expression; tastes and pursuits, framing the plan of our life; and combination among consenting adults (p. 17).
- No society is free where these liberties are not on the whole respected, whatever its form of government (p. 18).
- The tendency of the age is to strengthen society against the individual, and only a barrier of moral conviction will stop it (p. 19).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 10) under popular government rulers and ruled are still different people + (p. 11) the majority may desire to oppress a minority.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 11) limiting the power of government over individuals loses none of its importance under democracy.
- **Premises**: (p. 11) society enforces its mandates by opinion as well as law + (p. 11) opinion penetrates more deeply into the details of life than law does.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 11) there is a limit to the legitimate interference of collective opinion, and finding it is as indispensable as protection against political despotism.
- **Premises**: (p. 12) an opinion on conduct unsupported by reasons is only a preference + (pp. 12-13) existing moral rules largely express preference and class interest.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 15) a principle is needed, and Mill supplies one. Unstated premise: the fact that existing rules have bad origins shows they lack authority, which does not follow without the further claim that no good reasons can be found for them.
- **Premises**: (p. 16) utility is the ultimate appeal + (p. 16) the relevant utility is that of the permanent interests of man as a progressive being + (p. 18) mankind gain more by letting each live as seems good to himself than by compelling each to live as seems good to the rest.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 16) those interests authorise control only over actions that concern the interest of others. The middle premise is asserted here and argued in ch02 and ch03.

## Evidence offered

- A stylised history of liberty: Greece, Rome, England; immunities, then constitutional checks, then elective rulers (pp. 8-10).
- The United States as the democratic republic whose existence exposed the faults of the theory that the people need no protection from themselves (p. 10).
- Moralities shaped by class: Spartans and Helots, planters and negroes, princes and subjects, nobles and roturiers, men and women (pp. 12-13).
- The history of religious toleration: reformers as intolerant as the church they left; toleration won only where no party could win outright (pp. 13-14).
- Comte's Traité de Politique Positive as a modern plan for a despotism of society over the individual (p. 18).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The principle is called 'one very simple principle' (p. 15) and then qualified within two pages: positive duties to act (p. 16), harm by inaction (p. 16), and expediency reasons against enforcing even where others are affected (p. 17). ch04 and ch05 carry the qualifications further.
- 'Men and women' appears in the list of class moralities (p. 13). This is the seed of The Subjection of Women (ch06).
- The renunciation of abstract right (p. 16) sits beside the language of sovereignty and of independence that is 'of right, absolute' (p. 15). Utilitarianism V (ch14) is where Mill explains how a utilitarian can speak of rights.
- The exclusion of 'barbarians' (p. 16) uses the same test, capacity for improvement by discussion, that ch02 uses to justify free discussion among the civilized.
- Mill says expression falls under a different principle from thought because it concerns others, yet is practically inseparable from it (p. 17). ch02 does not rely on the self-regarding sphere at all.

## Open questions

- What counts as harm? The chapter slides between harm, evil, hurt, injury and affecting the interests of others (pp. 15-17).
- If opinion is a form of coercion (p. 15) but remonstrance and persuasion are permitted (p. 15), where is the line between them? ch04 takes this up.
- Who judges whether a society has reached the stage where liberty applies (p. 16)?
