# ap03: Notes and Commentary: Utilitarianism; back matter (pp. 268–304)

Scope note: the author is the editor, Dale E. Miller, not Mill. For each of the five chapters of Utilitarianism there is an interpretive essay followed by numbered notes keyed to the superscripts in Mill's text. This is the longest and most argumentative of the three commentaries: several notes are short essays that reconstruct Mill's reasoning, report the standard objections and give the editor's own verdict. This note records those interpretive claims, disputes and criticisms, and passes over plain identifications. The editor's references to 'pages 239–240' and 'pages 244–45' are to the printed edition, not to this workspace's paging. The unit ends with the Modern Library editorial board list (pp. 303-304), which has no bearing on the argument.

## Question this unit answers

What exactly is Mill claiming in Utilitarianism, what kind of utilitarian is he, and how well do his arguments stand up to the objections made since?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| utilitarianism (as a family) | p. 268 | Not one moral theory but a family of them, sharing the claim that right and wrong are connected in some way with the impartial promotion of something like happiness. |
| impartial promotion | p. 269 | No one person's happiness matters more than another's. Often, not always, read as maximising total happiness over the long term. |
| net happiness | p. 269 | Total happiness minus total unhappiness; the editor's more exact name for what is maximised. |
| a priori / a posteriori | p. 269 | Knowable by reason or some faculty other than the senses, and so self-evident on reflection; knowable only by experience. Mill is called the ultimate empiricist, denying a priori knowledge even of arithmetic. |
| moral intuitionists | p. 270 | Those who hold moral knowledge is a priori. The ones Mill knew held that general rules, not verdicts on particular cases, are known this way (p. 271). |
| act utilitarianism | pp. 273-274 | An act is right only if nothing else the agent could do would yield more utility; every other act is wrong. Name from Brandt (1959). |
| rule utilitarianism | p. 274 | An act is right or wrong as the best set of moral rules permits or forbids acts of its kind; the best set is the one whose general observance yields most happiness. |
| collapse | p. 274 | Lyons's 1965 result: the simple form of rule utilitarianism yields a rule for every situation and so becomes act utilitarianism. Versions requiring learnable rules do not collapse (pp. 274-275). |
| utility | p. 275 | In Hume and Bentham the property of producing pleasure; now usually the pleasure, happiness, welfare or preference satisfaction produced. Mill's use is said to be nearer the modern one. |
| hedonism | p. 276 | Only pleasure is intrinsically good and only pain intrinsically bad. No implication of sensual pleasure in the philosophical use. |
| decided-preference test | p. 276 | The editor's name for Mill's test of quality: consensus among those who have experienced both pleasures, quantities being equal. |
| contentment | p. 277 | How much happiness a being has relative to how much it is able to have. |
| act/agent distinction | p. 281 | The modern name for separating moral evaluation of actions from moral evaluation of persons. |
| rules of thumb | p. 282 | How a sophisticated act utilitarian understands secondary principles: guidelines that usually, not always, pick the happiness-maximising act. |
| sanction | p. 284 | Glossed as simply Mill's word for a source of pleasure or pain. |
| sympathy | p. 284 | What would now be called empathy: the capacity to share other people's sentiments. |
| associationism | p. 285 | The psychological theory that thoughts and feelings summon others with which they have become associated. Used to explain conscience (p. 285) and acquired desires (pp. 292-293). |
| howler | p. 288 | A piece of reasoning so bad it makes one howl with laughter; the charge laid against each step of the proof. |
| fallacy of composition | p. 290 | Inferring that a collection has a property because each member has it. |
| habituation | p. 294 | The forming of habits of thought, feeling and choice, so that a choice persists when desires change. |
| morally right (two senses) | p. 298 | Morally required, or morally permissible. Mill's analysis in Chapter V is of the first. |
| positive law | p. 297 | Human-created law. |
| metaphysical (Mill's use) | p. 293 | Often a synonym for psychological. |

## Leading propositions

Chapter I commentary:

- Utilitarianism is better called a family of distinct moral theories than one theory (p. 268).
- Chapter I is about how moral knowledge is acquired, a topic more abstract than moral theory (p. 269).
- Mill denies a priori knowledge even in mathematics; no intellect could work out arithmetic without experience (p. 269).
- Mill's targets are the intuitionists, criticised by name in his essays on Sedgwick (1835) and Whewell (1852) (p. 270).
- Although Mill places himself in the inductive school, he has to invoke intuition at crucial stages of his own argument (p. 270).
- Mill holds that we must settle which moral theory is best before we can know which particular acts are right; choosing a theory by its fit with prior verdicts puts the cart before the horse (p. 270).
- One of Mill's major criticisms of the intuitionists is that their rules conflict and they give no way to decide between them; utilitarianism does (p. 271).
- Mill remained a utilitarian but modified Bentham's version, whose faults he traced to an oversimple view of human nature (p. 271).
- Bentham's phrase about the greatest number is misleading; the three people in a car, two hungry and one with appendicitis, show that total happiness and the number pleased can come apart (p. 272).
- Kant's critics, clearly including Mill, think his reason for rejecting some maxims comes down to the misery their universal adoption would cause; Kant's defenders deny this (p. 273).

Chapter II commentary:

- The chapter is brilliant in places, especially on qualities of pleasure, and imprecise at the point that matters most: what makes acts right or wrong (p. 273).
- It is easy to see that Mill is a utilitarian and hard to see what sort. Some passages read as act utilitarian, others as rule utilitarian, and he may be neither (pp. 273, 275).
- The sentence defining the greatest happiness principle is not a clear endorsement of act utilitarianism, because of the word 'tend' and because 'right in proportion' implies degrees of rightness, which act utilitarians deny (p. 275).
- Mill's definition of happiness as pleasure and absence of pain may be inconsistent with what he says of happiness in Chapter IV (pp. 275-276).
- In holding that a smaller quantity of a higher pleasure can be worth more than a larger quantity of a lower, Mill sets himself against Bentham, for whom value equals quantity (p. 276).
- Two misunderstandings of the test: there need not be just two grades of pleasure; and Mill does not say any amount of a higher pleasure outweighs any amount of a lower. A second, stricter test marks the cases where quantity is of small account (pp. 276-277).
- The test has problems. Those who know both philosophy and sex do not unconditionally prefer the first, so the test fails even where Mill thinks the gap in quality is greatest (p. 278).
- Mill is on firmer ground when he shifts from comparing experiences to comparing kinds of life (p. 278).
- Mill is not saying sports fans would be happier at the opera: the quality of a pleasure depends on how it is enjoyed, a person may be unable to enjoy the higher activity, and quantity still counts (p. 279).
- Mill's definition of the standard as rules and precepts sounds rule utilitarian, but he speaks of securing a happy life to all sentient creation, which is not the same as the greatest total (p. 279).
- The passage on abstaining from acts of a class that would be generally injurious appears to contradict act utilitarianism outright. Interpreters disagree whether it can be reconciled (pp. 280-281).
- Rule-utilitarian readers take 'tend' to apply to classes of acts, since a single act either has an outcome or does not; act-utilitarian readers say this reads too much into the word (p. 281).
- Mill separates the evaluation of acts from that of agents: a person of good character can act wrongly because of a good disposition (p. 281).
- Mill was not a Christian and it is debatable whether he had any religious belief (p. 281).
- Effects of acts on the agent's own character are among their most important consequences; Mill faulted Bentham for ignoring them (p. 282).
- The closing paragraphs on secondary principles do not settle the question. Sophisticated act utilitarians also use secondary rules, as rules of thumb, and appeal to the principle only when rules conflict (pp. 282-283).

Chapter III commentary:

- The chapter is as much psychology as philosophy. Its aim is to show that people can be given motives to obey utilitarian morality (p. 283).
- In this chapter at least, Mill seems to assume that we desire only pleasure and the avoidance of pain (p. 283).
- Conscience works in two ways: guilt after the act, and a barrier of feeling when the act is merely contemplated (p. 284).
- Conscience is called the ultimate sanction because it is effective and because what a person believes wrong and what his conscience disapproves usually coincide (pp. 284-285).
- Mill thinks conscience is implanted by association of certain acts with punishment in childhood; it is still internal because, once formed, it works without anyone else (p. 285).
- So those who bring us up can attach conscience to utilitarian morality, or to anything, including cruelty (pp. 285-286).
- Mill fears analysis dissolves an artificial conscience, as he believed it nearly dissolved his own feelings at about twenty. A utilitarian conscience would resist, because as social equality advances it will seem only natural to care about everyone's happiness (p. 286).

Chapter IV commentary:

- Mill never states explicitly what the principle of utility says; the nearest is the sentence in Chapter II, and even its status is unclear (p. 287).
- The chapter says essentially nothing about the morality of actions. Its conclusion is about what is desirable as an end, and Mill presupposes that morality must be connected with promoting some end (p. 287).
- Mill gives no reason for that presupposition, and the editor finds it unconvincing; if pressed Mill might have to call it an intuition (p. 288).
- Mill treats desire as playing a part for ends comparable to that of the senses for facts (p. 288).
- The argument has three steps: happiness is desirable as an end; the general happiness is desirable as an end; only happiness is. Each has been called a howler, and the argument also has defenders who disagree about how to read it (p. 288).
- Step one. Moore's objection is that desirable means what ought to be desired, not what can be, so Mill infers a normative conclusion from a descriptive premise (pp. 288-289).
- Four points in Mill's defence: the conclusion is very plausible anyway; Mill says desire is evidence, where sight is proof; if he is wrong the whole human race is mistaken; and if step three succeeds, ought implies can leaves happiness as the only candidate (p. 289).
- Step two. Mill is often charged with the fallacy of composition. The editor thinks him almost certainly not guilty, citing an 1868 letter in which Mill says he meant only that the sum of goods is a good (p. 290).
- Two gaps remain: why anyone has reason to promote a good that is not his own, which may need an intuition of the kind Sidgwick admitted; and why promoting the general happiness should mean maximising the total (pp. 290-291).
- Step three. Mill appears to make virtue, money and the rest parts of happiness, which seems to contradict the Chapter II definition. On one plausible reading he means that people take pleasure in possessing them, so that strictly they are desired as means (pp. 291-292).
- The editor's own verdict is that it is not very plausible that pleasure is the only thing desired as an end. Nozick's machine shows people want the things, not only the feeling (p. 292).
- No reading of this stretch is plausible unless it explains how Mill could hold both that virtue is desired as an end and that pleasure and desire are that closely tied (p. 293).
- The discussion of habit matters more than the objection it answers. Concern for the formation of character runs through On Liberty and The Subjection of Women as well (pp. 293-294).

Chapter V commentary:

- The chapter was first meant to stand alone and is slightly longer than it needs to be (p. 294).
- The objection now usually pressed is that utilitarianism sometimes requires unjust acts. Mill does not discuss it (pp. 294-296).
- The surgeon case: one disagreeable patient could be cut up to save five public benefactors. Act utilitarians reply that the long-run effects would be bad, that rights are not absolute, and that medical staff should follow a rule of thumb. Whether this is enough is debatable (pp. 295-296).
- Rule utilitarians have an easier time, since by definition they hold that rules should not be broken for small gains (pp. 295-296).
- Mill is overoptimistic about agreement between justice and utility. The objection he does answer is the intuitionist's: the feeling of justice is too strong to rest on expediency (p. 296).
- Mill took seriously a socialism of worker-owned firms and small communities, and strongly opposed taking property from its owners (p. 297).
- Mill ties wrongness to punishment conceptually. The etymology is not the real support, and its accuracy has been questioned; the support is the reader's own reflection on usage (pp. 297-298).
- If wrong acts are just those that ought to be punished, Mill can hardly be an act utilitarian, since punishing is itself an act and would not be optimal in exactly the cases where the act punished was not (p. 298).
- Lyons (1994) reads Mill as holding that an act is wrong only if it would maximise happiness for everyone to internalise a rule against it. This is rule utilitarianism in all but name and does not collapse (p. 299).
- The line between justice and the rest of morality is fuzzy. A single act can be an injustice; a failure of charity can be judged only over time (pp. 299-300).
- The sentiment of justice is built from the desire to retaliate and from sympathy, and Mill wants it trained to respond only to real injustice and to injustice against anyone (p. 300).
- For Mill the main justification of punishment is deterrence (p. 301).
- Disagreement over specific principles of justice is Mill's argument against their self-evidence (p. 301).
- Mill carried the injustice of disappointing encouraged expectations so far as to hold that slave owners were owed compensation on emancipation (pp. 301-302).
- Mill's claim that violating rights usually costs more happiness than it gains is taken too much for granted, critics say; free expression is the example (p. 302).
- The passage treating equal weight for equal happiness and equal treatment of persons as one principle is perplexing. They are distinct: the first is a presupposition of utilitarian reasoning, the second a product (p. 303).
- The paragraph on saving a life at the cost of a right is also perplexing: why is the duty to save the life not itself a duty of justice? (p. 303)

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 275) act utilitarians hold that only the best available act is right + (p. 275) Mill says acts are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness + (p. 280) Mill says some acts are to be abstained from because their class is generally injurious.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 280-281) Mill's text does not fit act utilitarianism cleanly. The editor reports this as a live dispute and does not settle it.
- **Premises**: (p. 282) Mill requires secondary principles + (pp. 282-283) sophisticated act utilitarians require them too, as rules of thumb, and for Mill's own reason.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 282) the secondary-principles passage does not show Mill to be a rule utilitarian. This corrects a quick inference many readers make.
- **Premises**: (p. 278) the test asks whether those who know both would always prefer the higher pleasure + (p. 278) philosophers would not give up sex for more philosophy.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 278) the decided-preference test is not clearly satisfied even in Mill's best case. Qualified at once: comparing whole lives, the human against the pig and Socrates against the fool, Mill's verdict stands (pp. 278-279).
- **Premises**: (pp. 288-289) Moore: desirable means ought to be desired + (p. 289) inferences from is to ought are generally illegitimate.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 289) step one is fallacious. Editor's rejoinder: (p. 289) Mill claims evidence, not proof + (p. 289) if only happiness can be desired as an end, then by ought implies can only happiness can be desirable as an end. The rejoinder is conditional on step three.
- **Premises**: (p. 290) each person's happiness is a good to him + (p. 290) the 1868 letter: the sum of goods must be a good.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 290) Mill argues by addition to an impersonal good, not by composition. Gaps: (p. 291) why an impersonal good gives me a reason, and (p. 291) why the reason is to maximise the total.
- **Premises**: (p. 292) we expect pleasure on getting what we desire for its own sake + (p. 292) most people would refuse a machine that only made them believe their desires satisfied.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 292) pleasure is not the only thing desired as an end, so step three fails as psychology. This is the editor's own criticism, stated as a verdict.
- **Premises**: (p. 298) for Mill an act is wrong if and only if it ought to be punished + (p. 298) an act utilitarian must judge each act of punishing by its own consequences + (p. 298) optimal punishment and non-optimal conduct will not coincide.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 298) the Chapter V analysis of wrongness is very hard to combine with act utilitarianism, and (p. 299) lends itself to a rule reading through internalised rules.
- **Premises**: (pp. 295-296) cases can be devised where violating a right maximises happiness + (p. 302) Mill assumes the harm of violating rights usually outweighs any gain.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 302) an act utilitarian has difficulty showing that rights should very seldom be violated; the rule utilitarian has a somewhat easier time.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Act against rule. My ch11 note lists the abstinence sentence (ch11, pp. 206-207) and the Nautical Almanack passage (ch11, p. 211) as support for a rule reading. The editor agrees on the first (p. 280) and denies that the second shows anything, since act utilitarians use the same rules as rules of thumb (p. 282). He adds two pieces of evidence the ch11 note does not use: 'right in proportion' implies degrees of rightness (p. 275), and the standard is defined as rules and precepts (p. 279). He does not weigh Mill's direct handling of exceptions to veracity (ch11, p. 210).
- The editor's strongest textual argument for a rule reading comes from Chapter V's link between wrongness and punishment (pp. 298-299). The ch14 note records that link (ch14, pp. 232-233) and also the life-saving exception (ch14, p. 245) as the main obstacle to a strict rule reading. The editor calls that paragraph perplexing (p. 303) and does not bring it to bear on the act and rule question.
- Higher pleasures. The ch11 note asks whether a pleasure that judges would not trade for any quantity of another is still being measured as pleasure (ch11, p. 198). The editor reads the no-trade condition as a second, stricter test met only in some cases (p. 277), which removes part of the difficulty. His own objection is different: the judges' preferences are not in fact as Mill says (p. 278). He does not raise the question, noted in ch11, of whether the sense of dignity brings in a non-hedonic value.
- The proof, step one. The unstated premise identified in the ch13 note (ch13, p. 221) is Moore's objection (pp. 288-289). The editor's point that Mill says evidence, not proof (p. 289), agrees with the ch13 reading that this is a claim about evidence and with ch10's caution about proof (ch10, p. 195).
- The proof, step two. The ch13 note asks whether Mill claims a reason for each person or only an impersonal good (ch13, p. 221). The 1868 letter answers: an impersonal sum (p. 290). The gap the editor then names, why that gives anyone a reason (p. 291), is the one the ch13 note sends to ch12 and ch14 for support.
- The proof, step three. The ch13 note says the step is either empirical or true by definition (ch13, pp. 224-225). The editor offers a third reading, on which virtue is strictly a means to the pleasure of having it (p. 292). That reading conflicts with Mill's statement that such things are besides being means a part of the end (ch13, p. 222), which is why the editor says Mill's talk must then be taken as loose (p. 293).
- Intuition. The editor says Mill needs intuitions at two points: that morality must refer to an end (p. 288) and that an impersonal good gives reasons (p. 291). This turns ch10's complaint against the intuitive school (ch10, pp. 193-194) back on Mill. It is a criticism of incompleteness: Mill gives no argument where one is needed.
- Sanctions. The editor defines a sanction as a source of pleasure or pain (p. 284) and says Mill assumes only pleasure and pain are desired (p. 283). The ch12 note takes sanction as the motive to obey and the source of binding force (ch12, p. 213). The editor's version is narrower and more Benthamite than the text requires, though it fits ch13 (p. 225).
- The editor's account of why a utilitarian conscience survives analysis (p. 286) matches ch12 (pp. 217-219). He reports Mill's objection to Comte as the unhappiness of a society of constant duty (p. 286); Mill's stated worry in the text is interference with human freedom and individuality (ch12, p. 220).
- Rights and free expression. The editor uses the liberty of discussion from On Liberty II as his test case for whether utility can secure a right (p. 302). This is the question the ch14 note raises about reading the harm principle as a rule of justice (ch14, p. 242) and about calling it absolute (ch01, p. 15).
- Equality. The editor's point that equal weight in the sum and equal treatment are distinct principles (p. 303) is the unstated premise flagged in the ch14 note (ch14, pp. 244-245).
- Life-saving. The editor's question why the duty to save a life is not itself a duty of justice (p. 303) is the same alternative posed at the end of the ch14 note, and bears on On Liberty's enforceable duties to assist (ch01, p. 16).
- Character. The remark that Mill's arguments about institutions turn on their effects on character (p. 294) ties ch13's account of habit to ch03 and to ch07 and ch09, and repeats what the editor says in ap01 (p. 251) and ap02 (p. 258).

## Open questions

- The editor leaves the act and rule question open in Chapter II and leans toward a rule reading in Chapter V without saying so outright. Is the question well posed for Mill at all, given that the editor allows he may be neither (p. 275)?
- The claim that Mill holds theory must be settled before particular verdicts (p. 270) sits oddly with Mill's view that received rules embody long experience (ch11, pp. 210-211). The editor does not connect the two.
- A sentence in note 17 to Chapter V says utilitarians believe that the basis of happiness is the impartial promotion of happiness (p. 303). This looks like a slip for the basis of morality. Likewise a parenthesis opened in note 2 to Chapter IV is never closed (p. 291). Neither affects the argument.
- The 1868 letter is quoted without naming its recipient (p. 290), and the earlier edition in which Mill is said to have disowned arguing from etymology is not identified (p. 298). I cannot verify either from this book.
- The editor's remarks on the Mills' private life (p. 278) are offered as explaining Mill's ranking of pleasures. They are speculation and I do not rely on them.
