# ap01: Notes and Commentary: On Liberty (pp. 247–257)

Scope note: the author of this unit is the editor, Dale E. Miller, not Mill. It contains two notes on the dedication and epigraph, then for each chapter of On Liberty a short interpretive summary followed by numbered notes keyed to the superscripts in Mill's text. Most numbered notes identify people, terms and Latin phrases. This note records the interpretive claims and the notes that add more than an identification.

## Question this unit answers

What does a reader of On Liberty need to know about its references, and where is Mill's argument unclear or open to challenge?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| liberty principle / harm principle | p. 248 | The editor's names for the principle Mill states in Chapter I. Mill uses neither name. |
| compatibilist / soft determinist | p. 248 | The position Mill defends elsewhere on free will: actions are determined by antecedent causes and we are still morally responsible. |
| moral intuitionists | p. 248 | The target of Mill's remarks about those who put feelings in place of reasons. |
| prima facie | p. 248 | Having enough support to be convincing unless further considerations tell against it. |
| de jure | p. 248 | Glossed for Mill's context: interference would not violate the liberty principle, though it may still be a mistake on a balance of costs. |
| necessary but not sufficient condition | p. 256 | The editor's summary of the logical form of the liberty principle. |
| self-culture | p. 252 | The editor's term for choosing how to live and so forming one's own character. |
| energy / vigor | p. 253 | The one trait the editor says Mill wants in every character. |

## Leading propositions

Dedication and Chapter I:

- Mill said in his Autobiography that On Liberty was more directly and literally a joint production with Harriet Taylor Mill than anything else under his name (p. 247).
- Humboldt's book was written in 1791 and published in 1854 (p. 247).
- The 'democratic republic' is the United States, and Mill's view of it is heavily influenced by Tocqueville's Democracy in America (p. 247).
- The editor adds a gloss not in Mill: democracy failed to protect people in the United States partly because women and nonwhites could not take part (p. 247).
- Mill's description of the principle as one simple principle is 'extremely misleading': it is hard to interpret and may not be one principle, since Chapter V restates it as two maxims (p. 248).
- Mill sets free will aside here; elsewhere he is a compatibilist (p. 248).

Chapter II:

- The case has three components: received views wholly false, wholly true, and true in part (p. 249).
- The third case looms large for Mill, who took his own philosophical gift to be piecing together the partial truths of very different thinkers (p. 249).
- Mill is 'simply in error' in describing Aristotle as a utilitarian (p. 250).
- Under Marcus Aurelius Christians were punishable when identified but not systematically sought, though the number punished appears to have increased (p. 250).
- Mill's point that persecution of ideas produces cowardly people shows his constant concern with the effect of institutions on character (p. 251).

Chapter III:

- Mill gives three reasons for freedom from the despotism of custom: experiments by people of genius may find ways of life others would want; people differ in what makes them happy; and the capacity for choice weakens if unused (p. 252).
- Character is the centre of the chapter; Mill exaggerates slightly in implying that only self-culture gives a person a character at all (p. 252).
- Mill's account of Calvinism may be contestable. James Fitzjames Stephen's Liberty, Equality, Fraternity (1873) is a conservative reply to all three works in this volume, and he says Mill uses the word Calvinistic almost at random (p. 253).
- Mill's examples of Knox, Alcibiades and Pericles may not be well chosen; Stephen objects to the portrait of Knox, and Alcibiades' guardian was Pericles (pp. 253-254).
- Mill's concern with the effect of power on those who hold it echoes Chapter II of The Subjection of Women (p. 254).
- In Considerations on Representative Government Mill favours proportional representation and additional votes for the better educated, so that an elite can advise and hold swing votes without dominating (p. 254).
- The Tocqueville work Mill cites is probably The Old Regime and the French Revolution (p. 255).

Chapter IV:

- How well Mill succeeds in fixing the meaning of the principle is a matter for debate (p. 255).
- Mill does not say at any length how harm is to be defined; if every negative effect counts, the principle limits nothing (p. 255).
- It is unclear whether interference with A is permitted only when A would cause the harm. If so, how can we be required to come to the defence of others? (p. 255)
- The line between permitted reactions to self-regarding faults and forbidden ones is very thin (p. 255).
- Three misreadings to avoid: the doctrine is not selfish indifference; the 'private sphere' does not shield domestic violence; and the principle states a necessary but not a sufficient condition for interference (p. 256).

Chapter V:

- A better title might be 'Related Matters' (p. 257).
- Questions have been raised about whether refusing to enforce a slavery contract is consistent with Mill's opposition to measures against self-harm (p. 257).
- It is far from clear that preventing offences against decency is consistent with the liberty principle (p. 257).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 255) almost anything a person does can have some negative effect on someone + (p. 255) Mill does not restrict the definition of harm.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 255) as stated, the principle risks placing no limit on society. This is a criticism of incompleteness, not of error, and it matches the open question raised in ch01 and ch04 from Mill's own text.
- **Premises**: (p. 255) the principle seems to permit interference with a person only to prevent harm that person would cause + (p. 255) Mill also says people may be compelled to defend others.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 255) the scope of the principle is unclear on harm prevention as against harm causing. Mill's own answer, that inaction can cause evil (ch01, p. 16), is not mentioned by the editor.
- **Premises**: (p. 256) people cannot live together without constantly harming one another in small ways + (p. 256) preventing every harm would often cost more than it gains.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 256) the principle is a necessary and not a sufficient condition. This agrees with Mill's own statement at the start of Chapter V (ch05, p. 85).

## Evidence offered

- Mill's Autobiography on Harriet Taylor Mill's share in his work (p. 247).
- Boswell's report of Johnson's remark that religious truth can be established only by martyrdom (p. 250).
- A long quotation from Stephen on Calvin (p. 253).
- Historical identifications for the reformers Mill lists, the 1858 Tyrannicide pamphlet prosecution, the Maine Law and the United Kingdom Alliance, and the Mormons (pp. 249-251, 256-257).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The editor's three reasons for individuality (p. 252) are all instrumental to happiness. He leaves out Mill's claim that individual development is good in itself (ch03, pp. 56, 60), so his summary makes the chapter more straightforwardly utilitarian than the text is.
- The editor's misquotation of the principle as 'one simple principle' (p. 248) drops Mill's 'very' (ch01, p. 15). Minor, but the criticism of Mill's wording is itself loosely worded.
- The remark that the private sphere does not protect violence in the family (p. 256) is confirmed by Mill's own Chapter V (ch05, pp. 94-95).
- The reference to Stephen (p. 253) identifies the main contemporary critic of all three works; the editor uses him again in the commentary on The Subjection of Women (ap02).
- The note on plural voting (p. 254) qualifies the democratic reading of ch03's passage on exceptional individuals, and bears on the suffrage argument of ch08.

## Open questions

- Possible errors in the editor's notes, which I cannot settle from this book: the reign dates given for 'Henry VII (1509–1547)' at p. 249 look like those of Henry VIII; Cicero's dates are printed as '106–13 B.C.' (p. 251); and foot-binding is dated to the 'tenth century B.C. (in the Sung dynasty)' (p. 254). These may be editorial or conversion slips and should be checked against another source before being relied on.
- The editor reports that 'questions have been raised' about the slavery contract and decency (p. 257) without naming who raised them or giving the arguments.
