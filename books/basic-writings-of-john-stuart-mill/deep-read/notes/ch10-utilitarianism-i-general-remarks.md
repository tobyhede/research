# ch10: Utilitarianism I: General Remarks (pp. 192–195)

Scope note: a short opening chapter, pp. 192-195, with no footnotes by Mill.

## Question this chapter answers

Why is there still no agreed criterion of right and wrong, and what kind of proof can a first principle of morals receive?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| summum bonum | p. 192 | The highest good. Mill treats the question about it as the same as the question of the foundation of morality. |
| first principles | p. 192 | In a science, the last results of analysis, related to it as roots to a tree and not as foundations to a building. In a practical art they should come first, because rules of action take their character from their end (pp. 192-193). |
| intuitive school | p. 193 | Holds that moral principles are evident a priori. |
| inductive school | p. 193 | Holds that right and wrong are questions of observation and experience. Mill's side. |
| moral faculty / moral sense | p. 193 | On the better intuitionist view, a branch of reason supplying general principles and not perceptions of right in particular cases. |
| principle of utility / greatest happiness principle | p. 194 | Bentham's principle, said to have tacitly shaped even the moral doctrines of those who reject it. |
| proof (larger meaning) | p. 195 | Considerations capable of determining the intellect to give or withhold assent. Ultimate ends cannot be proved in the ordinary sense. |

## Leading propositions

- After more than two thousand years there is no agreement on the criterion of right and wrong (p. 192).
- In morals, unlike the sciences, the general principle should precede the particular rules, because all action is for the sake of an end (pp. 192-193).
- Appeal to a moral sense does not escape the difficulty: its own defenders say it gives only general principles (p. 193).
- Both schools agree that morality is the application of general laws to cases, and largely agree on the laws; they differ on the evidence and source of authority (p. 193).
- The intuitive school rarely lists its a priori principles or reduces them to one, and gives no rule for deciding between them when they conflict (p. 193).
- Whatever consistency moral beliefs have is mainly due to the tacit influence of the principle of utility (p. 194).
- Kant's universal law, when he tries to deduce duties from it, shows only that the consequences of universal adoption of immoral rules would be such as no one would choose (p. 194).
- Questions of ultimate ends are not amenable to direct proof; what can be proved good is proved so as a means to something admitted good without proof (pp. 194-195).
- Acceptance of the utilitarian formula need not rest on blind impulse: considerations can be presented that determine the intellect, and this is equivalent to proof (p. 195).
- The chief obstacle to accepting the formula is misunderstanding of it, so explanation comes before proof (p. 195).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 193) a system of a priori morality needs either one fundamental principle or an order of precedence among several + (p. 193) the intuitive school supplies neither.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 194) ethics without an acknowledged first principle is a consecration of people's actual sentiments. This is the same complaint as On Liberty's about rules resting on likings (ch01, p. 12).
- **Premises**: (p. 194) men's sentiments are greatly influenced by what they take to be the effects of things on their happiness.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 194) utility has had a large share in forming all moral doctrines. Unstated premise: a principle's tacit influence on moral belief counts in favour of its being the correct standard.
- **Premises**: (pp. 194-195) proof of goodness runs from means to ends + (p. 195) an ultimate end has nothing beyond it.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 195) the formula can be supported only by considerations addressed to the rational faculty, which ch13 supplies.

## Evidence offered

- Plato's Protagoras, where Mill says the young Socrates asserts utilitarianism against popular morality (p. 192).
- Algebra, whose certainty does not come from its taught first principles (p. 192).
- Kant's Metaphysics of Ethics and its formula of universal law (p. 194).
- Medicine and music as arts proved good by health and pleasure, which cannot themselves be proved good (pp. 194-195).

## Quotes

- "A test of right and wrong must be the means, one would think, of ascertaining what is right or wrong, and not a consequence of having already ascertained it." (p. 193)
- "has made ethics not so much a guide as a consecration of men’s actual sentiments" (p. 194)
- "Questions of ultimate ends are not amenable to direct proof." (p. 194)
- "Considerations may be presented capable of determining the intellect either to give or withhold its assent to the doctrine; and this is equivalent to proof." (p. 195)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The promise of 'such proof as it is susceptible of' (p. 194) is redeemed in ch13; the caution here should govern how the 'proof' there is read.
- The criticism of Kant is a single paragraph and asserts that his deductions fail 'almost grotesquely' (p. 194). No deduction of Kant's is examined. This is a claim I cannot verify from the text; ch14 returns to Kant (p. 240 region) more charitably.
- The attack on moral sense as a cover for sentiment matches On Liberty ch01 (p. 12) and ch04 (p. 77).
