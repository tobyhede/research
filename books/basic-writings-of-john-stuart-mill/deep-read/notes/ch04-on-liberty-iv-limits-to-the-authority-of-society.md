# ch04: On Liberty IV: Of the Limits to the Authority of Society over the Individual (pp. 69–85)

Scope note: chapter text pp. 69-85; one footnote by Mill on the Bombay Parsees (p. 85).

## Question this chapter answers

Where does the individual's sovereignty over himself end and society's authority begin, and what may others do about conduct that harms only the agent?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| interests that ought to be considered rights | p. 69 | Certain interests of others, fixed by express law or tacit understanding, which each is bound not to injure. Society may enforce this 'at all costs'. |
| jurisdiction | pp. 69-70 | Society's standing to consider interference. It arises as soon as conduct affects others' interests prejudicially; whether to interfere is then a further question of general welfare. |
| self-regarding conduct | p. 70 | Conduct affecting the interests of no one but the agent, or others only if they like, all being adults of ordinary understanding. |
| self-regarding virtues / faults | pp. 70, 72 | Prudence, self-respect, self-development and their opposites. Not properly immoralities and never wickedness, however far carried. |
| natural penalties | p. 72 | The loss of others' good opinion and company that follows spontaneously from self-regarding faults, as distinct from punishment purposely inflicted. |
| moral vices | p. 72 | Dispositions that lead to injuring others: cruelty, malice, envy, insincerity, love of domineering, greed, egotism. Fit objects of reprobation. |
| duty to oneself | pp. 72-73 | Means prudence, self-respect or self-development. Not socially obligatory unless circumstances make it a duty to others. |
| distinct and assignable obligation | p. 74 | A definite duty to particular persons or to the public. Self-harm that leads to its breach leaves the self-regarding class. |
| contingent or constructive injury | p. 75 | Remote harm to society from conduct that breaks no specific duty and hurts no assignable individual. Society can afford to bear it for the sake of freedom. |
| moral police | p. 78 | The public's enforcement of its own preferences as moral law. |
| social rights (the Alliance's) | pp. 81-82 | A claimed right that every other person act as he ought. Mill calls the principle monstrous. |

Shift to note: the test in ch01 was 'harm to others' (p. 15). Here it is first injury to interests that ought to count as rights (p. 69), then conduct that 'affects prejudicially the interests of others' (pp. 69-70), then 'definite damage, or a definite risk of damage' (p. 75). The narrowest of these is the one doing the work in the examples.

## Leading propositions

- Society is not founded on a contract, but everyone who receives its protection owes a return: not injuring the rights-interests of others and bearing a fair share of common burdens (p. 69).
- Conduct hurtful to others without violating their rights may be punished by opinion though not by law (p. 69).
- Harm to others' interests gives society jurisdiction; it does not settle that interference is right (pp. 69-70).
- Where conduct affects only the agent there should be perfect freedom, legal and social, to act and take the consequences (p. 70).
- The doctrine is not selfish indifference; people owe each other help and encouragement toward the better, by persuasion and not by whips (p. 70).
- The individual has the greatest interest in his own well-being and far the best knowledge of his own feelings and circumstances; society's interference rests on general presumptions that may be wrong or misapplied (p. 70).
- Others may judge a person a fool, warn him, avoid his company, caution others and prefer others in optional good offices, but may not purposely punish him (pp. 71-72).
- Self-regarding faults are not immoralities; duties to oneself are not socially obligatory (pp. 72-73).
- Objection admitted: no one is isolated, and self-harm reaches family, dependants and the community by loss and by example (pp. 73-74).
- Reply: when self-regarding conduct causes breach of a distinct and assignable obligation, the person is punishable for the breach and not for the vice (pp. 74-75).
- No one should be punished simply for being drunk; a soldier or policeman drunk on duty should be (p. 75).
- Merely contingent or constructive injury is an inconvenience society can bear for the greater good of freedom (p. 75).
- Society has had the whole of childhood in which to form its members and cannot claim the further power to command adults in their personal concerns (pp. 75-76).
- The strongest argument: when the public interferes with personal conduct it probably interferes wrongly, because it is judging what is good for others or merely consulting its own distaste (p. 77).
- There is no parity between a person's feeling for his own opinion or taste and another's offence at it (p. 77).
- The examples: a Muslim majority forbidding pork, Spain forbidding Protestant worship, Puritan suppression of amusements, majority veto on spending, artisan enforcement of equal wages, prohibition of alcohol, Sabbatarian law, hostility to Mormon polygamy (pp. 78-84).
- Sunday rest may be legally guaranteed where each person's observance depends on others observing it; that reason does not reach amusements (p. 82).
- Polygamy is an infraction of liberty, yet no community has a right to force another to be civilized so long as the sufferers do not ask for help and may freely leave (p. 84).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 70) each person is the one most interested in his own good + (p. 70) each has knowledge of his own case immeasurably beyond anyone else's + (p. 70) interference must proceed on general presumptions, likely to be wrong or misapplied.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 71) the errors a person commits against advice are outweighed by the evil of letting others constrain him to what they deem his good. This is an epistemic argument and it is utilitarian in form.
- **Premises**: (p. 77) on duties to others the majority judge their own interests and are oftener right than wrong + (p. 77) on self-regarding conduct they judge what is good for other people, or only their own preference.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 77) majority opinion imposed as law on self-regarding conduct is as likely wrong as right.
- **Premises**: (pp. 78-79) the feelings of Muslims about pork and of Spanish Catholics about Protestant worship are as sincere and strong as English feelings about personal immorality + (p. 79) no principle admits the one case and excludes the others.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 79) to allow interference in things that do not concern others' interests is to adopt the logic of persecutors. Unstated premise: offence or disgust felt at another's conduct is not an injury to one's interests. Mill asserts this by the purse analogy (p. 77) and does not argue it.
- **Premises**: (pp. 81-82) the Alliance's theory makes every failure of anyone to act as he ought a violation of my rights.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 82) the theory would justify any violation of liberty, so it cannot be the right account of harm to others.

## Evidence offered

- George Barnwell: hanged for the murder whatever his motive for wanting the money (p. 75).
- The licence of the Restoration as a reaction to Puritan moral intolerance (p. 76).
- The Maine Law and the United Kingdom Alliance, with quotations from its Secretary's correspondence with Lord Stanley (pp. 81-82).
- Sabbatarian campaigns against Sunday trains and museum opening (p. 83).
- The Mormons: founder killed by a mob, the community expelled, and English calls for an expedition against them (pp. 83-84).
- The Bombay Parsees, whose imposed abstinence from beef and pork became custom (p. 85, Mill's footnote).

## Quotes

- "As soon as any part of a person’s conduct affects prejudicially the interests of others, society has jurisdiction over it" (pp. 69-70)
- "In all such cases there should be perfect freedom, legal and social, to do the action and stand the consequences." (p. 70)
- "But disinterested benevolence can find other instruments to persuade people to their good, than whips and scourges" (p. 70)
- "but he, himself, is the final judge" (p. 71)
- "What are called duties to ourselves are not socially obligatory, unless circumstances render them at the same time duties to others." (p. 72)
- "No person ought to be punished simply for being drunk; but a soldier or a policeman should be punished for being drunk on duty." (p. 75)
- "the inconvenience is one which society can afford to bear, for the sake of the greater good of human freedom" (p. 75)
- "the odds are that it interferes wrongly, and in the wrong place" (p. 77)
- "And a person’s taste is as much his own peculiar concern as his opinion or his purse." (p. 77)
- "I am not aware that any community has a right to force another to be civilized." (p. 84)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The line between permitted 'natural penalties' and forbidden punishment (pp. 71-72) is fine. Mill allows avoidance, warning others and withholding good offices, and admits these may amount to 'very severe penalties' (p. 72). ch01 called the pressure of opinion a tyranny (p. 11) and ch03 complained that the eccentric are subject to 'depreciatory remark' (p. 64). The difference Mill relies on is intention: natural consequence against purposeful infliction (p. 72).
- Harm is narrowed to rights and assignable obligations (pp. 69, 74-75), which fits Utilitarianism V's account of justice as rights grounded in utility (ch14) better than the open 'harm' of ch01.
- The refusal to 'force another to be civilized' (p. 84) sits against ch01's licence for despotism over 'barbarians' (p. 16). The difference appears to be that the Mormons are a consenting community with freedom of exit, though Mill still calls their institution retrograde.
- On polygamy Mill says the women's choice is voluntary but explained by custom teaching women that marriage is 'the one thing needful' (p. 84). The Subjection of Women denies that such consent is free (ch06). Here Mill lets the consent stand for purposes of non-intervention by outsiders.
- The remark against moralists who teach that things are right because we feel them so (p. 77) is the anti-intuitionist position of Utilitarianism I (ch10).
- The Sunday-rest concession (p. 82) is a collective-action argument: a rule may bind all when each can keep it only if others do. ch05 uses similar reasoning.

## Open questions

- Which interests 'ought to be considered as rights' (p. 69)? The test is left to law or tacit understanding, which are themselves products of the majority opinion Mill distrusts.
- Is serious offence ever a harm? The pork and Sabbath examples say no; ch05 nonetheless permits prohibition of offences against decency.
- Does the epistemic argument (p. 70) hold for people whose self-knowledge is impaired, for example by addiction? Mill's qualifier is 'the ordinary amount of understanding' (p. 70).
