# ch11: Utilitarianism II: What Utilitarianism Is (pp. 195–213)

Scope note: chapter text pp. 195-212. Mill's two footnotes (on the word utilitarian; the reply to Davies on motive and intention) are printed at pp. 212-213.

## Question this chapter answers

What does the utilitarian standard actually say, and which of the common objections rest on misreading it?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Greatest Happiness Principle | p. 196 | Actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse. |
| happiness | p. 196 | Pleasure and the absence of pain. Later specified as a life of few and transitory pains and many and various pleasures, with the active predominating over the passive, and not expecting more from life than it can give (p. 201). |
| theory of life | p. 196 | The claim on which the moral theory is grounded: pleasure and freedom from pain are the only things desirable as ends. |
| quality of pleasure | p. 197 | A difference among pleasures other than amount. One pleasure is higher if all or almost all who have experience of both decidedly prefer it, apart from moral obligation, even with more discontent (pp. 197-198). |
| competent judges | pp. 197-199 | Those acquainted with and capable of both pleasures. Their verdict, or the majority's if they differ, is final (p. 200). |
| sense of dignity | p. 198 | Mill's preferred name for the unwillingness to sink to a lower grade of existence. Essential to the happiness of those in whom it is strong. |
| happiness and content | p. 198 | Distinguished: a being of low capacities is more easily content; that does not make him happier. |
| the standard | p. 200 | Not the agent's own greatest happiness but the greatest amount of happiness altogether, for all mankind and as far as possible the whole sentient creation (p. 201). |
| impartiality | p. 205 | Between his own happiness and others' the agent must be as impartial as a disinterested and benevolent spectator. |
| motive and intention | pp. 206, 213 | Motive is the feeling that makes the agent will the act; intention is what he wills to do. The morality of the act depends on intention; motive bears on the worth of the agent. |
| rights | p. 206 | Glossed in passing as 'the legitimate and authorized expectations' of others. |
| the Expedient | p. 209 | In its bad sense, what serves the agent's particular interest or a temporary purpose while violating a rule whose observance is far more useful. A branch of the hurtful. |
| secondary principles | p. 211 | The received rules of morality: mankind's accumulated beliefs about the effects of actions on happiness. Also 'intermediate generalizations', 'corollaries', 'landmarks and direction-posts'. |

Shift to note: 'happiness' begins as pleasure and absence of pain (p. 196), is qualified by quality as well as quantity (p. 197), and ends as a kind of life available to nearly everyone given decent education and institutions (pp. 201-203). 'Utility' is defended against the sense of mere usefulness (p. 195) and against Expediency (p. 209).

## Leading propositions

- Utility is not opposed to pleasure; from Epicurus to Bentham it has meant pleasure and exemption from pain (p. 196).
- Pleasure and freedom from pain are the only things desirable as ends (p. 196).
- The charge that this is a doctrine worthy of swine assumes humans are capable only of swine's pleasures (p. 197).
- It is compatible with the principle of utility to hold that some kinds of pleasure are more desirable than others, in quality and not only quantity (p. 197).
- The test of quality is the decided preference of those who have experience of both (pp. 197-198).
- Those acquainted with both prefer the manner of existence that employs their higher faculties; it is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied (pp. 198-199).
- People who fall back on lower pleasures have usually lost the capacity for the higher through lack of opportunity, not chosen against them (p. 199).
- The doctrine of quality is not indispensable to the standard, since the standard is general happiness and noble character certainly benefits others (p. 200).
- Happiness is attainable: wretched education and social arrangements are the only real hindrance to its being within reach of almost all (p. 201).
- The chief causes of an unsatisfying life are selfishness and want of mental cultivation (p. 202).
- Most of the great positive evils, including poverty and disease, are removable by human care and effort (p. 203).
- Self-sacrifice is admirable only as a means to the happiness of others; a sacrifice that does not increase the sum of happiness is wasted (pp. 204-205).
- The golden rule gives the complete spirit of the ethics of utility. Laws should bring each person's interest into harmony with the whole, and education should associate each person's happiness with the general good (p. 205).
- A standard of morals states the rule of action, not the motive; the motive bears on the worth of the agent, not the morality of the act (p. 206).
- Most good actions aim at the benefit of particular people; only those whose actions affect society at large need attend to public utility habitually (p. 206).
- In abstinences, the ground of obligation is that the act belongs to a class which would be generally injurious if generally practised (pp. 206-207).
- Utility is not godless: if God wills the happiness of creatures, utility is profoundly religious (pp. 208-209).
- Lying for present advantage is not expedient, because it weakens the trustworthiness of assertion; the rule admits recognised exceptions whose limits utility must mark (pp. 209-210).
- There is no need to calculate before each act: the whole past of the species has been learning the tendencies of actions, and those beliefs are the rules of morality (pp. 210-211).
- First principles need be appealed to only when secondary principles conflict; utility then serves as common umpire (p. 212).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 197) in estimating everything else quality is considered as well as quantity + (pp. 197-198) competent judges prefer the higher pleasures even at the cost of more discontent.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 198) some pleasures are superior in quality, in a degree that can outweigh quantity. Unstated premise: what competent judges prefer is thereby more pleasant, and not merely more worthy on some other ground. Mill names the ground 'a sense of dignity' (p. 198), which is the point at which critics say a non-hedonic value has entered.
- **Premises**: (p. 200) there is no tribunal even for quantity of pleasure other than the suffrage of the experienced.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 200) their judgment on quality deserves the same regard. This is a parity argument: the test is no worse for quality than for intensity.
- **Premises**: (p. 201) happiness properly understood is a mixed and moderate life, not continuous rapture + (pp. 202-203) its obstacles are selfishness, lack of cultivation, and removable evils.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 201) the objection that happiness is unattainable fails.
- **Premises**: (p. 210) mankind have long experience of the tendencies of actions + (p. 211) every practical art uses intermediate rules, as navigation uses the Nautical Almanack.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 211) accepting a first principle is consistent with, and requires, secondary principles; individual acts are not to be tested directly by the first principle in the ordinary case.
- **Premises**: (p. 212) every moral system meets conflicts of obligation + (p. 212) systems with several independent principles have no umpire among them.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 212) a single ultimate standard is an advantage, since it can decide conflicts.

## Evidence offered

- The Epicurean reply to the comparison with swine (p. 197).
- The appeal to what 'few human creatures' would consent to: becoming an animal, a fool, an ignoramus or a rascal for the sake of contentment (p. 198).
- The Stoic under the Roman Empire, who can do without happiness and so is free from anxiety (p. 204).
- The rescuer from drowning and the betrayer of a friend, as acts right or wrong whatever the motive (p. 206); the tyrant who rescues in order to torture, as a different act (p. 213, Mill's footnote).
- Veracity as a rule of 'transcendant expediency' with admitted exceptions for the malefactor and the dangerously ill (pp. 209-210).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The fool and the pig 'only know their own side of the question' (p. 199) repeats, for pleasures, On Liberty's argument about opinions (ch02, p. 36), and the test of competent judges presupposes that people have been free to try both, which is the 'experiments of living' of ch03 (p. 53). Mill does not draw the connection in this chapter; Schneewind does (fm01, p. 6).
- The explanation of why people sink to lower pleasures, that capacity for nobler feeling dies for want of sustenance in the occupations and society their position gives them (p. 199), is the argument The Subjection of Women makes about women's stunted faculties (ch08) and the reason liberty matters in ch03.
- A person denied 'the liberty to use the sources of happiness within his reach' by bad laws or 'subjection to the will of others' loses an otherwise attainable happiness (p. 203). This is the one explicit statement in Utilitarianism that liberty is a condition of happiness.
- The requirement of strict impartiality (p. 205) sits beside the statement that most people need attend only to the private utility of a few persons (p. 206). The reconciliation is through secondary rules and rights.
- The sentence on abstinences (pp. 206-207) and the Nautical Almanack passage (p. 211) support a rule-based reading; the opening definition (p. 196) and the handling of exceptions to veracity by direct weighing (p. 210) support an act-based one. The editor's commentary (ap03) reports this dispute.
- Education and opinion should establish an 'indissoluble association' between each person's happiness and the good of the whole (p. 205). On Liberty warns against State moulding of minds (ch05, p. 96). Mill's answer would be that the association here is with others' good in general and not with a particular creed, but the passage itself sets no limit on the means.
- Rights as 'legitimate and authorized expectations' (p. 206) is developed in ch14.

## Open questions

- If competent judges would not exchange a higher pleasure for any quantity of a lower (p. 198), is the higher pleasure's value still a quantity of pleasure at all?
- Who counts as competent, given that Mill says most people lose the capacity for higher pleasures (p. 199)? The test risks counting only those who already prefer what Mill prefers.
- When may an individual set aside a secondary rule? The text says only when rules conflict (p. 212), yet also says the received code is improvable and philosophers may find better (p. 211).
