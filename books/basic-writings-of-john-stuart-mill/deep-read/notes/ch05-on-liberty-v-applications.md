# ch05: On Liberty V: Applications (pp. 85–104)

Scope note: chapter text pp. 85-104. No footnotes by Mill. He calls these 'specimens of application', meant to illustrate the principles and not to follow them out (p. 85).

## Question this chapter answers

How do the two maxims of the essay decide concrete questions of trade, prevention, taxation, contract, family, education and the size of government?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the two maxims | p. 85 | First, the individual is not accountable to society for actions that concern only his own interests; advice, instruction, persuasion and avoidance are the only measures allowed. Second, for actions prejudicial to others' interests he is accountable and may be punished socially or legally if society thinks it requisite. |
| Free Trade | p. 86 | The doctrine that cheapness and quality are best secured by leaving producers and sellers free. It rests on grounds different from the principle of liberty, because trade is a social act. |
| preventive function / functions of police | p. 87 | Government precaution against crime or accident before the event. More liable to abuse than punishment. |
| preappointed evidence | p. 88 | Bentham's term: formalities required in advance (registers, witnesses) so that proof exists if a transaction is later questioned. |
| offences against decency | p. 89 | Acts harmless in private that violate good manners when done publicly and so may be prohibited. |
| moral anomaly | p. 90 | Punishing the accessory (pimp, gambling-house keeper) while the principal goes free. |
| paternal government | p. 92 | Treating adults, here the labouring classes, as children under an education of restraint. |
| liberty (as presumption from choice) | p. 93 | A person's voluntary choice is evidence that what he chooses is desirable or endurable to him. This is the stated reason for not interfering. |
| bureaucracy / pedantocracy | pp. 100, 102 | A governing body that absorbs the nation's ability; it enslaves governors and governed alike and decays without outside criticism. |
| dissemination of power, centralization of information | p. 102 | The practical principle for government: spread power as widely as efficiency allows; gather and diffuse knowledge from the centre. |

Shift to note: 'liberty consists in doing what one desires' (p. 87) is a new and thinner definition, introduced for the unsafe-bridge case. The slavery passage then says the point of liberty is defeated by a choice that ends all future choice (p. 93). The two definitions pull apart.

## Leading propositions

- Damage to others' interests can alone justify interference, but does not always justify it (p. 85).
- Losses inflicted by fair competition give the losers no right to protection; society interferes only against fraud, treachery and force (p. 86).
- Trade is a social act and within society's jurisdiction; restrictions on it are wrong only when they fail to produce their intended results. Control for prevention of adulteration, for sanitation and for protection of workers is in principle legitimate (p. 86).
- Restrictions aimed at making a commodity hard to obtain (the Maine Law, opium, poisons) are questions of liberty, and the liberty infringed is the buyer's (pp. 86-87).
- A person about to cross an unsafe bridge may be seized if there is no time to warn him; where there is only a risk, an adult in possession of his faculties should be warned and not prevented (p. 87).
- Labelling poisons and keeping a register of sales are legitimate; requiring a medical certificate in every case is not (pp. 87-88).
- A person once convicted of violence when drunk may be put under a special restriction; drunkenness in him is a crime against others (p. 88).
- Offences against decency done publicly may be prohibited (p. 89).
- Whatever it is permitted to do it must be permitted to advise; the doubtful case is the adviser with a pecuniary interest. Mill gives both sides and declines to decide (pp. 89-90).
- Taxing stimulants solely to make them harder to get differs only in degree from prohibition; taxing them for revenue up to the revenue-maximising point is to be approved (p. 91).
- Licensing sellers for public order is fit; limiting the number of houses to reduce temptation treats the labouring classes as children (pp. 91-92).
- A contract to sell oneself into slavery is void: the principle of freedom cannot require that a person be free not to be free (p. 93).
- Engagements involving personal relations should be releasable, but reliance by the other party and the existence of children create moral obligations that greatly affect moral freedom even if not legal freedom (pp. 93-94).
- Liberty is misplaced where a person acts for another under pretext that the other's affairs are his own. The State must control the power it allows one person over others (p. 94).
- Wives need only the same rights and legal protection as everyone else (pp. 94-95).
- The State should compel education and need not provide it; a general State education is a contrivance for moulding people alike. Enforcement is by public examination confined to fact and positive science (pp. 95-97).
- Causing a child to exist without a fair prospect of a desirable existence is a crime against the child; laws forbidding marriage without means to support a family do not violate liberty (pp. 97-98).
- Three objections to government doing things for people, apart from liberty: individuals do it better; doing it educates them; and every added function increases government's power (pp. 98-99).
- If all able people were drawn into the government's service, no reform against the bureaucracy's interest would be possible (p. 100).
- The worth of a State is the worth of the individuals composing it (p. 103).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 86) it is better for the general interest that people pursue their objects undeterred by competitors' disappointment + (p. 86) society recognises no right to immunity from that kind of loss.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 86) harm to interests is necessary but not sufficient for interference. The decision is made by general interest, that is by utility, and not by the harm principle alone.
- **Premises**: (p. 93) the reason for not interfering with voluntary acts is that choice is evidence of the agent's good + (p. 93) by selling himself a person forgoes all future use of his liberty.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 93) the slavery contract defeats the purpose that justifies leaving him free, so it is not protected. Mill adds that these reasons are 'of far wider application' and are limited only by the necessities of life. Unstated premise: liberty is valued as a continuing capacity, not as the satisfaction of whatever is now desired. That fits ch03 and conflicts with the definition on p. 87.
- **Premises**: (p. 94) a person should be free in his own concerns + (p. 94) a child's or wife's affairs are not the father's or husband's own concerns.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 94-95) the family is a sphere of power over others, where the State's duty is control and not abstention.
- **Premises**: (p. 96) diversity of character and opinion requires diversity of education + (p. 96) State education casts minds in the mould that pleases the predominant power.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 95-96) the State should require education and test it, and leave its provision to others.
- **Premises**: (p. 99) each added function widens government's influence over hopes and fears + (pp. 100-101) a bureaucracy holding all practical ability cannot be checked or reformed from outside, as in Russia + (p. 102) it also decays without outside criticism.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 102) disperse power as far as efficiency allows and centralise information.

## Evidence offered

- Competitive examinations and overcrowded professions as cases of legitimate harm (p. 86).
- The unsafe bridge (p. 87); the poison register modelled on the formalities of contract (p. 88).
- Continental laws forbidding marriage without means (p. 98).
- Russia, where the Czar cannot govern against the bureaucracy; France and the United States as peoples used to managing their own affairs; the Chinese mandarin and the Jesuit as slaves of their organisation (pp. 100-101).
- The Poor Law Board as a model of central superintendence with limited coercive power (p. 103).

## Quotes

- "that the individual is not accountable to society for his actions, in so far as these concern the interests of no person but himself" (p. 85)
- "for liberty consists in doing what one desires, and he does not desire to fall into the river" (p. 87)
- "Whatever it is permitted to do, it must be permitted to advise to do." (p. 89)
- "The principle of freedom cannot require that he should be free not to be free. It is not freedom, to be allowed to alienate his freedom." (p. 93)
- "so much less do the generality of mankind value liberty than power" (p. 95)
- "A general State education is a mere contrivance for moulding people to be exactly like one another" (p. 96)
- "the greatest dissemination of power consistent with efficiency; but the greatest possible centralization of information, and diffusion of it from the centre" (p. 102)
- "The worth of a State, in the long run, is the worth of the individuals composing it" (p. 103)
- "with small men no great thing can really be accomplished" (p. 104)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The chapter shows how much the 'one very simple principle' of ch01 (p. 15) leaves open. Harm is not sufficient (p. 85); trade is outside the principle (p. 86); indecency is prohibited as an offence against others (p. 89) although ch04 denied that offence is injury (p. 77); and the interested-instigator case is left undecided (p. 90).
- The slavery passage (p. 93) is the one place Mill overrides a self-regarding adult choice. He says the reasoning has 'far wider application' but does not say how far.
- The passage on husbands and wives (pp. 94-95) is the programme of The Subjection of Women in one sentence, and the charge that people value power more than liberty (p. 95) is its psychology (ch06, ch09). The treatment of marriage as a releasable engagement qualified by obligations to children (pp. 93-94) is as far as Mill goes on divorce in this volume; ch07 declines to discuss it.
- The view that parents may be restrained from having children they cannot support (p. 98) is a wide reading of harm to others: it counts lowering wages by competition as an offence. That sits badly with the earlier denial that competitive loss is a wrong (p. 86).
- The developmental argument for local self-government (p. 99) applies ch03's doctrine that faculties grow by use to political life.
- The closing sentence joins the liberty argument to national strength: a State that dwarfs its people for the sake of docility loses the vital power it needs (pp. 103-104).

## Open questions

- If the slavery argument generalises, which other self-binding choices does it reach: lifelong vows, marriage without exit, addiction?
- Why is indecency a harm when religious offence is not? Mill says only that the subject is connected indirectly and passes on (p. 89).
- Mill approves 'paternal' restraint for people proved incapable of being governed as freemen (p. 92), as for 'backward' societies in ch01 (p. 16). What proof would suffice?
