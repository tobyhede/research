# fm01: Front Matter: Biographical Note, Schneewind Introduction, Note on the Text (pp. 1–8)

Scope note: the unit holds the copyright page and an unsigned biographical note (pp. 1-3), the table of contents (p. 3), J. B. Schneewind's introduction 'Making Our Lives Our Own' (pp. 3-7), the note on the text (pp. 7-8), and Mill's dedication of On Liberty with its Humboldt epigraph (p. 8). None of it is by Mill except the dedication. The claims below are the editors', and they are a reading of Mill that the later notes test against Mill's own text.

## Question this unit answers

Why are these three works printed together, and what single project do they serve?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| transitional age | p. 4 | Mill's name for his own time: the old consensus formed by recognised authorities has broken down and public opinion is anarchic (pp. 4-5). |
| comprehensive doctrine | p. 4 | A shared view of morality, politics and religion that unifies a society and lets improvement happen without disorder. Schneewind says the young Mill thought stability needed one (p. 5). |
| principle of utility / greatest happiness principle | p. 5 | Bentham's single principle: in all actions try to bring about the greatest happiness of the greatest number of sentient beings, animals included. |
| happiness (Mill's specific view) | pp. 5-6 | Not only pleasure and absence of pain. The greatest source of pleasure is the free exercise of our abilities in a society of equals. This is Schneewind's summary, not a quotation from Mill. |
| commonsense morality | p. 5 | A repository of beliefs about what increases happiness. We can usually live by it and use the principle of utility mainly to revise it. |
| experiments in living | p. 6 | Trying unconventional ways of life to find out what makes lives more worthwhile. Schneewind treats them as the source of data for a scientific study of happiness (pp. 6-7). |
| internalization of the demand for conformity | p. 6 | Schneewind's modern gloss on the social tyranny Mill fears: we are raised to want conventional things and so stop ourselves developing our own tastes. |
| individualism | p. 6 | The charge of narrow self-centredness often made against Mill, which Schneewind rejects by pointing to The Subjection of Women. |

## Leading propositions

- On Liberty is the central work; The Subjection of Women illustrates some of its main claims and Utilitarianism gives a philosophical grounding for them (p. 4).
- Mill's thought is shaped by one question: how a reformer who alters basic institutions to increase happiness can also meet the need for social stability (p. 4).
- In The Spirit of the Age (1831) Mill held that opinion is shaped by a few accepted authorities, that their agreement makes orderly improvement possible, and that Europe since the French Revolution has been in a transitional stage without such agreement (pp. 4-5).
- Mill wanted utilitarianism to become the new consensus, and thought better reasoning would get it there; A System of Logic (1843) was meant to teach clear thinking (p. 5).
- A large part of Mill's argument for utilitarianism is indirect: he shows that current practices deprive people of happiness and expects readers to see they already accept the principle (p. 5).
- The principle of utility is too abstract to apply directly. Commonsense morality and a specific view of happiness make it usable (pp. 5-6).
- The liberty problem of Mill's time is oppression by the majority, and its more insidious form is internal: people come to want only conventional things (p. 6).
- The richest individual self is developed only through relations with others who are equally free; men deprive themselves of this with half the human race, and women are the primary sufferers (p. 6).
- Liberty has an indirect benefit: experiments in living tell us which pleasures are higher, and that knowledge is needed to guide social change (pp. 6-7).
- Granting equality to women would be the experiment in living that affects the most people (p. 7).
- Mill's conclusions about liberty and equality could be as strongly linked to other moral principles as to utility, and society may not need agreement on a comprehensive doctrine at all (p. 7).
- Texts used: On Liberty from the second edition (1863), The Subjection of Women from the third (1870), Utilitarianism from the fourth (1871) (p. 8).
- Mill's dedication calls Harriet Taylor Mill the inspirer and in part the author of all that is best in his writings, and says the book lacked her final revision (p. 8).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 5) the bare principle of utility cannot be applied to cases + (p. 5) commonsense morality already encodes much about what increases happiness + (p. 6) Mill holds that the greatest pleasure is free exercise of abilities among equals.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 6) the principle is used to revise accepted morality where it blocks free development and equality. On Liberty and The Subjection of Women are two such revisions.
- **Premises**: (p. 6) some pleasures are preferable to others + (p. 6) only experiment shows which + (pp. 6-7) experiment needs freedom for everyone to try different modes of living.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 7) liberty is a condition of knowing what happiness is, so a utilitarian has reason to protect it. Unstated premise: the results of one person's experiment are evidence about what would make others happy.
- **Premises**: (p. 7) the value of free development among equals may be more certain than any deeper principle.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 7) the case for liberty and equality survives even if utilitarianism is rejected. This is the editor's position and it loosens the dependence Mill himself asserts.

## Evidence offered

- Biographical facts: Mill born 1806, educated by his father, elected to Parliament 1865, moved a motion for women's votes in 1867, died 1873 (p. 1, p. 4).
- Mill's own early essay The Spirit of the Age as the source of the stability problem (p. 4).
- Whitman's Democratic Vistas, which cites the essay on Liberty for two constituents of a grand nationality, variety of character and full play for human nature (p. 7).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Schneewind's ordering (Liberty first, Subjection as illustration, Utilitarianism as ground) is the order of the volume and not the order of composition or of logical dependence. On Liberty itself says it rests on utility 'in the largest sense' (ch01, p. 16).
- The claim that higher pleasures are found by experiment joins Utilitarianism II (ch11) to On Liberty III (ch03). Check there whether Mill makes the link himself or whether it is the editor's construction.
- The claim that Subjection answers the charge of self-centred individualism is tested in ch07 and ch09, where marriage between equals is described.
- The Humboldt epigraph announces the theme of ch03 before the harm principle is stated in ch01.

## Open questions

- Does Mill anywhere in these three works say that the greatest source of pleasure is free exercise of abilities among equals, or is that a synthesis by the editor?
- If the conclusions can be detached from utility (p. 7), is Utilitarianism doing any work in the volume beyond supplying a vocabulary?
