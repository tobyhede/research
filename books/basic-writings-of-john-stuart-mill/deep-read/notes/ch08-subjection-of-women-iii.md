# ch08: The Subjection of Women III (pp. 146–172)

Scope note: chapter text pp. 146-171. Mill's two footnotes (on women regents in Indian principalities; a quotation from Reynolds's Discourses) are printed at pp. 171-172.

## Question this chapter answers

Should women be admitted to the suffrage and to all the occupations and public functions now reserved to men, and what can be inferred from the fact that no woman has produced work of the very first rank?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| disabilities | p. 146 | Legal exclusions of women from occupations and public functions. Said to be kept up in order to maintain subordination in domestic life. |
| suffrage | p. 148 | The right to share in choosing those who exercise a public trust. A means of self-protection due to everyone, independent of fitness to govern. |
| women as they are | p. 149 | Women as experience has shown them under existing education and circumstances. Mill argues from this humble ground and sets natural constitution aside. |
| negative and positive evidence | p. 149 | What women have not done proves little; what any woman has done proves that women can do it. |
| intuition / intuitive perception | pp. 152-153 | Rapid and correct insight into present fact. Nothing to do with general principles. |
| practical talent | p. 153 | Discerning the particular cases in which general principles do and do not apply. Mill says women as they now are have a peculiar aptitude for it. |
| nervous temperament | p. 156 | A constitutional and hereditary sensibility found in both sexes, perhaps in more women. Capable of sustained excitement and, when trained, of strong self-control. |
| originality | p. 163 | In the high sense, the great new ideas that form an era in thought or found a school in art. The one deficiency Mill grants in women's work. |
| amateurs and professionals | p. 166 | The explanation of women's lesser eminence in the fine arts: they are taught the arts but not to live by them. |
| laws of the formation of character | p. 160 | As in ch06: the unstudied psychology of how circumstances make character. |

Shift to note: ch06 held that no one is entitled to any positive opinion on differences between the sexes (p. 123). This chapter repeats the disclaimer four times (pp. 152, 160, 162, 165) and between them describes women's 'peculiar tendencies and aptitudes' at length. Mill's stated licence is that doubt does not forbid conjecture (p. 162) and that he speaks of women 'as they are'.

## Leading propositions

- The disabilities of women outside the family are clung to only to maintain their subordination within it, because most men cannot yet tolerate living with an equal (p. 146).
- Power now pretends to oppress for the good of the oppressed, so exclusions are justified by alleged incapacity (p. 146).
- To justify exclusion it is not enough that women are on average less gifted; it would have to be true that no woman is fit and that the most eminent women are inferior to the most mediocre men who now hold the posts (pp. 146-147).
- Many women have proved capable of everything men do, falling short only of the very highest rank in some things (p. 147).
- Exclusion wrongs the women, who have an equal moral right to choose their occupation at their own risk, and injures those who would have benefited from their services and from wider competition (pp. 147-148).
- The suffrage stands apart from all questions of faculty: a voice in choosing one's governors is a means of self-protection due to everyone (p. 148).
- On whatever conditions men vote, there is no justification for not admitting women on the same (p. 148).
- Where women's interests as such are involved, they need the vote as their guarantee of just consideration; laws made by masters do not protect slaves (pp. 148-149).
- Any woman who succeeds in an open profession proves by that fact that she is qualified (p. 149).
- The only things law excludes women from are those they have proved they can do: nothing forbids a woman to write Shakespeare's plays, but a woman may not hold the smallest political office (p. 150).
- Reigning queens, regents and governors show a high proportion of talent for rule (pp. 150-151).
- Princesses are the only women allowed the same range of interests and development as men, and in their case no inferiority is found (p. 152).
- The general bent of women's talents, as they have hitherto been, is toward the practical (p. 152).
- Women's sense of present fact is a corrective to the characteristic error of speculative minds, and the criticism of a superior woman is of the greatest value to a thinker (pp. 153-154).
- Nervous susceptibility is largely the product of upbringing; where constitutional, it does not unfit men for affairs and need not unfit women (pp. 155-157).
- Mobility of mind, if women have it, affects the kind of excellence and not its worth, and is a positive advantage in practice (p. 158).
- The fact of smaller brains is doubtful, the relation of size to intellect is not understood, and quality and activity matter as well as quantity (pp. 159-160).
- Popular ideas of women's nature differ from country to country according to how women there have been formed (pp. 160-161).
- No work of the first rank in philosophy, science or art has been by a woman; this is the one marked case of apparent inferiority (p. 162).
- It is explained without supposing natural incapacity: too little time has passed, originality now requires long preparation women have been denied, women's literature has had to begin as imitation, women artists are amateurs, women have no time of their own, and they are not encouraged to desire fame (pp. 162-169).
- The whole habit of women's lives is a complete explanation of nearly all the apparent differences between women and men, including all that imply inferiority (p. 169).
- The compliment that women are morally better than men is empty: nowhere else is it thought suitable that the better should obey the worse. If true, it shows only the corrupting effect of power (p. 169).
- That women do not complain as a body does not make the privilege less unjust; they cannot be expected to devote themselves to emancipation until men join them (pp. 170-171).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 147) under competition or any choice that regards the public interest, posts will not go to women inferior to their male competitors + (p. 147) many women have shown the required capacity.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 147) a general exclusion can be justified only by the claim that no woman at all is fit, which is false. This is ch06's argument against presumptions (p. 119) applied to public functions.
- **Premises**: (p. 148) the right to choose one's governors is distinct from fitness to govern + (p. 148) it is a means of self-protection + (p. 148) the law already trusts a woman to choose the man who will govern her for life.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 148) women should have the suffrage on the same terms as men. The argument is independent of every claim about women's capacities, which is why Mill puts it first.
- **Premises**: (p. 149) negative evidence proves little, positive evidence is conclusive + (pp. 150-151) women have governed well where they were allowed to.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 152) where women's capacity for government has been tried it has been found adequate. Unstated premise: the sample of queens and regents is not selected in a way that flatters the result. Mill notes how few there have been (p. 150) and does not consider that weak queens may be less remembered or that their ministers governed.
- **Premises**: (p. 162) no first-rank work is by a woman + (pp. 162-169) each cause that would produce this result without natural incapacity is known to be present.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 169) the fact is fully accounted for by circumstances. This shows that natural incapacity is not needed to explain the facts; it does not show there is none. Mill's stronger sentence at p. 169 goes beyond what the argument supports and beyond his own agnosticism at p. 160.

## Evidence offered

- Elizabeth, Deborah, Joan of Arc and Victoria (pp. 149-150); Blanche of Castile and Anne de Beaujeu, referred to as mother and sister of French kings; Margaret of Austria; Catherine II; Catherine de' Medici (pp. 150-151).
- Mill's footnote from his India House experience: of well-governed Hindu principalities, three in four are under a woman regent (p. 171). This is testimony from his own official knowledge and cannot be checked from the text.
- The disappearance of 'hysterics' and fainting fits since they went out of fashion (p. 155).
- National temperaments: French, Italians, Greeks, Romans and Irish compared with English, Germans and Swiss (p. 157). These are stereotypes used as premises.
- A man who weighed brains reported that the heaviest he knew was a woman's (p. 159). Hearsay, and Mill then offers his own speculative physiology of size against circulation, labelled 'entirely hypothetical' (p. 160).
- Sappho, Myrtis, Corinna, Aspasia, Hypatia, Heloisa, Mrs. Somerville, Madame de Staël and George Sand (pp. 163-164).
- Mill's own case: a very large proportion of his original thoughts came from a woman's suggestion (p. 165).
- Roman literature as imitation of Greek; women artists as amateurs; acting as the one art women follow professionally and equal men in (pp. 165-166).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The chapter's own method is at odds with ch06. There Mill denied anyone could know the nature of the sexes; here he grants 'admitted points of superiority in women' such as insight into character (p. 151), builds an account of women's practical bent (pp. 152-155), and conjectures a physiological basis (p. 160). He disclaims each time, but the disclaimers and the generalisations alternate. The fair reading is that he argues on two tracks: nothing is known about nature, and even taking women as they now are, the case for exclusion fails.
- The suffrage argument rests on self-protection (p. 148), the ground On Liberty gives for all legitimate interference (ch01, p. 15), and on the need of every class to have its interests represented, which ch14 states as equal counting (p. 244).
- The explanation of women's lack of first-rank work repeats On Liberty ch03 on the conditions of originality: 'Genius can only breathe freely in an atmosphere of freedom' (p. 61). Women 'more subdued to opinion' (p. 161) are the extreme case of the conformity that chapter describes.
- The claim that the English have 'no inclination but that of following a rule' (p. 161) is nearly word for word On Liberty ch03 (p. 58).
- The observation that everything a woman does is done at odd times, and that the superintendence of a household consumes thought (pp. 167-168), undercuts ch07's recommendation that married women ordinarily take household management as their vocation (pp. 145-146). Mill does not notice the conflict.
- That a wife's suggestions appear as her husband's ideas, 'if I may judge by my own case' (p. 165), connects to the dedication of On Liberty (fm01, p. 8).
- The remark that servitude corrupts the masters more than the slaves (p. 169) is developed in ch09 as the moral cost to men.

## Open questions

- Is the appeal to queens consistent with ch06's own rule that experience of women under present conditions tells us little?
- If the differences 'as they are' are valuable (women's practicality correcting men's abstraction, pp. 153-154), would Mill regret their disappearance under equal education? He says he believes there will prove to be no natural tendencies distinguishing women's genius (p. 165).
- The causes offered for women's lack of original work are social and many are not legal. What, beyond removing legal disabilities, does Mill think is required?
