# ch12: Utilitarianism III: Of the Ultimate Sanction of the Principle of Utility (pp. 213–220)

Scope note: chapter text pp. 213-220; no footnotes by Mill.

## Question this chapter answers

What motives are there to obey the utilitarian standard, and where does its binding force come from?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sanction | p. 213 | The motive to obey a moral standard; the source of its obligation or binding force. |
| customary morality | p. 214 | The morality that education and opinion have consecrated, and the only one that feels obligatory in itself. |
| external sanctions | p. 214 | Hope of favour and fear of displeasure from other people or from God, together with sympathy and affection for them and love and awe of Him. |
| internal sanction | p. 215 | A feeling in one's own mind: a pain attendant on violating duty. |
| conscience | p. 215 | That feeling when disinterested and attached to the pure idea of duty, in practice encrusted with associations from sympathy, love, fear, religion, childhood and self-esteem. |
| natural (of moral feelings) | p. 217 | Acquired, but a natural outgrowth of human nature, as speech, reasoning and building cities are. Not innate. |
| social feelings / feeling of unity | p. 218 | The desire to be in unity with our fellow creatures. The natural basis of sentiment for utilitarian morality. |
| analysis (dissolving force of) | p. 217 | Intellectual scrutiny, which wears away moral associations that are wholly artificial. |

Shift to note: 'natural' here means a normal outgrowth of human nature under cultivation (p. 217). Compare ch03, where a person's 'nature' is his own developed bent (p. 58), and The Subjection of Women, where what passes for women's nature is called artificial (ch06).

## Leading propositions

- The question of sanction arises for every moral standard, whenever people are asked to rest morality on a basis they are not used to (p. 214).
- Only customary morality feels obligatory in itself, so a principle offered as its foundation seems less binding than its corollaries (p. 214).
- The principle of utility has, or could have, all the sanctions of any other system (p. 214).
- External sanctions will attach to utility in proportion to general intelligence, because people desire happiness and commend conduct that promotes theirs (pp. 214-215).
- The internal sanction of any standard is one and the same: a feeling in the mind (p. 215).
- The binding force of conscience is a mass of feeling that must be broken through to do wrong and is met afterwards as remorse (p. 215).
- So the ultimate sanction of all morality, external motives apart, is the conscientious feelings of mankind (pp. 215-216).
- Belief that obligation is an objective reality adds nothing: a person is moved only by his own subjective feeling, and conscience is as easily silenced by intuitionists as by utilitarians (p. 216).
- If the moral feeling were innate, regard for the pleasures and pains of others is what it would most plausibly attach to (p. 217).
- Mill's own view is that moral feelings are acquired, and no less natural for that (p. 217).
- The moral faculty can be cultivated in almost any direction, so that almost anything can be given the authority of conscience (p. 217).
- Associations that are wholly artificial yield to analysis; utility would be exposed to this if it had no natural basis (pp. 217-218).
- It has one: the social feelings of mankind, which strengthen with civilization (p. 218).
- Society between equals can exist only on the understanding that the interests of all are regarded equally (p. 218).
- Every step in political improvement removes opposition of interest and levels inequalities of legal privilege, so the feeling of unity grows (p. 219).
- If this feeling were taught as a religion, no one could doubt the sufficiency of the sanction; Comte has shown the possibility, and the danger is excess that would interfere with freedom and individuality (pp. 219-220).
- Even now, a person of developed social feeling needs to feel that his real aim and that of others do not conflict; this conviction is the ultimate sanction of the greatest-happiness morality (p. 220).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 215) the internal sanction of every moral standard is a feeling + (p. 216) feelings of conscience exist and can be cultivated in connexion with any rule.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 216) utility has the same sanction as any other standard and is no worse placed.
- **Premises**: (p. 217) artificial moral associations dissolve under analysis + (p. 218) the desire for unity with others is a powerful natural sentiment that grows with civilization.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 218) the association of duty with general happiness will not be analysed away, because it harmonises with a leading part of human nature. Unstated premise: the social feelings are themselves robust under analysis, that is, a reflective person will endorse them. Mill asserts they present themselves 'as an attribute which it would not be well for them to be without' (p. 220).
- **Premises**: (p. 218) society is impossible, except as master and slave, unless the interests of all are consulted + (p. 218) between equals, interests must be regarded equally + (p. 218) everyone but an absolute monarch has equals.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 218-219) life in society trains people to regard others' good as a thing naturally to be attended to. The argument ties the growth of utilitarian motivation to the growth of equality.

## Evidence offered

- The horror of crime in a well-brought-up young person as the model of an acquired but deep-rooted feeling (p. 214).
- The ease with which conscience is silenced in all minds, admitted by all moralists (p. 216).
- Speech, reasoning, city-building and agriculture as acquired yet natural capacities (p. 217).
- Comte's Système de Politique Positive as showing that service of humanity can have the power of a religion (p. 219).

## Quotes

- "why am I bound to promote the general happiness?" (p. 214)
- "a feeling in our own mind; a pain, more or less intense, attendant on violation of duty" (p. 215)
- "the same as of all other moral standards—the conscientious feelings of mankind" (p. 216)
- "there is hardly anything so absurd or so mischievous that it may not, by means of these influences, be made to act on the human mind with all the authority of conscience" (p. 217)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The chapter answers a question of motivation, not of justification. It shows people can be brought to care about general happiness; whether they ought to is left to ch13.
- Mill admits that conscience can be attached to almost anything (p. 217). This is the same fact that On Liberty treats as a danger: sincere moral feeling behind persecution (ch01, p. 13; ch02, pp. 27-29).
- The proposal to teach the feeling of unity 'as a religion', with the whole force of education, institutions and opinion (p. 219), is immediately checked by the warning about Comte and 'human freedom and individuality' (p. 220). On Liberty attacks Comte's system as a despotism of society over the individual (ch01, p. 18). The two books meet here, and Mill gives no rule for how far the cultivation may go.
- The claim that society between equals requires equal regard for interests, and that levelling legal privilege extends moral concern (pp. 218-219), is the moral psychology behind The Subjection of Women: the family as a school of equality or of despotism (ch07).
- 'Master and slave' is named as the one form of society possible without consulting all interests (p. 218); Subjection classes marriage law with it (ch06, ch07).

## Open questions

- If the binding force is only as strong as the feeling (p. 216), has Mill any answer to a person who lacks it other than external sanctions? He says no (p. 216).
- Is the growth of social feeling with civilization (p. 218) consistent with On Liberty's account of the same civilization producing conformity and 'collective mediocrity' (ch03, p. 62)?
