## 30-second brief

- **Problem:** how far may society, through law or through opinion, control what individuals think and do, and on what standard should that question, and the position of women, be decided?
- **Thesis:** Mill limits coercion of competent adults in societies he regards as civilised to preventing harm to others. This can include enforcing duties such as giving evidence or saving a life. Free discussion and individuality are conditions of human improvement. The legal subordination of women is an unjustified relic of force. The standard behind all three claims is general happiness, of which justice and rights are the most binding part.
- **Who benefits:** anyone arguing about free speech, paternalism, equality or how to weigh consequences.
- **Worth reading?** Yes. On Liberty I to III, Subjection I and Utilitarianism II and V carry most of it.

## 3-minute mental model

### Core ideas, ranked

1. **The harm principle.** Power may be used over an adult member of a civilized community, against his will, only to prevent harm to others. His own good is not enough. The rule binds public opinion as well as law. [On Liberty I, line 296](./source/source.txt#L296)
2. **Silencing an opinion is always a loss.** If it is true we lose the truth, if false we lose the understanding that comes from contesting it, and usually each side holds part of the truth. [On Liberty II, line 326](./source/source.txt#L326) [line 449](./source/source.txt#L449)
3. **Individuality is part of well-being and the source of progress.** A person who lives by custom makes no choice and exercises no faculty. [On Liberty III, line 487](./source/source.txt#L487) [line 493](./source/source.txt#L493)
4. **Custom is not evidence, and no one knows the nature of the sexes.** Women's subordination began in force, every domination has seemed natural to its holders, and what is called women's nature is a product of their position. [Subjection I, line 724](./source/source.txt#L724) [line 736](./source/source.txt#L736) [line 763](./source/source.txt#L763)
5. **Happiness is the standard, with pleasures ranked by quality and applied through secondary rules.** [Utilitarianism II, line 1044](./source/source.txt#L1044) [line 1050](./source/source.txt#L1050) [line 1110](./source/source.txt#L1110)
6. **Justice and rights are utility's most stringent rules.** A right is what society ought to defend me in possessing, and the reason is general utility, above all the interest in security. [Utilitarianism V, line 1285](./source/source.txt#L1285) [line 1306](./source/source.txt#L1306)
7. **The family should be a school of equality and is now a school of despotism.** Hence equal marriage law, the suffrage and open occupations. [Subjection II, line 829](./source/source.txt#L829) [Subjection III, line 851](./source/source.txt#L851)

### Three things to remember

- The harm principle states when society may consider interfering, not when it should. Mill says damage to others' interests can alone justify interference and does not always justify it. [On Liberty V, line 641](./source/source.txt#L641) The editor puts it as a necessary and not a sufficient condition. [Commentary, line 1510](./source/source.txt#L1510)
- Mill's enemy is opinion as much as law. He calls social tyranny more formidable than many kinds of political oppression, and in the Subjection he argues that men want women's sentiments and not only their obedience. [On Liberty I, line 284](./source/source.txt#L284) [Subjection I, line 742](./source/source.txt#L742)
- Mill claims to rest everything on utility and forgoes abstract right. [On Liberty I, line 302](./source/source.txt#L302) Whether the utility he means is still a sum of pleasures is the open question of the volume, and the editors say so. [Schneewind, line 216](./source/source.txt#L216) [Commentary, line 1852](./source/source.txt#L1852)

### What it changes in the reader's thinking

**[Analysis]** The three works read together are one argument about character. Mill asks of every institution what kind of people it makes: whether it leaves them to choose, to hear the other side, and to live among equals. Read this way the harm principle is less a boundary line than a burden of proof, and the essay on women is its hardest test case, because there the coercion is done by education and affection more than by penalty. The reader also learns to separate three questions that are usually run together: whether conduct harms anyone, whether interference would do more good than harm, and whether the feeling against the conduct is anything more than custom.

## 10-minute deep brief

### 1. The harm principle

- **Author's claim:** the sole end for which mankind may interfere with anyone's liberty of action is self-protection. Compulsion, whether by legal penalty or by the moral coercion of public opinion, is justified only to prevent harm to others. [On Liberty I, line 296](./source/source.txt#L296)
- **Reasoning:** popular government does not remove the danger, because self-government means the government of each by all the rest, and society can also enforce its will without the magistrate. [On Liberty I, line 281](./source/source.txt#L281) [line 284](./source/source.txt#L284) The rules now enforced rest on the likings and dislikings of society or its powerful part. [On Liberty I, line 290](./source/source.txt#L290) The ground of the principle is utility in the largest sense, not abstract right. [On Liberty I, line 302](./source/source.txt#L302) Each person has the greatest interest in his own well-being and far the best knowledge of his own circumstances, while society acts on general presumptions, so when the public interferes with personal conduct the odds are that it interferes wrongly. [On Liberty IV, line 570](./source/source.txt#L570) [line 594](./source/source.txt#L594)
- **Evidence:** worked cases. A Muslim majority's disgust at pork gives no ground for forbidding it, and the same logic would license every persecution. [On Liberty IV, line 600](./source/source.txt#L600) No one should be punished simply for being drunk, but a soldier or policeman drunk on duty should be. [On Liberty IV, line 588](./source/source.txt#L588)
- **Boundary:** the principle applies only to adults in the maturity of their faculties, and Mill excludes children and societies he calls backward. [On Liberty I, line 299](./source/source.txt#L299) People may be compelled to some positive acts, such as giving evidence or saving a life. [On Liberty I, line 302](./source/source.txt#L302) Harm is narrowed in Chapter IV to injury to interests that ought to be considered rights, and to definite damage or risk of it. [On Liberty IV, line 567](./source/source.txt#L567) [line 588](./source/source.txt#L588) Trade falls outside the principle, public indecency may be prohibited, and a contract to sell oneself into slavery is void. [On Liberty V, line 644](./source/source.txt#L644) [line 653](./source/source.txt#L653) [line 665](./source/source.txt#L665) The editor calls the description of this as one simple principle extremely misleading and notes that Mill never says at length how harm is to be defined. [Commentary, line 1356](./source/source.txt#L1356) [line 1507](./source/source.txt#L1507)
- **Application [Application]:** when a rule about conduct is proposed, ask who is harmed and whether the harm is to an interest that deserves protection or only to someone's taste. Then ask separately whether enforcing the rule would do more good than harm.

> “His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant.” [On Liberty I, line 296](./source/source.txt#L296)

### 2. Liberty of thought and discussion

- **Author's claim:** if all mankind minus one were of one opinion, they would have no more right to silence the one than he would have to silence them. [On Liberty II, line 326](./source/source.txt#L326)
- **Reasoning:** three cases. The suppressed opinion may be true, and to refuse it a hearing is to assume infallibility. [On Liberty II, line 332](./source/source.txt#L332) We are entitled to act on an opinion only because it has been left open to contradiction. [On Liberty II, line 341](./source/source.txt#L341) If the received opinion is true, without contest it is held as a dead dogma and its holder does not know the grounds or even the meaning of it. [On Liberty II, line 386](./source/source.txt#L386) [line 392](./source/source.txt#L392) Most often the two sides share the truth between them. [On Liberty II, line 425](./source/source.txt#L425) The appeal to an opinion's usefulness does not escape, since usefulness is itself a matter of opinion. [On Liberty II, line 353](./source/source.txt#L353) Mill recapitulates the grounds at the end of the chapter and rests them on the mental well-being of mankind. [On Liberty II, line 446](./source/source.txt#L446) [line 449](./source/source.txt#L449)
- **Evidence:** conscientious persecutors, with Marcus Aurelius as the hardest case. [On Liberty II, line 365](./source/source.txt#L365) The history of persecution, which Mill says has usually succeeded. [On Liberty II, line 374](./source/source.txt#L374) In England the effective penalty is social stigma, and its price is moral courage. [On Liberty II, line 380](./source/source.txt#L380)
- **Boundary:** Mill does not claim that truth wins. He calls that a pleasant falsehood. [On Liberty II, line 374](./source/source.txt#L374) Discussion does not cure partisans; it works on the calmer bystander. [On Liberty II, line 440](./source/source.txt#L440) An opinion loses its immunity when its expression is a positive instigation to a mischievous act, and instigation may be punished only where an overt act followed. [On Liberty III, line 487](./source/source.txt#L487) [On Liberty II, line 466](./source/source.txt#L466) Good manners in debate are for opinion to enforce, never law. [On Liberty II, line 458](./source/source.txt#L458) **[Analysis]** The examples are all doctrines of religion, morals and politics. Mill gives no accounting of the harm false opinions do while they circulate, and the chapter does not use the harm principle at all.
- **Application [Application]:** treat the wish to shut down a view as a claim to be infallible, and test your own position by stating the strongest opposing case as its holders would.

> “All silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility.” [On Liberty II, line 332](./source/source.txt#L332)

> “He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that.” [On Liberty II, line 392](./source/source.txt#L392)

### 3. Individuality

- **Author's claim:** in things that do not primarily concern others, a person's own character should rule his conduct. Where custom rules instead, one of the principal ingredients of human happiness, and the chief ingredient of progress, is wanting. [On Liberty III, line 487](./source/source.txt#L487)
- **Reasoning:** the human faculties are exercised only in making a choice, so conformity to custom as custom develops nothing. [On Liberty III, line 493](./source/source.txt#L493) Human nature is a tree that grows, not a machine built to a model. [On Liberty III, line 496](./source/source.txt#L496) People differ in what suits them, and a person's own way of laying out his life is best because it is his own. [On Liberty III, line 526](./source/source.txt#L526) Those who do not want liberty for themselves still gain, because originality needs freedom and liberty is the only unfailing source of improvement. [On Liberty III, line 517](./source/source.txt#L517) [line 535](./source/source.txt#L535)
- **Evidence:** the ascendancy of what Mill calls collective mediocrity in England and America, and China as a talented nation made stationary by custom. [On Liberty III, line 523](./source/source.txt#L523) [line 535](./source/source.txt#L535)
- **Boundary:** actions cannot be as free as opinions, and free scope is limited by injury to others. [On Liberty III, line 487](./source/source.txt#L487) Exceptional people may claim only freedom to point out the way, never power to compel. [On Liberty III, line 523](./source/source.txt#L523) **[Analysis]** Two defences run side by side: individuality as good in itself and individuality as useful. Mill complains that spontaneity is not seen as having intrinsic worth, yet he also says the essay rests on utility. [On Liberty III, line 490](./source/source.txt#L490) The editor's summary keeps only the instrumental reasons. [Commentary, line 1464](./source/source.txt#L1464) The historical claims about China and Europe are asserted in a few sentences.
- **Application [Application]:** when judging a way of life, ask whether it was chosen and what faculties choosing it used, and count variety among lives as a public good.

> “He who does anything because it is the custom, makes no choice.” [On Liberty III, line 493](./source/source.txt#L493)

### 4. Custom is not evidence: the case against women's subordination

- **Author's claim:** the legal subordination of one sex to the other is wrong in itself and a chief hindrance to human improvement, and should be replaced by perfect equality. [Subjection I, line 712](./source/source.txt#L712)
- **Reasoning:** the presumption is for freedom and impartiality, so the burden of proof lies on those who restrict. [Subjection I, line 718](./source/source.txt#L718) Mill accepts that custom may stand unless he shows it arose from causes other than its soundness. [Subjection I, line 721](./source/source.txt#L721) He then argues that it arose from force, which law turned into right. [Subjection I, line 724](./source/source.txt#L724) Every domination has appeared natural to those who held it. [Subjection I, line 736](./source/source.txt#L736) Consent proves little, because women are educated to make submission their ideal. [Subjection I, line 739](./source/source.txt#L739) [line 742](./source/source.txt#L742) No one is entitled to a positive opinion on natural differences, since women have been seen only under subjection. [Subjection I, line 763](./source/source.txt#L763) [line 769](./source/source.txt#L769) And none is needed: what women cannot do by nature need not be forbidden. [Subjection I, line 781](./source/source.txt#L781)
- **Evidence:** the history of slavery, ended in half of America only three or four years before he wrote. [Subjection I, line 733](./source/source.txt#L733) The modern principle that people are no longer born to their place, to which women are the standing exception. [Subjection I, line 748](./source/source.txt#L748) Experience cannot have decided between two courses when only one has been tried. [Subjection I, line 760](./source/source.txt#L760)
- **Boundary:** this is a debunking argument. It removes the presumption in favour of the practice; the positive case is left to the later chapters. **[Analysis]** Mill denies that women's nature can be known and still relies on there being one that free competition will reveal, and in Chapter III he offers conjectures about women as they are while saying that doubt does not forbid conjecture. [Subjection III, line 890](./source/source.txt#L890) The thesis names legal subordination, but the analysis of education reaches much further. The editor draws that consequence: rights can be taken away by social pressure as well as by law. [Commentary, line 1576](./source/source.txt#L1576)
- **Application [Application]:** when a practice is defended as natural, ask whether the alternative has ever been tried and whether those who call it natural benefit from it.

> “What is now called the nature of women is an eminently artificial thing” [Subjection I, line 763](./source/source.txt#L763)

### 5. The utilitarian standard: quality of pleasure and secondary rules

- **Author's claim:** actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, and happiness is pleasure and the absence of pain. The standard is the greatest amount of happiness altogether, not the agent's own. [Utilitarianism II, line 1044](./source/source.txt#L1044) [line 1065](./source/source.txt#L1065)
- **Reasoning:** some kinds of pleasure are more valuable than others, and the test is the decided preference of those who have experience of both. [Utilitarianism II, line 1050](./source/source.txt#L1050) [line 1053](./source/source.txt#L1053) Their verdict is final. [Utilitarianism II, line 1062](./source/source.txt#L1062) Happiness so understood is a mixed and moderate life, and bad education and bad social arrangements are the only real hindrance to its being within reach of almost all. [Utilitarianism II, line 1074](./source/source.txt#L1074) The agent must be as impartial between himself and others as a benevolent spectator. [Utilitarianism II, line 1092](./source/source.txt#L1092) There is no need to calculate before each act: the received rules of morality are mankind's accumulated experience of the tendencies of actions, and the first principle is needed only when those rules conflict. [Utilitarianism II, line 1110](./source/source.txt#L1110) [line 1113](./source/source.txt#L1113)
- **Evidence:** appeals to the reader's own preferences, summed up in the comparison of Socrates and the fool, and the analogy of the sailor who goes to sea with the Nautical Almanack already calculated. [Utilitarianism II, line 1056](./source/source.txt#L1056) [line 1110](./source/source.txt#L1110)
- **Boundary:** the editor finds the chapter imprecise on what makes acts right and reports an unsettled dispute over whether Mill is an act or a rule utilitarian. [Commentary, line 1852](./source/source.txt#L1852) One passage, on abstaining from acts of a class that would be generally injurious, appears to contradict act utilitarianism. [Utilitarianism II, line 1095](./source/source.txt#L1095) [Commentary, line 1940](./source/source.txt#L1940) The use of secondary rules settles nothing, because act utilitarians use them too. [Commentary, line 1955](./source/source.txt#L1955) The editor also doubts that the preference test works for single experiences. [Commentary, line 1904](./source/source.txt#L1904) **[Analysis]** Mill names the ground of the judges' preference a sense of dignity, which is the point where a value other than pleasure may have entered. [Utilitarianism II, line 1056](./source/source.txt#L1056)
- **Application [Application]:** use ordinary moral rules as the default and go back to consequences only when rules conflict or a rule plainly needs revising.

> “It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied.” [Utilitarianism II, line 1056](./source/source.txt#L1056)

> “it is not easy to see just what sort of utilitarian he is.” The editor. [Commentary, line 1852](./source/source.txt#L1852)

### 6. Justice and rights as the most binding part of utility

- **Author's claim:** justice is the part of morality in which some individual can claim the act as his moral right. To have a right is to have something society ought to defend me in possessing, and the only reason it ought is general utility. [Utilitarianism V, line 1255](./source/source.txt#L1255) [line 1285](./source/source.txt#L1285)
- **Reasoning:** we call an act wrong when we think the person ought to be punished for it by law, opinion or conscience. [Utilitarianism V, line 1252](./source/source.txt#L1252) The sentiment of justice is the desire to punish someone who has harmed a definite individual. In itself it has nothing moral in it; what is moral is its subordination to social sympathy. [Utilitarianism V, line 1264](./source/source.txt#L1264) [line 1273](./source/source.txt#L1273) The feeling is so strong because the interest at stake is security, which no one can do without. [Utilitarianism V, line 1285](./source/source.txt#L1285) Justice is a name for the moral rules that concern the essentials of well-being most nearly, among them the rules against hurting one another, wrongful interference with freedom included. [Utilitarianism V, line 1306](./source/source.txt#L1306) [line 1309](./source/source.txt#L1309)
- **Evidence:** rival maxims of justice conflict on punishment, wages and taxation, and each is unanswerable on its own ground, so only social utility can decide. [Utilitarianism V, line 1300](./source/source.txt#L1300) [line 1303](./source/source.txt#L1303) History has been a series of accepted inequalities coming to be seen as injustice, and Mill expects the same of the aristocracies of colour, race and sex. [Utilitarianism V, line 1318](./source/source.txt#L1318)
- **Boundary:** a rule of justice can be overruled in a particular case, as in taking food or medicine by force to save a life. [Utilitarianism V, line 1321](./source/source.txt#L1321) Equal treatment holds except where recognised social expediency requires otherwise. [Utilitarianism V, line 1318](./source/source.txt#L1318) The editor says Mill does not face the modern objection that utility can require injustice, is overoptimistic that the two agree, and takes too readily for granted that violating rights seldom pays. [Commentary, line 2070](./source/source.txt#L2070) [line 2124](./source/source.txt#L2124) He also argues that the link between wrongness and punishment is hard to square with act utilitarianism. [Commentary, line 2091](./source/source.txt#L2091) **[Analysis]** This chapter supplies what On Liberty lacks, an account of how a utilitarian can speak of rights. The harm principle can be read as one of these rules of justice.
- **Application [Application]:** treat a right as a claim to society's protection of a vital interest, and ask of any proposed exception whether it would weaken the security the rule exists to give.

> “To have a right, then, is, I conceive, to have something which society ought to defend me in the possession of.” [Utilitarianism V, line 1285](./source/source.txt#L1285)

### 8. Proof and sanction

- **Author's claim:** questions of ultimate ends admit no direct proof, only considerations that can determine the intellect. [Utilitarianism I, line 1029](./source/source.txt#L1029) The consideration offered is that the sole evidence that anything is desirable is that people desire it. [Utilitarianism IV, line 1177](./source/source.txt#L1177) The motive to obey the standard is the same as for any other: the conscientious feelings of mankind. [Utilitarianism III, line 1144](./source/source.txt#L1144)
- **Reasoning:** three steps. Each person desires his own happiness, so it is a good to him. The general happiness is therefore a good to the aggregate of persons. [Utilitarianism IV, line 1177](./source/source.txt#L1177) And nothing else is desired as an end, because things such as virtue and money, first wanted as means, become parts of happiness. [Utilitarianism IV, line 1183](./source/source.txt#L1183) [line 1186](./source/source.txt#L1186) Desiring a thing and finding it pleasant are, Mill says, inseparable. [Utilitarianism IV, line 1198](./source/source.txt#L1198) On sanctions, conscience is an acquired feeling that can be attached to almost anything, but utility has a natural basis in the desire for unity with others, which grows as society becomes more equal. [Utilitarianism III, line 1153](./source/source.txt#L1153) [line 1159](./source/source.txt#L1159)
- **Evidence:** the love of money as a means that has become an end, and an appeal to the reader's self-observation. [Utilitarianism IV, line 1186](./source/source.txt#L1186) [line 1198](./source/source.txt#L1198)
- **Boundary:** Mill leaves the verdict to the reader. [Utilitarianism IV, line 1204](./source/source.txt#L1204) He warns that the cultivation of social feeling could go so far as to interfere with freedom and individuality. [Utilitarianism III, line 1159](./source/source.txt#L1159) The editor reports that each step has been called a howler and quotes Moore's objection that desirable means what ought to be desired. [Commentary, line 1997](./source/source.txt#L1997) [line 2003](./source/source.txt#L2003) He defends Mill against the charge of a fallacy of composition, citing a letter of 1868, but finds it not very plausible that pleasure is the only thing desired as an end. ## Critical assessment

Everything in this section is **[Analysis]** unless it carries a source link.

- **Strongest contribution:** On Liberty II. The argument does not depend on truth winning, on any theory of rights, or on the harm principle. It needs only that people are fallible and that beliefs held without contest decay. The burden-of-proof method of Subjection I is a close second, because it shows how to argue against a universal custom without claiming knowledge one does not have.
- **Weakest or least-supported claims:** the proof in Utilitarianism IV, which its own editor declines to defend in full. [Commentary, line 2037](./source/source.txt#L2037) The description of the liberty principle as one very simple principle, which the later chapters qualify repeatedly. [On Liberty I, line 296](./source/source.txt#L296) [On Liberty V, line 641](./source/source.txt#L641) And the historical generalisations about stationary societies, which are asserted, not argued. [On Liberty III, line 535](./source/source.txt#L535)
- **Hidden assumptions:** that offence or disgust at another's conduct is not an injury to one's interests. [On Liberty IV, line 594](./source/source.txt#L594) That once legal bars fall, competition will sort people by fitness. [Subjection I, line 781](./source/source.txt#L781) That what competent judges prefer is more pleasant and not better on some other ground. [Utilitarianism II, line 1053](./source/source.txt#L1053) That societies can be ranked by stage of civilization, with liberty owed only to the advanced. [On Liberty I, line 299](./source/source.txt#L299)
- **Meaningful counterargument:** if utility is the only ground, a majority that finds a restriction expedient has an argument Mill must meet case by case, since he allows unequal treatment where recognised social expediency requires it. [Utilitarianism V, line 1318](./source/source.txt#L1318) The editor presses the same point about free expression: it is not hard to devise cases where censorship would seem to promote happiness. [Commentary, line 2124](./source/source.txt#L2124) Schneewind's answer is to loosen the tie, saying Mill's conclusions could be linked as strongly to other moral principles. [Schneewind, line 216](./source/source.txt#L216) That defends the conclusions at the cost of Mill's own claim to have forgone abstract right.
- **Important question left unanswered:** what counts as harm, and which interests ought to be considered rights. On Liberty leaves the first undefined and refers the second to law or tacit understanding. [On Liberty IV, line 567](./source/source.txt#L567) Utilitarianism V gives the principle of an answer, security, but no list. [Utilitarianism V, line 1285](./source/source.txt#L1285)
- **A caution on this edition:** Schneewind attributes to Mill the view that the greatest source of pleasure is the free exercise of our abilities in a society of equals. [Schneewind, line 198](./source/source.txt#L198) That is a synthesis of the three works, not a sentence of Mill's, and the brief does not treat it as one.

## Action layer

All items are **[Application]**, derived from tests the book itself states.

- **Try now:** before supporting a restriction on conduct, ask Mill's questions in order. Does the conduct damage the interests of others, or only offend them? [On Liberty IV, line 567](./source/source.txt#L567) Is there a definite damage or risk to an assignable person or to the public? [On Liberty IV, line 588](./source/source.txt#L588) If so, would interference do more good than harm? [On Liberty V, line 641](./source/source.txt#L641) Could advice, warning or persuasion do the work instead? [On Liberty V, line 638](./source/source.txt#L638)
- **For beliefs:** state the strongest case against a view you hold, in the form its defenders would give it. [On Liberty II, line 392](./source/source.txt#L392)
- **For inherited arrangements:** ask whether the alternative has ever been tried, and place the burden of proof on the restriction. [Subjection I, line 760](./source/source.txt#L760) [line 718](./source/source.txt#L718)
- **Conditions required:** Mill's principle assumes adults of ordinary understanding and a society capable of improvement by free discussion. [On Liberty I, line 299](./source/source.txt#L299) His confidence in individual judgment assumes people know their own circumstances better than others do. [On Liberty IV, line 570](./source/source.txt#L570)
- **Warning signs:** moral rules defended only by the strength of feeling behind them. [On Liberty I, line 290](./source/source.txt#L290) A practice defended as natural by those who gain from it. [Subjection I, line 736](./source/source.txt#L736) Education run to one pattern by whoever holds power. [On Liberty V, line 671](./source/source.txt#L671) Administration that draws all ability into itself. [On Liberty V, line 692](./source/source.txt#L692)
- **Failure modes of the book's own advice:** the tests do not say what counts as harm, and Mill himself departs from them for public decency and for contracts that end future choice. [On Liberty V, line 653](./source/source.txt#L653) [line 665](./source/source.txt#L665)
- **Seven-day experiment:** take one rule you would like to see enforced on other people's private conduct. Write down who is harmed by the conduct, what the harm is, and whether your objection would survive if the conduct were merely unusual. Then write the best case against your own view.

## Reading recommendation

**Read selected chapters.** On Liberty I, II and III; The Subjection of Women I and the opening of III on the suffrage; Utilitarianism II and V. Add the editor's chapter essays on Utilitarianism for the objections, and Schneewind's introduction for the frame. That is roughly half of Mill's text. What this report cannot compress is the movement of On Liberty II, where each objection is stated at full strength before it is answered, and the cumulative effect of Subjection I's parallels between the rule of men over women and other dominations. On Liberty IV and V are best read after the first three chapters, as a record of how many qualifications the principle needs. Utilitarianism I and III and most of Subjection III can be skimmed.
