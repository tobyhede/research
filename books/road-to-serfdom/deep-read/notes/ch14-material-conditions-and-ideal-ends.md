# ch14: Material Conditions and Ideal Ends (pp. 207–220)

Chapter text runs pp. 207–218; footnotes pp. 218–220. Bracketed notes are the editor's (Caldwell's); notes 3, 4, 6 and the first part of note 8 are Hayek's.

## Question this chapter answers

Is it true that the present generation has risen above economic motives and is sacrificing material welfare for higher ideals? Hayek's answer is no on both counts: the generation is not less interested in material welfare, it simply refuses to accept economic constraints ("economophobia") (p. 208); and the drift to collectivism has lowered, not raised, moral standards, because morals exist only where individuals are free and responsible (pp. 213-216). Two subsidiary questions follow: what economic conduct will post-war recovery require (pp. 210-213), and what should the democracies say to the Germans (pp. 217-218)?

## Leading propositions

- The demands for social reconstruction most strongly pressed are almost all economic; people are more than ever governed by economic doctrines, and by false ones: "potential plenty", inevitable monopoly, destruction of stocks, suppression of inventions (p. 208).
- Destruction of stocks and suppression of inventions cannot happen under competition; they are made possible only by monopoly, usually government-aided (p. 208; p. 219).
- What is new is unwillingness to sacrifice any demand to economic argument; man has come to hate and revolt against the impersonal forces to which he formerly submitted (p. 208).
- This is part of a general unwillingness to submit to any rule whose rationale is not understood, "often a commendable attitude", but fatal where intelligibility cannot be had (p. 208).
- A complex civilization necessarily rests on individuals adjusting to changes whose causes they cannot understand; no single mind can grasp the circumstances that determine anyone's income or occupation (pp. 208-209).
- Even a planner could not explain a particular direction of labor or pay without vindicating the whole plan, so it could be explained only to a few (p. 209).
- Submission to the impersonal forces of the market made the growth of civilization possible; it does not matter that the submission once rested on religious humility or exaggerated respect for early economists (p. 209).
- It is much harder to understand rationally why one must submit than to submit from awe (p. 209).
- The only alternative to submitting to the market's impersonal forces is submitting to "an equally uncontrollable and therefore arbitrary power of other men"; the new deliberate restraints will be more painful than the old (p. 209).
- It is right that we lag in using the possibilities of social collaboration, but wrong that we must master social forces as we have mastered natural ones; that is the path to totalitarianism and to the destruction of civilization (pp. 209-210).
- Individual freedom cannot be reconciled with the supremacy of a single purpose; the only exception is war and other temporary disasters (p. 210).
- This applies even to the conquest of unemployment, which "must be the goal of our greatest endeavor" but not "at any price" (p. 210).
- After the war many workers in war trades must move to lower-paid work; some would "under any system" have to accept a relative fall (pp. 210-211).
- If unions prevent relative wage reductions, the alternatives are coercion (compulsory transfer) or unemployment until lower wages are accepted; a socialist society would face the same problem and would use coercion (p. 211).
- Monetary policy can cure this only by a general and considerable inflation, which lowers real wages "in a concealed and underhand fashion" and does more harm than the ill it cures (p. 211).
- Aiming always at the monetary maximum of employment is self-defeating: it requires progressive inflation, holds up reallocation of labor, lowers productivity and increases the share of workers employable only by artificial means (pp. 211-212).
- Post-war Britain will be very poor; recovery needs restrained consumption, renewal of industrial apparatus, best use rather than mere full use of resources, and no attempt to cure poverty by redistribution rather than growth (p. 212).
- The decisive factor in Continental totalitarianism, absent so far in England and America, was a large recently dispossessed middle class (p. 212).
- Hope of avoiding the threatened fate rests largely on resuming rapid economic progress; this requires that no group's accustomed standard obstruct adjustment (p. 212).
- A uniform minimum should be secured to all; with it, all claims to privileged group security and to excluding newcomers must lapse (p. 213).
- "Damn economics, let us build up a decent world" is irresponsible; modern democracy will not bear a substantial peacetime fall in living standards or prolonged stationariness (p. 213).
- Fifty years' approach to collectivism has probably lowered moral standards; stronger indignation at inequity is not matched in individual conduct (p. 213).
- Morals exist only in the sphere of free individual decision and responsibility (pp. 213-214).
- Collectivism's effect on individual conduct has been "almost entirely destructive", inevitably, since its main promise is relief from responsibility (p. 214).
- People have become more tolerant of particular abuses and more indifferent to inequities in individual cases because they look to the state to set everything right (p. 214).
- The passion for collective action may be collective indulgence in selfishness (p. 214).
- Collectivism has nothing to put in place of the individualist virtues except obedience; periodic elections are no test of moral values (pp. 214-215).
- Standards of collective action derive from rules of individual conduct, so they cannot rise while the latter relax (p. 215).
- The values now demoted are all moral: liberty, independence, truth, intellectual honesty, peace, democracy, respect for the individual as a man; what is sacrosanct is the protected standard of this or that group (p. 215).
- Group discrimination, indifference to injustice done by government in a group's interest, and compulsory transfer of populations are increasingly countenanced, even by supposed liberals (p. 215).
- The declining virtues are those in which the Anglo-Saxons (and the Swiss and Dutch) excelled; collectivism's centralizing tendency destroys the institutions that formed them (p. 216).
- Twenty years ago almost all Englishmen were liberals in the broad sense; abroad, Carlyle, Disraeli, the Webbs and Wells are popular among Nazis and totalitarians, while the tradition of Macaulay, Gladstone, Mill and Morley marks kindred spirits (p. 216).
- British propaganda is ineffective because its directors have lost belief in the distinctive values of English civilization (p. 217).
- The moral values on which the Left intelligentsia pride themselves are largely the product of the institutions they want to destroy (p. 217).
- Offering Germans state socialism, Realpolitik, "scientific" planning or corporativism is offering a stale copy of their fathers' ideas; it concedes that the liberals were wrong (p. 217).
- Germans whose values are nearest ours want protection against the monster state and freedom to rebuild "their own little worlds" (p. 218).
- To win the war of ideologies the democracies must regain belief in their traditional values, not apologize or seek compromise between liberal values and totalitarian ideas (p. 218).

## Arguments

**A. Economophobia, not the end of economic man**

- **Premises**: (p. 208) Current demands are almost all economic. (p. 208) Beliefs are governed by (false) economic doctrines. (p. 208) What has changed is refusal to accept that aims conflict or that anything must be given up.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 208) the "End of Economic Man" misdescribes both the past and the trend; the real phenomenon is revolt against economic necessity.

**B. The epistemic argument for submitting to impersonal forces**

- **Premises**: (pp. 208-209) In a complex society the causes of any individual's change of fortune involve more circumstances than any single mind can grasp. (p. 209) Coordination must therefore take account of facts no individual can survey. (p. 209) Civilization grew through submission to market forces that did this. (p. 209) Refusing to submit to what one cannot understand or attribute to a conscious will is an incomplete rationalism.
  **Unstated premises**: (i) the market's outcomes are the only available mechanism that makes use of dispersed facts (the knowledge argument, stated more fully in ch. 4 and elsewhere in Hayek); (ii) the two alternatives are exhaustive, so partial correction of market outcomes is either harmless or a step toward the second; (iii) the "forces" are not themselves the product of alterable legal and institutional choices in any way that matters to the argument.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 209) the only alternative to submission to the market is submission to the arbitrary power of other men, which will be more painful.

**C. No single purpose in peace**

- **Premises**: (p. 210) Freedom is incompatible with permanent subordination of society to one purpose. (p. 210) War is an exception because the sacrifice is temporary and preserves freedom in the long run.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 210) wartime methods cannot be carried into peace; even the conquest of unemployment must not be pursued "at any price".

**D. Post-war wages and the limits of monetary policy**

- **Premises**: (pp. 210-211) War has moved large numbers into trades that cannot employ them afterwards at the same relative wages. (p. 211) If unions prevent relative wage reductions, the options are compulsory transfer or unemployment. (p. 211) Monetary expansion can adjust relative wages only by raising all others, i.e. by a large general inflation that reduces the real wages concerned covertly. (pp. 211-212) A policy of always securing the monetary maximum of employment requires progressive inflation and blocks reallocation, lowering productivity.
  **Unstated premises**: unemployment of the relevant kind is mainly a problem of relative wages and misallocation between industries, not of deficient aggregate demand (Hayek's position against Keynes, assumed, not argued); money wages are rigid downward only because of union resistance.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 211-212) "full employment" pursued by monetary means is self-defeating; if neither unemployment nor coercion is accepted, only "desperate expedients" remain.

**E. Growth as a political necessity**

- **Premises**: (p. 213) Everyone is convinced material conditions must improve. (p. 213) Democracy will not survive a substantial peacetime fall in living standards or long stagnation. (p. 212) Dispossession of a middle class was decisive for Continental totalitarianism. (p. 212) Growth requires rapid adaptation unobstructed by group claims.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 212-213) economic considerations cannot be damned; redistribution in place of growth is dangerous; protected group standards must give way, with a uniform minimum as the only guaranteed security.

**F. Collectivism is destructive of morals**

- **Premises**: (p. 213) Moral value attaches only to free decisions involving sacrifice of one's own interests. (pp. 213-214) Compelled good conduct earns no praise; unselfishness at others' expense is not a virtue. (p. 214) Collectivism's main promise is relief from responsibility. (p. 215) Collective standards derive from individual ones.
  **Unstated premises**: (i) the scope of free moral decision shrinks in proportion as economic decisions are collectivized, i.e. economic freedom is the chief arena of moral choice (asserted as "the sphere where material circumstances force a choice upon us", p. 214); (ii) political participation and support for collective provision are not themselves exercises of moral responsibility (asserted for elections, p. 215).
  **Conclusion**: collectivism "cannot but be antimoral in its effect, however lofty the ideals to which it owes its birth" (p. 214); the moral sense has been blunted (p. 216).

**G. Propaganda and the Germans**

- **Premises**: (p. 217) Germans know what the traditional values of democratic life are. (p. 217) Collectivist ideas are German ideas of a generation ago. (p. 218) Anti-Nazi Germans want protection from the state, not large-scale organization.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 217-218) the democracies can win over the "decent elements" only by defending their own liberal tradition, not by offering a half-way version of the German path.

## Evidence offered

- **Bare assertion (labelled as beyond doubt)**: that men are "more than ever before governed by economic doctrines" (p. 208); "Nor can there be much doubt".
- **Mechanism argument, in a footnote**: in a competitive market no owner of stocks can gain by destroying them (p. 219). On suppressed patents Hayek concedes the case is "more complicated" and cannot be dealt with in a note, then asserts it is "more than doubtful" that it has happened in any important instance (p. 219). Confident conclusion, argument withheld.
- **Mechanism argument (epistemic)**: the impossibility of any single mind surveying the determinants of prices and incomes (pp. 208-209). No example is worked.
- **Historical claim, unsupported**: submission to market forces "made possible the growth of a civilization which without this could not have developed" (p. 209).
- **Mechanism argument (economic)**: relative wages, compulsory transfer versus unemployment, inflation as concealed real-wage cut, self-defeating monetary maximum (pp. 211-212). No data; the claim that the policy is "certain in the end to defeat its own purposes" (p. 212) is stated as certainty on theoretical grounds only.
- **Prediction**: Britain will be "very poor indeed" after the war but can recover in "a few years" by restraint and investment (p. 212).
- **Historical generalization**: the dispossessed middle class as the "one decisive factor" in Continental totalitarianism (p. 212). Stated without support here; note that elsewhere in the book the decisive factor is said to be ideas, so "one decisive factor" overstates.
- **Bare assertion**: democracy will not bear falling or stationary living standards (p. 213).
- **Conceptual argument plus authority**: the definition of morals, supported by a Milton quotation from Areopagitica (pp. 213-214; p. 219).
- **Rhetorical questions in place of evidence**: "Can there be much doubt" that personal obligation has weakened (p. 214); the run of questions about which values now rank lower (p. 215). The claim of moral decline is hedged at first ("more than doubtful", "by no means clear", p. 213) and then stated flatly ("both inevitable and undeniable", p. 214). This is the clearest case in the chapter of confident language outrunning evidence.
- **Quotation from an opponent**: Acland's "The Forward March" on the community telling the individual not to bother about getting a living, deciding employment, running "camps for shirkers", and on Hitler having "stumbled across" part of what humanity will require (p. 219). Four page references are strung together in one sentence; treat as a composite. No editorial correction is offered for these.
- **Contemporary examples asserted**: closed groups' right to exclude, callous indifference to government injustice, "compulsory transfer of populations" countenanced by supposed liberals, atrocities condoned by "so-called 'liberals'" (pp. 215-216). No names or instances.
- **Personal testimony**: Hayek as one who "must forever remain a foreigner" observing English contempt for English values (p. 216).
- **Literary allusion**: Wordsworth's lines on Shakespeare and Milton; the editor gives the source and the full wording, which differs slightly from Hayek's rendering (p. 220). Milton's "precedence of teaching nations how to live" (p. 220). Ezra Pound's broadcasting from Italy is used as a guilt-by-association aside against "detractors of Milton" (p. 220).
- **Assertion about German opinion**: what Germans know and want (pp. 217-218). No source; presumably Hayek's own acquaintance.
- **Editor's note on Drucker** (pp. 218-219): Drucker argued fascism arose from the failure of both capitalism and socialism to deliver freedom and equality in the economic sphere, and recommended that the democracies create their own "noneconomic societies" retaining individual freedom. So Hayek's target is a prescriptive thesis as well as a descriptive one; Hayek addresses only the slogan.

## Quotes

- "Man has come to hate, and to revolt against, the impersonal forces to which in the past he submitted, even though they have often frustrated his individual efforts." (p. 208)
- "the only alternative to submission to the impersonal and seemingly irrational forces of the market is submission to an equally uncontrollable and therefore arbitrary power of other men" (p. 209)
- "The only exception to the rule that a free society must not be subjected to a single purpose is war and other temporary disasters when subordination of almost everything to the immediate and pressing need is the price at which we preserve our freedom in the long run." (p. 210)
- "to aim always at the maximum of employment achievable by monetary means is a policy which is certain in the end to defeat its own purposes" (p. 212)
- "The one thing modern democracy will not bear without cracking is the necessity of a substantial lowering of the standards of living in peacetime or even prolonged stationariness of its economic conditions." (p. 213)
- "Only where we ourselves are responsible for our own interests and are free to sacrifice them has our decision moral value. We are neither entitled to be unselfish at someone else’s expense nor is there any merit in" (p. 213)
- "Collectivism has nothing to put in their place, and in so far as it already has destroyed them it has left a void filled" (p. 214)
- Hayek's footnote on demobilization: "Nothing would discredit the system of free enterprise more than the acute, though probably short-lived, dislocation and instability such an attempt would produce." (p. 219)

## Concessions and qualifications

- The unwillingness to submit to rules not understood is "often a commendable attitude" (p. 208).
- The impersonal forces "have often frustrated his individual efforts" and are "seemingly irrational" (pp. 208, 209).
- Critics are "quite right" that we are behind in using the possibilities of social collaboration (pp. 209-210).
- War and "other temporary disasters" justify subordinating almost everything to one purpose; it is "sensible temporarily to sacrifice freedom" (p. 210). **State authority accepted**: wartime direction of the economy, on condition that it is temporary.
- The conquest of unemployment "must be the goal of our greatest endeavor" (p. 210).
- Retraining "certainly ought to be provided on a liberal scale" (p. 211). **State action accepted.**
- Some unemployment is the unavoidable accompaniment of free choice of jobs (p. 212).
- "Let a uniform minimum be secured to everybody by all means" (p. 213). **State action accepted**, with the condition that group privileges and the right to exclude newcomers lapse.
- Wartime restrictions cannot be removed "at one stroke"; a "carefully thought-out policy of gradual relaxation of controls", possibly over several years, is required (p. 219). **State authority accepted transitionally.**
- The present generation "probably surpasses most of its predecessors" in indignation about inequities (p. 213).
- Collectivism owes its birth to "lofty" ideals (p. 214).
- Every generation reorders its values (p. 215).
- The Swiss and Dutch share the individualist virtues (p. 216).
- Recent improvements in social institutions exist but "count but little" beside the basic difference of two ways of life (p. 218).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The "reinterpretation in economic terms" of liberty, equality and security (p. 208) refers back to Carr in ch. 13 (p. 194) and to ch. 2 and ch. 9.
- "Potential plenty" and inevitable monopoly (p. 208) repeat ch. 4 and ch. 13 (pp. 194, 197); here they are simply called false.
- The epistemic argument (pp. 208-209) is the fullest statement in these three chapters of why "impersonal forces" deserve deference; it supports the bare dichotomy of ch. 13 (p. 201) and repeats it in almost the same form (p. 209). It should be read with ch. 4 (complexity and the price system) and ch. 7.
- Tension, my observation: Hayek asks for submission to forces that cannot be understood, and admits submission was historically sustained by religion or deference to economists (p. 209). Yet the book's appeal is to reasoned persuasion of a public. He concedes it is "infinitely more difficult rationally to comprehend the necessity" (p. 209). Synthesis should ask whether his position needs a non-rational support he does not supply. Also compare with ch. 13's hostility to treating "fact" as the standard of value in Carr: submission to market outcomes because they are the outcome of impersonal forces can look like a similar deference to what is.
- Tension with ch. 13 and ch. 12: the "one decisive factor" in Continental totalitarianism is here a dispossessed middle class (p. 212), a material and sociological cause; chs. 12–13 make ideas the cause. Hayek does not reconcile them. This also sits awkwardly with the chapter's thesis that ideal ends, not material conditions, are what matter.
- The single-purpose passage (p. 210) depends on ch. 5 (no agreed complete ethical code) and ch. 6; the war exception matches ch. 13's footnote on wartime controllers (p. 206).
- The employment passage (pp. 210-212) is the book's main statement on macroeconomic policy and is narrower than ch. 9's acceptance of combating general fluctuations; check ch. 9 for what monetary and public-works measures he allows. Here he rejects only the policy of the monetary maximum and "at any price".
- The uniform minimum (p. 213) restates ch. 9's first kind of security and its condition that it not become security of a particular standard.
- The definition of morals (pp. 213-214) underlies ch. 10 (collectivist ethics) and ch. 11, and extends ch. 6: as the Rule of Law leaves a protected sphere of decision, morals need the same sphere. It also echoes ch. 13's defence of "abstract general principles" (p. 193).
- Tension, my observation: morals require freedom to sacrifice one's own interests (p. 213), yet the economic argument requires individuals to "obey the necessities of this readjustment" (pp. 212-213) and to "submit". Submission to market necessity is treated as compatible with moral freedom, submission to political decision is not. The asymmetry rests on the impersonal/personal distinction and needs testing.
- The virtues list includes "respect for custom and tradition" (p. 216), which sits beside ch. 13's dismissal of the "traditionalist forces of the Right" (p. 201) and anticipates Hayek's later essay on why he is not a conservative; flag for thematic synthesis on liberalism versus conservatism.
- "Compulsory transfer of populations" and discrimination between nationals (p. 215) lead into ch. 15's treatment of nationalism and international order.
- The propaganda section (pp. 217-218) links to ch. 13's thesis of English imitation of German ideas and to ch. 15's "not grandiose schemes" theme: "organization on a colossal scale" versus "their own little worlds" (p. 218).
- The Milton epigraph (p. 207) asserts that a minority may rightfully compel a majority to "retain" liberty; this links to ch. 5's point that democracy is a means and not an end. Hayek does not comment on the epigraph, and its anti-majoritarian implication is not argued in the chapter.

## Open questions

- Is the dichotomy on p. 209 (market forces or arbitrary power of men) defensible given Hayek's own acceptance of a minimum income, retraining, regulated monopoly (ch. 13) and gradual decontrol? What principle marks permitted correction from the beginning of "mastering the forces of society"?
- Does Hayek's account of unemployment (relative wages, misallocation) survive comparison with the demand-deficiency account he does not address? Note he grants a short-run monetary maximum exists (p. 211) but says it is unsustainable.
- Is the claim that democracy cannot survive stagnation or falling standards (p. 213) consistent with asking people to accept lower relative positions and restrained consumption (pp. 211-212)? He seems to need both acceptance of individual loss and aggregate growth.
- Is the definition of morals (pp. 213-214) too narrow? It excludes by definition any moral worth in collectively organized provision and treats voting as morally empty (p. 215). Synthesis should compare with opponents' view that institutions can embody moral commitments.
- What is the evidence for moral decline over fifty years (pp. 213-216)? None is given beyond examples without instances. Is the claim falsifiable?
- "Inevitable and undeniable" (p. 214) versus the book's repeated rejection of inevitability: is this conceptual necessity (from the definition) or prediction?
- How does the national-character argument (Anglo-Saxon virtues, pp. 216-217) square with Hayek's rejection in ch. 12 of explanations of Nazism from German national character? He appears to hold that institutions and ideas form character (p. 216), which would reconcile them; check ch. 12.
- Who are the "supposed liberals" who countenance population transfers and condone atrocities (pp. 215-216)? Unnamed; the reference to "powers with whose professed principles they sympathize" presumably means the Soviet Union, but Hayek does not say so.
