# ch01: The Abandoned Road (pp. 63–73)

Scope note: the chapter text runs from the heading on p. 63 to p. 71; footnotes run pp. 71-73. Unbracketed footnotes are Hayek's; bracketed ones are the editor's (Caldwell).

## Question this chapter answers

What was the road Western civilisation was on, why was it abandoned, and in what sense is the modern trend toward socialism a reversal of direction and not a continuation of progress?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| individualism | p. 65 | Respect for the individual man as man: recognition of his own views and tastes as supreme in his own sphere, however narrowly circumscribed, plus the belief that men should develop their own gifts and bents. Explicitly not egotism or selfishness. Rooted in Christianity and classical antiquity, fully developed in the Renaissance. Contrast term: socialism and 'all other forms of collectivism'. |
| freedom / liberty | p. 65 | Words 'so worn with use and abuse' that Hayek hesitates to use them; 'tolerance' is offered as the one word still carrying the full meaning. Historically: freeing the individual from ties binding him to customary or prescribed ways in his ordinary activities (p. 66). |
| economic freedom / freedom in economic affairs | p. 65 | The freedom 'without which personal and political freedom has never existed in the past'. Historically an undesigned by-product of political freedom (p. 66); also called industrial freedom or liberty (p. 66) (p. 68). |
| liberalism | p. 67 | Not a fixed creed. Its fundamental principle: in ordering our affairs, make as much use as possible of the spontaneous forces of society and resort as little as possible to coercion. Compatible with, and requiring, deliberate creation of a framework in which competition works beneficially. |
| laissez faire | p. 64, p. 68 | A rough rule of thumb, wrongly presented by some liberals as an exceptionless rule; distinguished from liberalism proper and blamed for much of the harm to the liberal cause. |
| spontaneous forces (of society) | p. 67 | The uncontrolled efforts of individuals which produce a complex order nobody designed (p. 66); what liberal policy assists and supplements. |
| collectivism | p. 65 | Genus of which socialism is one form; used here without definition as the opposite of individualism defined in ch. 3 (p. 81). |
| socialism | p. 65 | The destination of the modern trend; equated, via Tocqueville and Acton, with slavery. Still undefined. |
| 'conscious' direction / planning | p. 69 | Replacing the impersonal, anonymous market mechanism by collective direction of all social forces to deliberately chosen goals. First statement of what will be defined as planning in ch. 3. |
| 'positive' action | p. 68 | Deliberate use of government powers to improve the institutional framework (monetary system, monopoly, and other tasks); liberalism was slow at it and so came to be seen as a 'negative' creed. |
| 'Western' (German sense) | p. 71 | West of the Rhine: liberalism, democracy, capitalism, individualism, free trade, internationalism, love of peace, as despised by German thinkers before the Nazis. |

## Leading propositions

- When civilisation takes an unexpected turn toward barbarism, people blame anything except their own ideals; the one explanation refused is that the pursuit of cherished ideals has produced results utterly different from those expected (p. 63).
- The war grew out of a struggle of ideas inside a common European civilisation; the tendencies that ended in totalitarianism were not confined to the countries that succumbed (pp. 63-64).
- A change of ideas and the force of human will, not any spontaneous change in the facts, made the world what it is (p. 64).
- England and America lagged behind, but by 1931 had already moved so far that only those who remember the years before 1914 know what a liberal world was like (p. 64).
- The change is one of direction, not just magnitude: for at least twenty-five years before totalitarianism became a threat, the West had been moving away from its basic ideas (p. 64).
- Freedom in economic affairs has been progressively abandoned, and without it personal and political freedom has never existed in the past (p. 65).
- Tocqueville and Acton warned that socialism means slavery (p. 65).
- The trend toward socialism breaks not only with nineteenth-century liberalism but with the individualism inherited from Christianity, Greece, Rome and the Renaissance (p. 65).
- The National Socialist revolution was a counter-Renaissance, the decisive step in destroying an individualist civilisation (p. 65).
- The growth of individual freedom is closely associated with the growth of commerce, spreading from northern Italy to the Low Countries and Britain wherever no despotic power stifled it (p. 66).
- The argument for economic freedom came after the fact: free economic activity was the undesigned and unforeseen by-product of political freedom (p. 66).
- The growth of science followed individual liberty; inventiveness existed earlier but was suppressed where dominant views were binding on all (pp. 66-67).
- The material result surpassed all expectations; probably no class failed to benefit substantially, and by 1900 the Western workingman had comfort, security and independence scarcely thought possible a century before (p. 67).
- Success bred ambition and impatience; the principles that made progress possible came to be seen as obstacles to faster progress (p. 67).
- Liberalism is not a stationary creed; there is 'all the difference' between deliberately creating a system in which competition works beneficially and passively accepting institutions as they are (p. 67).
- The wooden insistence on laissez faire did great harm to the liberal cause, though it was in a sense unavoidable as a defence against special interests (pp. 67-68).
- The liberal's attitude to society is the gardener's: he must understand the plant to create conditions for its growth (p. 68).
- The nineteenth-century rules were only a beginning; governments possess enormous powers for good and evil in money, monopoly and many other fields, which better understanding would let us use successfully (p. 68).
- The very success of liberalism became the cause of its decline (pp. 68-69).
- Toward the turn of the century belief in liberal tenets was relinquished in favour of a complete remodelling of society, and understanding of how the free system worked declined with it (p. 69).
- The change was fostered by transferring the habits of thought of the natural scientist and engineer to social problems (p. 69).
- The dominant view now is to replace the impersonal market mechanism by collective, 'conscious' direction of all social forces to deliberately chosen goals (p. 69).
- From about 1870 the flow of ideas reversed: English ideas stopped spreading east and German ideas spread east and west for sixty years (p. 70).
- Socialism did not originate in Germany but was perfected there (p. 70).
- The West went on importing German ideas despite, or because of, German contempt for 'Western' ideals, and came to believe its own convictions were rationalisations of selfish interest (p. 71).

## Arguments

- **The self-inflicted-crisis argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 64) the pre-totalitarian history of Germany, Italy and Russia shows few features unfamiliar to us; (p. 64) for a generation the democracies have been guided by the same ideals, which have already transformed them; (p. 64) no change in the facts compelled the change of ideas; (unstated) the ideals themselves, not the special circumstances of those countries, are what produced totalitarianism there.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 63) the present state of the world may be the result of genuine error on our own part, and (p. 64) winning the war only buys another chance to face the basic problems.

- **Economic freedom as a condition of other freedoms.**
  **Premises**: (p. 65) personal and political freedom has never existed in the past without freedom in economic affairs; (p. 65) we have progressively abandoned economic freedom; (p. 65) a new form of slavery has arisen before our eyes; (unstated) what has never existed without a condition cannot exist without it, that is, the historical correlation is a necessary connection.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 65) the abandonment of economic freedom and the new slavery are connected, as Tocqueville and Acton warned. The chapter gives the historical correlation only; the mechanism is left to chs. 5-9.

- **Civilisation-as-individualism argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 65) the essential feature of Western civilisation since the Renaissance is individualism; (p. 66) its spread tracked commerce and the absence of despotic power; (pp. 66-67) science and material progress followed the unchaining of individual energies and were stifled where majority belief bound everyone; (p. 67) the results surpassed all expectations and benefited every class; (unstated) the correlation of freedom with progress is causal and would hold in future.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 69) the present trend is 'an entire abandonment of the individualist tradition which has created Western civilization', so what is at stake is the source of the progress people take for granted.

- **Why liberalism declined (an internal diagnosis).**
  **Premises**: (pp. 67-68) liberals hardened a strong presumption into an exceptionless rule, laissez faire, so that one breach brought down the whole position; (p. 68) improvement of the institutional framework was necessarily slow because it depended on growing understanding; (p. 68) liberalism could offer individuals only a share in general progress and so looked 'negative'; (p. 69) some used liberal phraseology to defend antisocial privileges; (p. 69) success made remaining evils seem both unbearable and unnecessary.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 68-69) the success of liberalism caused its decline; the abandonment was a mistake of impatience, not a refutation.

- **Reversal, not continuation.**
  **Premises**: (p. 69) each step looked like a difference of degree; (p. 69) cumulatively the question has changed from how to use spontaneous forces to how to replace them by conscious direction; (p. 70) Mannheim says outright that mankind is tending to regulate the whole of its social life.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 69) the change is a complete reversal of the trend of modern European history.

- **German-provenance argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 70) from about 1870 Germany became the centre from which ideas spread; (p. 70) Germany led in socialist theory and practice by a generation; (p. 71) German thinkers themselves set their ideas against 'Western' liberalism and democracy; (unstated) ideas carry their political consequences with them when transplanted.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 70-71) the collectivist ideas now dominant in England and America are imports from the tradition that produced the totalitarian regimes.

## Evidence offered

- Epigraph from F. D. Roosevelt on free enterprise having 'not yet been tried' (p. 63). Kind: quotation used as a motto. Editor's note 1 gives the source (message to Congress on monopolies, 29 April 1938) and its context: Roosevelt was attacking concentrated corporate power as 'collectivism' and calling for more federal regulation of business to restore a competitive order (p. 71). My observation: the epigraph fits Hayek's own framework-for-competition liberalism better than a laissez-faire reading would suggest.
- Macmillan Report of 1931 on Parliament's growing regulation of the day-to-day affairs of the community in Hayek's note 2 (p. 71). Kind: quotation from an official document; the only documentary evidence in the chapter for the claim that England had already left the liberal road. Editor supplies the reference (Cmd. 3897, its pages 4 and 4-5) (p. 71). Hayek adds that in 'the inglorious years 1931–39' England transformed its economic system beyond recognition (p. 71), asserted without detail.
- Tocqueville and Acton as authorities that socialism means slavery (p. 65). Kind: appeal to authority, no passage quoted here; editor's note 3 points to the notes on the 1956 foreword (p. 71).
- Belloc's *The Servile State* on socialist doctrine producing a servile state in Hayek's note 4 (pp. 71-72). Kind: quotation from a predecessor; editor supplies edition and page (p. 72).
- 'The Nazi leader' who called the Nazi revolution a counter-Renaissance (p. 65). Kind: quotation from an opponent, unnamed and unsourced; the editor adds no identification.
- Historical narrative of commerce and freedom spreading from northern Italy to the Low Countries and Britain (p. 66). Kind: broad historical sketch, no sources. Hayek's note 8 adds the subjection of the German bourgeoisie by the territorial princes in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries as 'the most fateful' setback (p. 72); the editor suggests readers would see analogies with the hyperinflation's destruction of middle-class savings and with the kulaks (pp. 72-73). That analogy is the editor's, not Hayek's.
- Automatic toys, mining and watch-making as proof that inventiveness predated industrial freedom but was suppressed (p. 66). Kind: historical illustration, unsourced.
- Comte on 'the revolt of the individual against the species' (p. 67). Kind: quotation from an opponent; editor supplies the citation (p. 73).
- Rise in the workingman's condition by 1900 (p. 67). Kind: bare assertion in confident language ('there can be no doubt'), hedged once ('probably no class').
- Mannheim on regulating the whole of social life (p. 70). Kind: quotation from an opponent, cited by Hayek to *Man and Society*, pp. 175-176 (p. 73). Hayek himself calls it 'the extreme position' (pp. 69-70), so it is evidence of the limiting case, not of typical opinion.
- The 1870 turning point and the eastward retreat of English ideas (p. 70). Kind: periodisation by assertion, hedged ('probably').
- German socialist party strength and doctrinal lead (p. 70). Kind: historical fact stated generally; editor's note 14 points to Howard and King and to Hayek's own *Collectivist Economic Planning* (p. 73).
- Where language outruns evidence: 'has never existed in the past' (p. 65) is a universal historical claim offered without a survey; 'socialism means slavery' (p. 65) rests on two named authorities; 'entire abandonment' and 'complete reversal' (p. 69) describe a climate of opinion that the Introduction itself says is still 'remote' from Germany's condition (p. 57).

Concessions and qualifications recorded (state action accepted):
- Liberalism has 'no hard-and-fast rules fixed once and for all' (p. 67).
- Deliberately creating a system within which competition works as beneficially as possible is distinguished from, and preferred to, passive acceptance of existing institutions (p. 67).
- Laissez faire is a 'rough rule of thumb' and its wooden defence harmed the cause (pp. 67-68).
- The task of policy is 'to assist, and where necessary to supplement' the operation of social forces (p. 68).
- Named 'obvious tasks' for government: handling of the monetary system, and the prevention or control of monopoly; plus 'an even greater number' of less obvious tasks in other fields (p. 68).
- Governments possess 'enormous powers for good and evil', and there was every reason to expect they could one day be used successfully (p. 68).
- Rising standards revealed 'very dark spots in society' that men were rightly no longer willing to tolerate (p. 67); 'man had every right to be ambitious' (p. 67).
- The irritation with those who used liberal phraseology to defend antisocial privileges was 'just' (p. 69).
- The Anglo-Saxon belief that they were until recently governed by laissez faire has 'some justification' relative to other countries (p. 64).
- Most of the new ideas, socialism in particular, did not originate in Germany (p. 70).

Editor versus author in the notes:
- Note 1 (editor): context for the Roosevelt epigraph, and the remark that Hayek was then more hopeful about the United States than about Britain (p. 71).
- Note 7 (editor): Hayek's rejection of the individualism-egoism link is developed in 'Individualism: True and False' (p. 72).
- Note 10 (editor): the success-caused-decline argument repeats 'The Trend of Economic Thinking' (p. 73).
- Note 11 (Hayek): refers to his own 'Scientism and the Study of Society' and 'The Counter-Revolution of Science' for the engineering-mentality thesis (p. 73).
- Note 13 (editor): says Hayek would view Sombart's move from left socialism to fascist anticapitalism as exemplifying a natural tendency (p. 73).
- No editorial correction of a Hayek quotation in this chapter; the editor supplies missing references for the Macmillan Report, Belloc and Comte.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Direction of dependence between freedoms (my observation): on p. 65 economic freedom is the condition of personal and political freedom; on p. 66 economic freedom arose as a by-product of political freedom. The two can be reconciled as historical origin versus continuing condition, but Hayek does not reconcile them, and the causal claim that carries the book (lose economic freedom, lose the rest) is the p. 65 one. Tested in ch. 7.
- 'Freedom' is declared too worn to use and replaced by 'tolerance' (p. 65); ch. 2 then makes the definition of freedom the central issue (pp. 74-75). The individualism of p. 65 is restated in ch. 3 as respect for individual ends within a framework.
- The framework-versus-laissez-faire distinction (pp. 67-68) is the seed of ch. 3's list of accepted state activity (pp. 83-85). The gardener image (p. 68) should be compared with the 'planning for competition' formula at p. 87.
- 'Conscious direction to deliberately chosen goals' (p. 69) is the proto-definition of planning made precise at p. 82.
- The engineering habit of mind (p. 69) returns in ch. 4 as the technical specialist's idealism (pp. 94-96).
- The German-provenance thesis (pp. 70-71) continues the Introduction (pp. 57-58) (pp. 61-62) and is the frame for ch. 12.
- Mannheim (p. 70) is flagged as a recurring target ('more than once').
- Tension with the Introduction: there the democracies are 'still so remote' from German conditions (p. 57); here England 'transformed its economic system beyond recognition' in 1931-39 (p. 71). Synthesis should note that Hayek treats 1930s British protection and cartelisation as already a decisive departure.
- The 'success of liberalism caused its decline' thesis (pp. 68-69) implies the decline had no good reasons; yet Hayek concedes real 'dark spots' (p. 67) and privileges defended in liberal language (p. 69). How much of the critique of nineteenth-century liberalism does he grant?

## Open questions

- Is the claim that political freedom has never existed without economic freedom (p. 65) an empirical generalisation or a conceptual claim? What counts as 'freedom in economic affairs' given the list of legitimate government tasks on p. 68?
- If liberalism has no fixed rules and government has 'enormous powers for good' (p. 68), what principle separates legitimate framework-building from the 'conscious direction' he condemns (p. 69)? The chapter gives the gardener metaphor, not a criterion; chs. 3 and 6 must supply it.
- The historical sketch makes commerce and freedom travel together (p. 66). Does the book ever consider cases of commercial growth under despotic power, or freedom lost under liberal economic policy?
- Is 'socialism means slavery' (p. 65) Tocqueville's and Acton's claim in their own terms? Check the editor's notes to the 1956 foreword.
- Is Mannheim representative? Hayek calls him extreme (p. 69). How much of the book's target is the extreme position?
- The 1870 date and the 'sixty years' of German leadership (p. 70): what does Hayek think changed in 1870, and does ch. 12 argue it?
