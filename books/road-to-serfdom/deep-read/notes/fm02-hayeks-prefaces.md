## Question this chapter answers

What does Hayek himself, at three dates (1944, 1956, 1976), say the book was for, whom it addressed, how it was received, what experience since publication showed, and what he would concede, retract or re-emphasise?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| political book | p. 37 | Hayek 1944: a book whose whole argument is derived from certain ultimate values, as opposed to a neutral scientific treatise or an essay in social philosophy. |

## Leading propositions

**Epigraphs and dedication (Hayek's choice, 1944)**

- The Hume epigraph says liberty is seldom lost all at once; the Tocqueville epigraph professes love of freedom to the point of worship in the present time; the dedication is to the socialists of all parties (p. 37). (My note: the Hume line frames the thesis as one about gradual loss, not sudden seizure.)

**Preface to the original editions (Hayek, 1944)**

- This is a political book, not to be disguised as social philosophy; everything in it derives from certain ultimate values, which the book itself is meant to make clear (p. 37).
- The beliefs are not determined by his personal interests; as an economist he would hold a more important position in the society he opposes (p. 38).
- The views he opposes are the very ones he held as a young man and which led him into economics (p. 38).
- He had every reason not to write it: it will offend friends, it displaced work he values more, and it will prejudice the reception of his academic work (p. 38).
- He wrote it as a duty because most economists had been absorbed by the war machine and silenced by official positions, leaving public opinion on post-war economic policy to amateurs and cranks (p. 38).
- The central argument was first sketched in the 1938 Contemporary Review article and the 1939 Chicago pamphlet, both titled Freedom and the Economic System (p. 38; Caldwell's note 2, p. 39).

**Foreword to the 1956 American paperback edition (Hayek, 1956)**

- Aim and audience: written in England in wartime, almost exclusively for English readers, and mainly for a special class, his left-inclined friends and colleagues; it continued a decade of arguments with them (p. 39).
- Occasion: English opinion, especially among holders of advanced views, completely misconceived the nature of the totalitarian movements, and England was likely to try after the war the kind of policies he believed had helped destroy liberty elsewhere (p. 39).
- Because Russia was a wartime ally he restrained his comments on it and chose illustrations mainly from Germany (pp. 39-40).
- Reception in England: taken in the spirit in which it was written and seriously examined by its addressees, apart from some Labour leaders who attacked it as the work of a foreigner (p. 40).
- Reception in the United States: rejected by the first three publishers approached; then sold at an almost unprecedented rate; drew lavish praise and passionate hatred; its intended addressees rejected it out of hand without examining the argument (p. 40).
- Some of the welcome came from people he doubts ever read it, and the way it was used reminded him of Acton's warning about freedom's auxiliaries whose objects differ from one's own (pp. 40-41).
- The transatlantic difference is explained by intellectual situation, not temperament: in Europe the ideals had long come down to earth and disillusion had begun; in the United States they were ten or fifteen years old and still unsoiled by practical experience (p. 41).
- The claim that the political development of Germany, Italy, Russia and Japan had something to do with their economic policies was indignantly rejected by American planners (pp. 41-42).
- By 1956 the main conclusions are widely accepted: that fascism and communism are variants of the same totalitarianism which central control of all economic activity tends to produce is almost a commonplace, and democratic socialism is recognised as precarious and unstable (p. 42).
- Events and more popular works did more than this book to produce that sobered mood; Hayek's note names Orwell's 1984 as the most effective (p. 42; note 13, p. 51).
- The general thesis was not original; the book's merit is its detailed examination of why planning produces unlooked-for results and by what process (p. 42).
- Hot socialism is nearly dead in the Western world, and an argument applicable only against it might look like tilting at windmills (p. 42).
- But its conceptions have penetrated current thought: many believe in measures which, though not designed to remodel the economy, may in aggregate unintentionally do so (p. 43).
- The Welfare State needs careful sorting out if its results are not to be very similar to those of full socialism; some of its aims are practicable and laudable (p. 43).
- The danger is in the choice of instruments: administrative coercion and discrimination where modified general rules of law would serve, and direct state controls or monopolistic institutions where financial inducements would serve (p. 43).
- Because future ideology will aim at piecemeal change, what matters is understanding which kinds of measures paralyse the driving forces of a free society (p. 43).
- The book is not an exhortation to resist all improvement or experiment; it is a warning that changes should pass the tests set out in the chapter on the Rule of Law before commitments are made from which withdrawal may be difficult (p. 43).
- True liberalism is distinct from conservatism; the liberal must sometimes make common cause with conservatives, but confusing the two is dangerous (p. 44).
- Conservatism is often closer to socialism than to liberalism in its paternalistic, nationalistic and power-adoring tendencies, and is bound to defend established privilege (p. 44).
- He leaves the text unchanged although there are many points he would explain at greater length, state more cautiously, or fortify with more illustration and proof; brevity is its greatest merit (p. 44).
- What he wants to add belongs in separate studies: Individualism and Economic Order, The Counter-Revolution of Science, and a coming treatment of equality and justice (pp. 44-45; notes 15-17, pp. 51-52).
- Six years of Labour government strengthened his concern and taught many the reality of the difficulties he had pointed out (p. 45).
- The inherent logic of its policies drove an unwilling socialist government into coercion it disliked: the 1947 Economic Survey disavowed compulsion, and six months later peacetime conscription of labour was back on the statute book (pp. 45-46).
- Six years of socialist government did not produce anything resembling a totalitarian state; this does not refute the book, because its main point is a slow psychological change in the character of the people (p. 46).
- Political ideals and attitudes to authority are as much effect as cause of institutions, so a strong tradition of liberty is no safeguard (p. 46).
- The consequences can be averted if the spirit of liberty reasserts itself in time and the people recognise the danger and change course; there is not yet much ground to believe this has happened in England (p. 46).
- The character of the British people has already changed, under Labour and over the longer period of a paternalistic welfare state (p. 46).
- Tocqueville did not consider how long such a government would stay in the hands of benevolent despots when ruffians could more easily keep power (pp. 47-48).
- He never accused socialist parties of deliberately aiming at totalitarianism; the claim is that the unforeseen but inevitable consequences of socialist planning create a situation in which, if the policy is pursued, totalitarian forces get the upper hand (p. 48).
- The British socialists' democratic inhibitions seemed weaker than those of the German Social Democrats of the 1920s, who never came as close to totalitarian planning (p. 48).
- The most serious development is the growth of arbitrary administrative coercion and the progressive destruction of the Rule of Law, to the point where it is doubtful that it still prevails in Britain (p. 48).
- The paragraph with the extraction artifact (p. 49), paraphrased: it now seems unlikely that a future Labour government would resume the experiments in large-scale nationalization and planning.
- The defeat of systematic socialism has given only a breathing space; without a revised conception of social aims the drift continues in the direction in which outright socialism would only have gone a little faster (p. 49).

**Preface to the 1976 edition (Hayek, 1976)**

- The book was written in spare time from 1940 to 1943 while his mind was mainly on pure economic theory, and became the starting point of thirty years' work in a new field (p. 53).
- Its cause was annoyance at the misinterpretation of the Nazi movement in English progressive circles; the line runs from a memorandum to Beveridge, through the 1938 article and the Chicago pamphlet, to this tract for the times (p. 53).
- For a long time he was not happy about it: fellow social scientists made him feel he had used his abilities on the wrong side, and he worried he had exceeded his competence (p. 54).
- In 1944 he had not sufficiently freed himself from the prejudices and superstitions of general opinion, nor from the prevalent confusions of terms and concepts (p. 54).
- The book's account of the consequences of socialist policies is incomplete without an account of what a properly run market order requires and can achieve; The Constitution of Liberty (1960) and Law, Legislation, and Liberty were written to supply it (p. 54).
- On rereading he is no longer apologetic but proud of it, and recommends it to the general reader as a simple, nontechnical introduction (pp. 54-55).
- He still defends all the main conclusions, on the whole (p. 55).
- The chief qualification is terminological: socialism then meant nationalization and central planning; it has come to mean redistribution through taxation and the welfare state (p. 55).
- By the old meaning Sweden is much less socialist than Great Britain or Austria, though commonly thought more so (p. 55).
- Under the newer kind of socialism the effects discussed in the book come about more slowly, indirectly and imperfectly; the ultimate outcome tends to be very much the same, by a somewhat different process (p. 55).
- The book does not say that any movement toward socialism is bound to lead to totalitarianism, though that danger exists; it warns that unless the principles of policy are mended, unpleasant consequences will follow which the advocates do not want (p. 55).
- Where he was wrong: he under-stressed the experience of communism in Russia (pardonable, he says, since Russia was an ally), and he made concessions to interventionism he now thinks unwarranted (p. 55).
- He was not aware how bad things already were: he had treated as rhetorical the question whether the Rule of Law would prevail if Hitler had gained unlimited power constitutionally, only to find that Kelsen, Laski and others had maintained precisely this (p. 55).
- Further study has increased his alarm; the influence of socialist ideas and naive trust in holders of totalitarian power have markedly increased since 1944 (pp. 55-56).
- He no longer resents being known for what he had regarded as a pamphlet for the time; the book may contain much he could not then have convincingly demonstrated, but it was a genuine effort to find the truth (p. 56).

## Arguments

**1944 preface: why an economist should write a political book**

- **Premises**: most economists are absorbed in the war machine and silenced by official position (p. 38) + in consequence opinion on post-war policy is led by amateurs and cranks (p. 38) + he still has leisure for literary work (p. 38) + (unstated) the views of professional economists would, if voiced, largely coincide with his apprehensions; he asserts only that many who cannot speak share them (p. 38).
  **Conclusion**: he has a duty to publish despite the personal costs (p. 38).
- **Premises**: he gains nothing personally from the society he favours (p. 38) + he once held the opposing views himself (p. 38) + publication damages his friendships and academic standing (p. 38).
  **Conclusion**: the argument cannot be dismissed as interest or upbringing (p. 38). (My note: this is a defence against a genetic objection, not a support for the thesis.)

**1956: why the receptions differed**

- **Premises**: in Europe the socialist ideals had been applied long enough for adherents to have seen difficulties and unlooked-for results (p. 41) + in the United States the intelligentsia had caught the infection only ten or fifteen years earlier and the New Deal had not soiled the enthusiasm (p. 41) + (unstated) readers judge an abstract argument by whether it matches their own experience.
  **Conclusion**: the same book was soberly examined in England and either abused or uncritically cheered in America (pp. 40-42).

**1956: why the book still matters after hot socialism**

- **Premises**: hot socialism is nearly dead (p. 42) + its conceptions persist in piecemeal measures whose aggregate effect may unintentionally remodel the economy (p. 43) + the welfare state's instruments often rely on administrative coercion, discrimination, direct controls and monopoly (p. 43) + such instruments are incompatible with a free society (p. 43) + (unstated) the mechanism the book identified for comprehensive planning also operates, more weakly, in partial measures.
  **Conclusion**: the book's analysis of the process is now of the greatest importance, and reforms should be tested against the Rule of Law criteria (p. 43).

**1956: the reply to the charge that British experience refuted the book**

- **Premises**: Britain under Labour did not become totalitarian (p. 46) + the book's main point is a psychological change in a people's character (p. 46) + that change takes one or two generations (p. 46) + institutions shape political ideals as much as the reverse (p. 46).
  **Conclusion**: six years without totalitarianism disprove nothing; a strong tradition of liberty is no safeguard (p. 46).
- **Supporting step**: the change is already visible in Britain, shown by the Barnes youth survey and matching Tocqueville's prediction (pp. 46-47).
- **Escape clause**: the consequences can be averted if the people throw out the party and also recognise the danger and change course (p. 46).
- My observation: the reply moves the test horizon out to one or two generations and makes the predicted effect a change of character that Hayek himself says is not easily demonstrated (p. 46). That makes the thesis harder to refute by any near-term record. The survey he cites describes conditions before Labour took office, from wartime regulation (p. 46), so it is evidence about war controls at least as much as about socialist planning.

**1956: the conditional-inevitability argument**

- **Premises**: socialist planning has consequences its authors do not foresee, and these are inevitable (p. 48) + pursuing the policy further in that state of affairs requires methods most socialists disapprove of (p. 48) + the old socialist parties were inhibited by democratic ideals and lacked the ruthlessness needed (p. 48) + (unstated) someone without those inhibitions will be available and will be preferred once the policy is persisted in.
  **Conclusion**: if the policy is to be pursued, totalitarian forces will get the upper hand (p. 48).
- **Illustration**: the Labour government's own Economic Survey disavowed compulsion, then restored labour direction within six months; the logic of its policies drove it there (pp. 45-46).

**1956: liberalism is not conservatism**

- **Premises**: conservatism is not a social program (p. 44) + its paternalist, nationalist and power-adoring tendencies bring it close to socialism (p. 44) + a conservative movement must defend established privilege and lean on government to protect it (p. 44) + liberalism is the denial of all privilege (p. 44).
  **Conclusion**: liberals should not call themselves conservatives, even where they must ally with them (p. 44).

**1976: the thesis survives the change in the meaning of socialism**

- **Premises**: socialism in 1944 meant nationalization and central planning (p. 55) + it now means redistribution through taxation and welfare institutions (p. 55) + under the latter the effects come more slowly, indirectly and imperfectly (p. 55) + (unstated) redistribution and welfare institutions set off the same dynamic as central planning; no mechanism is given here.
  **Conclusion**: the ultimate outcome tends to be very much the same, by a process not quite that described in the book (p. 55).

**1976: the denial of inevitability**

- **Premises**: critics allege he said any move toward socialism is bound to end in totalitarianism (p. 55) + the book is a warning conditional on not mending the principles of policy (p. 55).
  **Conclusion**: the book does not assert inevitability, though the danger exists (p. 55).

## Evidence offered

**1944 preface**

- Personal testimony only: his own motives, his earlier socialist views, the costs of publication (p. 38). The claim that most economists were silenced and that opinion was led by cranks is asserted without names or figures (p. 38).

**1956 foreword**

- Autobiographical testimony about origins, audience and his contact with the Continent (p. 39).
- Reception claims rest on his own impression; he names Wootton's Freedom under Planning as the representative left criticism and cites one reviewer who thought it confirmed him (note 5, pp. 49-50), and Finer's Road to Reaction as the specimen of abuse (note 9, p. 51).
- Publication history: rejection by three houses, attributed in his note 7 to political prejudice on the strength of William Miller's statement as reported by Couch (p. 40; note 7, p. 50). Caldwell's bracketed addition gives the background and shows the source is a hostile letter, which fits one firm, not necessarily all three (p. 50).
- One historical datum for American enthusiasm for planning: the 1934 National Planning Board's attention to planning in Germany, Italy, Russia and Japan (p. 41). He declines to name individuals (p. 41).
- A quoted critic (identified by Caldwell as Alvin Hansen) for the counter-claim that the four countries reached totalitarianism by different roads (p. 42; note 12, p. 51).
- Official document: two passages of the Economic Survey for 1947, set against the later Control of Engagement Order (pp. 45-46; Caldwell's notes 18-20, p. 52). Hayek concedes the power was never used and argues that the existence of the power is what matters (pp. 45-46).
- Sociological survey: long extract from L. J. Barnes's 1945 report on youth in an English county (pp. 46-47; note 21, p. 52). One report, one county, pre-Labour, wartime regulation. Hayek's wording outruns it: the change in national character can hardly be mistaken, yet the changes are not easily demonstrated but clearly felt if one lives in the country (p. 46).
- Authority by quotation: Tocqueville on the new servitude (p. 47); Ivor Thomas, a Labour defector (p. 48; note 24, p. 52); The Economist on Crichel Down and Hewart's New Despotism (p. 48; note 25, pp. 52-53); the jurist G. W. Keeton that Britain lives on the edge of dictatorship (p. 49; note 26, p. 53). These are opinions of like-minded observers. He presents them as observers less suspect of preconceived opinions (p. 48), but Thomas had left Labour and later joined the Conservatives (Caldwell, p. 52).
- Comparative assertion without data: the German Social Democrats of the 1920s never approached totalitarian planning as closely as British Labour (p. 48).
- Referral: Jewkes's Ordeal by Planning for the detailed case (p. 49). The foreword itself does not examine British economic policy in detail and says so (p. 48).
- Confident language outrunning evidence: that it is doubtful whether the Rule of Law still prevails in Britain (p. 48); that the century of socialism probably ended around 1948 (p. 42); that the main conclusions are widely accepted (p. 42).

**1976 preface**

- Autobiographical testimony about dates, origin and his own discomfort (pp. 53-54). The unnamed philosopher anecdote (identified by Caldwell as Carnap) illustrates hostile reaction by people who had not read the book (p. 54; note 2, p. 56).
- One comparative example for the terminological point: Sweden against Great Britain and Austria (p. 55). No evidence is given for the claim that the welfare-state route tends to the same ultimate outcome (p. 55), nor for the claim that socialist influence and trust in totalitarian rulers have markedly increased (pp. 55-56).
- Kelsen and Laski are cited, without reference, as having held that the Rule of Law would prevail under a constitutionally empowered Hitler (p. 55).
- A concession about evidence: the book may contain much he could not have convincingly demonstrated when he wrote it (p. 56).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Inevitability, 1956 against 1976.** In 1956 the consequences of socialist planning are called inevitable and totalitarian forces will prevail if the policy is pursued (p. 48), and the logic of its policies drove Labour into coercion (p. 45). In 1976 he denies having said that any movement toward socialism is bound to lead to totalitarianism (p. 55). The two are reconcilable only by reading the 1956 claim as conditional on (a) full socialist planning and (b) persistence in it, with the escape clause that a people can change course (p. 46). My observation: the 1976 denial rejects the any-movement version, yet the paragraph just before it says the welfare-state route tends to very much the same ultimate outcome (p. 55), which reintroduces a strong tendency claim for partial measures. The denial is of necessity, not of direction.
- **Welfare state, 1956 against 1976.** 1956: some welfare-state aims are practicable and laudable and the question is which instruments are used (p. 43). 1976: he regrets concessions to interventionism as unwarranted and treats welfare-state redistribution as a slower road to much the same end (p. 55). The 1976 position is harder than the 1956 one, and both need checking against the book's own allowances for social insurance and a minimum income in the main chapters (chapter 9 is the likely place; not in my units).
- **Is socialism dead or growing?** 1956: hot socialism is nearly dead, its illusions discarded by its leaders (p. 42). 1976: the influence of socialist ideas has markedly increased since the book was written (pp. 55-56). Consistent only because the word has changed meaning (p. 55).
- **What counts as the book's merit.** 1956: the thesis was not original; the merit is the analysis of process (p. 42). 1976: the book may contain much he could not have convincingly demonstrated (p. 56), and in 1956 there were points he would state more cautiously or fortify with more proof (p. 44). These concessions sit beside the claim that experience strengthened his concern (p. 45).
- **Scope of the thesis as restated in 1956.** The totalitarianism is that which central control of all economic activity tends to produce (p. 42). That wording limits the claim to comprehensive control, which is narrower than the worry about piecemeal measures a page later (p. 43).
- **Russia.** Both later texts say the book underplayed the Soviet case for wartime reasons (pp. 39-40, p. 55). Synthesis should treat the German-centred evidence of the main chapters as a choice Hayek later called a fault (p. 55).
- **Rule of Law.** 1956 calls chapter 6 the central chapter and the source of the tests any reform should pass (p. 43), calls it all too brief (p. 45), and says its reasons explain the British administrative state (p. 48). The Rule of Law chapter is thus the part Hayek himself nominates as the positive criterion.
- **Chapter 10.** Hayek quotes his own chapter 10 on socialists' democratic inhibitions to show he never imputed totalitarian aims (p. 48; Caldwell's note 23, p. 52).
- **Origins.** The 1976 preface names the Beveridge memorandum as the first step (p. 53); that memorandum is the 1933 document printed in the appendix (see ap01, pp. 237-241).
- **Control of Engagement Order.** Hayek 1956 reads it as proof that planning forces coercion (pp. 45-46). Friedman reads the same episode as the moment liberty won and planning was dropped (see ap01, p. 250). Same fact, opposite lessons.
- **Chapter 16.** The conclusion calls the book essentially critical (p. 234). The 1976 preface agrees that it is incomplete without an account of the market order (p. 54).
- **Conservatism.** The 1956 distinction (p. 44) anticipates the essay Caldwell cites, Why I Am Not a Conservative (note 14, p. 51); a link for cross-book synthesis with The Constitution of Liberty.

## Open questions

- Which concessions does the 1976 Hayek think unwarranted (p. 55)? He does not say. Candidates in the main text should be listed (social insurance, minimum income, regulation of hours and safety, monetary policy) and marked as retracted-in-general but not individually.
- Does the main text ever state the thesis without the conditional, so that Knight's and others' inevitability reading is fair? Compare the wording in chapters 5, 7 and 10 with the 1956 formulation (p. 48) and the 1976 denial (p. 55).
- Is the psychological-change thesis (p. 46) actually prominent in the 1944 text, as Hayek says in 1956, or is it a later re-emphasis? Check where the book argues that institutions change character.
- What observable outcome, at what horizon, would Hayek accept as refuting the thesis, given the generational timescale (p. 46) and the slower, indirect welfare-state route (p. 55)?
- By what mechanism does redistribution through taxation tend to the same outcome as central planning (p. 55)? The prefaces assert it; later works are said to supply it (p. 54).
- How far do the 1956 judgments on Britain (Rule of Law doubtful, edge of dictatorship, pp. 48-49) hold up, and does their failure to materialise count against the thesis or under the escape clause (p. 46)?
- How does the claim that liberalism denies all privilege (p. 44) square with whatever the main chapters permit the state to do for particular groups?
