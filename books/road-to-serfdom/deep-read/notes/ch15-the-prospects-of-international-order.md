# ch15: The Prospects of International Order (pp. 220–234)

Chapter text runs pp. 220–233; footnotes pp. 233–234. Bracketed notes are the editor's (Caldwell's); notes 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9 (first part), 10 (first part), 11 (first part), 13 and 14 (first part) are Hayek's.

## Question this chapter answers

What form of international order can secure peace after the war without reproducing, on a larger scale, the dangers of planning? Hayek's answer: neither independent national planning (which produces conflict between states) nor international economic planning (which can only be rule by force of the strongest), but a federation: a supernational political authority with strictly limited, mainly negative powers, bound by the Rule of Law, able to forbid states to harm one another and to restrict trade, and not empowered to direct their economic life (pp. 221-223, 228-232).

## Leading propositions

- The abandonment of nineteenth-century liberalism has cost most in international relations, where the retreat began (pp. 220-221).
- Independent national planning is harmful in aggregate even economically and produces international friction; there is little hope of peace while every country may adopt whatever measures suit its immediate interest (p. 221).
- National planning makes economic relations into relations between nations as trading bodies and so into sources of friction and envy between whole nations (p. 221).
- Substituting negotiation between states for competition replaces a metaphorical struggle with a contest of force between armed states subject to no law; such transactions "must end in clashes of power" (p. 221).
- If existing trends continue, victory over National Socialism will produce "a world of many national socialisms" (p. 221).
- International planning avoids some dangers of national planning but creates greater ones (p. 222).
- As the scale of planning grows, agreement on the order of ends decreases and reliance on force grows (p. 222).
- People will sacrifice for fellow nationals whose ways are familiar; no common ideals of distributive justice exist across even western Europe (p. 222).
- Most people fail to see the difficulty because they assume they will do the deciding for others (pp. 222-223).
- Planning a vast area of many peoples by democratic procedure is impossible; international planning "cannot be anything but a naked rule of force" (p. 223).
- German brutality toward smaller peoples is not simply special wickedness; "it is the nature of the task they have assumed which makes these things inevitable" (p. 223).
- Even an unselfish dominant power would be committed to force; and unselfishness is unlikely, as English proposals to shape European reconstruction to British industry show (pp. 223-224).
- It is a delusion that working-class solidarity would overcome international conflicts of interest; under world planning class strife would become strife between the working classes of different countries (p. 224).
- Minimum-wage protection demanded by richer workers deprives poor-country workers of their only means of improvement (p. 224).
- In a planned international system rich nations would be hated and envied far more than in a free economy (p. 224).
- "Planning to equalize standards of life" (e.g. a TVA for the Danube Basin) means fixing an order of priority among peoples' claims, for which no basis exists; in practice the large nations would decide, and earn common hatred (pp. 224-226).
- Taking on such moral responsibility by force puts one "in a position in which it becomes impossible to act morally" and corrupts the victors (p. 226).
- An international authority can be just and useful if it merely keeps order and creates conditions for peoples to develop their own lives (p. 226).
- Conferring "merely" specific economic powers on international bodies does not escape the difficulty: economic planning is not a technical task; control of an essential commodity or service is one of the most far-reaching powers; autonomous international monopolies would be "the worst of all conceivable rackets" (pp. 226-227).
- A raw-material controller would be master of the fate of industries and countries (p. 227).
- Nations may abide by formal rules they have agreed, but "will never submit" to direction by international planning or to having the rank of their needs fixed by majority vote (pp. 227-228).
- The "realists" who favour such schemes expect great powers to use "international" authorities to impose their will on small nations; this is the only condition under which international planning is practicable (p. 228).
- For small states this means more complete subjection than renouncing a defined part of political sovereignty (p. 228).
- Advocates of a centrally directed New Order for Europe, Carr foremost, disregard the rights of small nations (p. 228).
- No order or peace is possible if states "regain unfettered sovereignty in the economic sphere" (p. 228).
- What is needed is a power to restrain nations from harming neighbours: rules defining what a state may do and an authority to enforce them; its powers are "mainly of a negative kind" (pp. 228-229).
- The need is for an international political authority, not economic authorities; the powers to devolve are the minimum needed to keep peace, "essentially the powers of the ultra-liberal 'laissez faire' state", strictly bound by the Rule of Law (p. 229).
- The need for such authority grows as states become economic actors (p. 229).
- Federation is the only form of association of peoples that creates international order without undue strain on their desire for independence, and the only way international law can become real (pp. 229-230).
- Federal division of power limits both the whole and the member states; many fashionable kinds of planning would become impossible, but desirable planning would remain free (p. 230).
- Within a federation, centralization within states may be reversed and powers devolved to local authorities (p. 230).
- Federation was the ideal of almost all nineteenth-century liberals (p. 230).
- "We shall not rebuild civilization on the large scale"; democracy has never worked well without much local self-government; small countries are the model (p. 231).
- The supernational authority must be very powerful in enforcing common law and so constituted that neither it nor national authorities can become tyrannical; limiting power may sometimes prevent its use for good (p. 231).
- The great powers acquire the moral right to impose rules on others by first submitting to them themselves (p. 231).
- An international authority that limits the state's power over the individual is one of the best safeguards of peace (p. 231).
- A comprehensive world organization ("some new League of Nations") is needed, but must not be overloaded; the League was weak because it tried to be world-wide (p. 232).
- Close federal union is at first practicable only in a region as narrow as part of western Europe; wider cooperation is possible among the British Empire, western Europe and probably the United States (p. 232).
- War between blocs remains possible; measures that would make war altogether impossible "may well be worse than even war itself" (pp. 232-233).

## Arguments

**A. National planning breeds international conflict**

- **Premises**: (p. 221) National planning requires shutting out outside influences, hence restrictions on movement of men and goods. (p. 221) It makes national resources the exclusive property of nations as wholes and turns trade into dealings between states. (p. 221) States are judges in their own cause, armed, and subject to no superior law.
  **Unstated premise**: under competition, economic rivalry between individuals of different nations does not engage state power (i.e. liberal states do not act for their nationals' commercial interests).
  **Conclusion**: (p. 221) such transactions "must end in clashes of power"; the outcome is many national socialisms in recurrent conflict.

**B. Scale and agreement**

- **Premises**: (p. 222) Planning requires agreement on an order of ends. (p. 222) Agreement on ends diminishes as the group widens. (p. 222) Where agreement is lacking, force must be used.
  **Unstated premise**: carried over from ch. 5: a plan necessarily implies a complete ranking of ends, and no procedure (bargaining, compensation, voting) can substitute for prior agreement.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 222-223) international planning is more coercive than national planning and "cannot be anything but a naked rule of force".

**C. No international distributive justice**

- **Premises**: (p. 222) Willingness to sacrifice depends on familiarity and shared views of appropriate status. (p. 222) These are absent between Norwegian and Portuguese, Dutch and English, French and Italian. (p. 225) There is "no basis" for ranking the Rumanian peasant's claim against the Albanian's.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 225-226) any planned equalization of living standards is a decision by the powerful, perceived as supreme injustice by many, enforceable only by force.

**D. The task, not the agent, produces brutality**

- **Premises**: (p. 223) Directing the economic life of peoples with divergent values commits one to force. (p. 223) This holds whatever the intentions. (p. 226) Imposing one's moral views by force on other communities makes moral action impossible.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 223, 226) German brutality follows from the task of Grossraumwirtschaft; the victors would be morally corrupted by taking on the same task.

**E. Against functional international economic authorities**

- **Premises**: (p. 226) Economic planning is not a merely technical task. (pp. 226-227) Exclusive control of an essential commodity or service confers far-reaching power. (p. 227) "Technical necessities" cannot be questioned by outsiders, so the power is uncontrollable. (p. 227) A raw-material controller decides which countries may start which industries.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 227) autonomous international monopolies, subject to no government, would be "the worst of all conceivable rackets".

**F. Rules, not direction**

- **Premises**: (pp. 227-228) Nations may abide by formal rules they have agreed to; they will never accept having their needs ranked by majority vote. (p. 228) Unfettered economic sovereignty is incompatible with peace. (p. 229) An authority not itself an economic actor can hold the scales between interests.
  **Unstated premises**: (i) the line between "restraining harm" and "directing" is determinate enough to be put in a constitution; (ii) what counts as action "harmful to their neighbors" is essentially trade restriction and can be defined without contested economic judgment; (iii) states would surrender this economic sovereignty more readily than they would accept planning.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 229) the international authority should have the negative powers of the laissez-faire state, under the Rule of Law.

**G. Federation as the institutional form**

- **Premises**: (p. 230) International law needs an enforcing power. (p. 230) The obstacle to creating one was the belief that it must have all the powers of a modern state. (p. 230) Federal division of powers removes that need and limits both levels.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 229-230) federation is the only way to realize international law consistently with national independence; it also blocks harmful planning at both levels.

**H. Smallness and democracy**

- **Premises**: (p. 231) Democracy needs local self-government as a school of responsibility. (p. 231) Where the scope of measures is so large that only the bureaucracy has the knowledge, private initiative flags.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 231) civilization will not be rebuilt "on the large scale"; federation should permit devolution and protect small states.

**I. Modesty of aim**

- **Premises**: (p. 232) The League was weakened by trying to be universal. (p. 232) Close association is feasible only among similar countries.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 232-233) aim at a strong regional federation inside a looser world body; reducing the risk of war is "all we can reasonably hope to achieve".

## Evidence offered

- **Citation in place of argument**: Robbins, "Economic Planning and International Order", "passim", for the whole first section (p. 233); Hayek says the points "can be touched upon only very briefly".
- **Bare assertion with "needs little emphasis"**: the incompatibility of unrestricted national economic policy with peace (p. 221). The interwar trade restrictions are alluded to ("only too visible before 1939") but not described.
- **Mechanism argument**: scale reduces agreement and raises coercion (p. 222).
- **Hypothetical examples (thought experiments)**: Norwegian fisherman and Portuguese; Dutch worker and Coventry mechanic; French peasant and Italian industrialization (p. 222); Spanish iron versus South Wales, optics concentrated in Germany, refining reserved to producer countries (p. 223); Rumanian, Albanian, Slovak, Slovene, Macedonian, Bulgarian, Czech and Hungarian claims (pp. 225-226). These are persuasive illustrations presented as rhetorical questions; none is an actual case.
- **Historical example**: German Grossraumwirtschaft and its brutality (p. 223). Used as proof that the task entails force; "If anything is certain" is the language used.
- **Historical example, in a footnote**: British colonial development involves imposing values on those assisted, and has made colonial experts skeptical of international colonial administration (p. 233).
- **Contemporary opinion, unsourced**: English people arguing that reconstruction of Europe should serve British industry (pp. 223-224).
- **Assertion about race**: world planning would mean "a more or less conscious endeavor to secure the dominance of the white man"; Europeans will never submit their standard of life to a world parliament (p. 233). Offered as self-evident.
- **Analogy**: a Tennessee Valley Authority for the Danube (p. 225). The editor suggests the studies Hayek may have had in mind, with "may", so the target is not identified by Hayek (p. 233).
- **Quotation from an ally about an opponent**: Manning's question about Carr, "If the Nazi way with small sovereign states is indeed to become the common form, what is the war about?" (p. 228), sourced in Hayek's note (p. 233). Carr himself is not quoted in this chapter. A Time and Tide remark on the Carr flavour of The Times and New Statesman (pp. 233-234).
- **Report of allied opinion**: "disquiet and alarm" among smaller Allies (p. 228). No source.
- **Analogy**: preventing killing requires an authority, not a declaration; so with international law (p. 230).
- **Self-citation**: "The Economic Conditions of Inter-state Federalism" (1939) for the claim that federation makes many kinds of planning impossible (p. 234). The argument itself is not reproduced.
- **Citation**: Jennings, "A Federation for Western Europe" (p. 234).
- **Appeal to tradition**: nineteenth-century liberals, Tennyson, Sidgwick (pp. 230, 234).
- **Generalization, asserted**: "more beauty and decency" in small peoples; democracy has "nowhere" worked well without local self-government; Holland and Switzerland as models (p. 231).
- **Personal conviction stated as such**: the League's weakness came from its universality (p. 232).

**Editor's corrections of Hayek's quotations and citations**

- Epigraph: Acton wrote "federalism", not "federation" (p. 233).
- Sidgwick wrote "some further integration", not "some future integration" (p. 234).
- Hayek's note 13 gives Robbins's title as "Economic Planning and Economic Order", whereas note 2 gives "Economic Planning and International Order" (pp. 233, 234); the editor does not flag this.
- The editor identifies Tennyson's poem as "Locksley Hall" and quotes the lines (p. 234), and records that both Hayek and Robbins favoured European federation (p. 234).
- All slips are minor and none changes sense; contrast ch. 13, where one omission bears on the charge against Carr.

## Concessions and qualifications

- International planning "would avert some of the obvious dangers raised by planning on a national scale" (p. 222).
- People will make sacrifices for fellow nationals, including so that "nobody should sink below a certain level" (p. 222); the national minimum is thus treated as morally feasible within a nation.
- English standards of decency in international affairs are "as high, if not higher" than elsewhere (p. 223).
- There is "urgent need for amelioration of economic conditions" in the Danube region, on humanitarian, economic and peace grounds, and this needs a new political setting (p. 225).
- "By all means let us assist the poorer people as much as we can in their own efforts" (p. 226). Assistance accepted; direction rejected.
- The opponents are right that states cannot regain "unfettered sovereignty in the economic sphere" (p. 228).
- **International authority accepted** (pp. 228-232), with conditions:
  - a power to restrain nations from action harmful to neighbours, with a set of rules defining what a state may do and an authority able to enforce them (pp. 228-229);
  - powers "mainly of a negative kind", above all to say "No" to restrictive measures (p. 229);
  - a political, not an economic, authority, "not mixed up in the economic game" (p. 229);
  - only the "minimum of powers" needed for peace, those of the laissez-faire state (p. 229);
  - "strictly circumscribed by the Rule of Law" (p. 229);
  - federal in form, with "strictly defined powers" transferred and all else left to member countries (p. 229);
  - "very powerful" for enforcing the common law, with a constitution that prevents tyranny at both levels (p. 231);
  - the great powers must first submit to the rules themselves (p. 231);
  - it should also limit the powers of the state over the individual (p. 231);
  - states renounce "a clearly defined part of political sovereignty" (p. 228).
- Federation is not "an obstacle to all planning"; it leaves "the way free for all desirable planning" and international planning "where true agreement can be reached" (p. 230).
- Fusing countries into one centralized state is "more impracticable" and of doubtful desirability (p. 229).
- Many claims made for "Federal Union" were "ill-considered and often extremely silly" (p. 229).
- Nineteenth-century liberals "may not have been fully aware" how essential federation was to their principles (p. 230).
- Limiting power "occasionally may also prevent its use for desirable purposes" (p. 231).
- Applying the federal principle is "a task of extreme difficulty" (p. 232).
- There is "an imperative need" for a comprehensive world body, "some new League of Nations" (p. 232).
- Regional federations leave war between blocs possible (p. 232).
- War cannot be made impossible; only the risk of friction can be reduced (pp. 232-233).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The scale argument (p. 222) applies ch. 5 (planning needs agreement on a complete code of ends; democracy cannot supply it) and ch. 10 (the larger the group, the lower the common denominator) to the international case. If ch. 5's argument fails, so does this.
- The rule/direction contrast (pp. 227-229) applies ch. 6's formal rules versus substantive commands to states. The "ultra-liberal 'laissez faire' state" (p. 229) is notable: within the nation Hayek expressly rejects laissez-faire as a principle (ch. 1, ch. 3) and accepts a minimum income, regulation of monopoly (ch. 13, p. 200) and retraining (ch. 14, p. 211). So the international authority is assigned a narrower role than the national state. My observation: this is consistent if welfare functions stay at national or local level, but he does not say here where they sit.
- "Planning" shifts meaning at p. 230 ("all desirable planning"); see the Key terms row. Synthesis should not read p. 230 as a retreat from the anti-planning thesis, but it does show that Hayek's objection is to central direction and restriction, not to deliberate collective action as such. Compare ch. 3.
- The negative-power design presupposes that a federal authority could strike down national restrictive measures. Hayek's cited 1939 article (p. 234) argues that this would make much national planning impossible. So ch. 15 is also an argument about domestic policy: federation is a device for constraining planning at home (p. 230). This links to the Acton epigraph, where federation is prized as a check "on democracy" (p. 220), and to ch. 5's view of democracy as a means. Tension, my observation: Hayek calls federalism "the application to international affairs of democracy" (p. 229) while valuing it because it limits what democratic majorities can do.
- "They will never submit" to planning (p. 227) versus the expectation that states will submit to a "very powerful" enforcing authority and renounce economic sovereignty (pp. 228, 231). Hayek's reply is that agreement on rules is easier than agreement on ends (pp. 227-228). My observation: the prohibition of "restrictive measures" is itself a substantive economic programme (free movement of goods, capital and perhaps people) on which agreement cannot be assumed.
- The claim that brutality follows from "the nature of the task" (p. 223) parallels ch. 10 (why the worst get on top: the system selects for ruthlessness) and qualifies ch. 12, which stresses specifically German ideas. Both explanations are structural-ideological and reject national character.
- The attack on "realists" and Carr (pp. 227-228) continues ch. 13 (pp. 192-195). Here Carr is characterized only through Manning's review, not quoted; given the editor's corrections in ch. 13 (p. 203), the characterization should be treated with caution.
- Autonomous international commodity monopolies (p. 227) extend ch. 13's argument on state-protected monopoly (p. 199): monopoly "subject to none" is the international analogue.
- Minimum wages as protection for rich-country workers (p. 224) extends ch. 13's argument that privileged labor gains at the expense of the poorest (p. 200) and ch. 9 on security for some at others' cost.
- Smallness, local self-government and "their own little worlds" link ch. 14 (pp. 216, 218) to p. 231. Local responsibility is where ch. 14's moral argument and ch. 15's institutional argument meet.
- "A system of rules which they have the power to enforce" and the victors "first submitting" (p. 231) connects to ch. 14's call to offer Germans a real alternative (pp. 217-218).
- The closing sentence that abolishing war could be worse than war (pp. 232-233) parallels ch. 14's rejection of "at any price" for full employment (p. 210) and ch. 9 on security: a general pattern in which Hayek refuses to make any single good absolute.
- Hayek's footnote on colonial development (p. 233) and on "the dominance of the white man" (p. 233) show him using the imperial case as an argument against international planning; it leaves unexamined whether the liberal international economy he prefers had itself rested on imperial force. Flag for thematic synthesis on empire.
- "Realpolitik" ended the federal hopes of liberals (pp. 230-231); the same word appears among the German borrowings in ch. 14 (p. 217).

## Open questions

- Is "restraining action which will damage others" a workable constitutional criterion? Almost any national policy (tariffs, subsidies, monetary policy, migration control, minimum wages) affects others. Who interprets harm, and is that interpretation any less discretionary than planning?
- Does the federation require free movement of people as well as goods and capital? Hayek speaks of restrictions on "the movements of men and goods" (p. 221) but does not say whether the federal "No" extends to immigration controls.
- Where do the national welfare functions Hayek accepts (minimum income, ch. 14 p. 213) sit in a federation whose central authority has only laissez-faire powers and whose members cannot restrict movement? Check ch. 9 and the 1939 article.
- Is the claim that there are no common ideals of distributive justice across nations (p. 222) an empirical claim about 1944 or a claim in principle? If empirical, later European integration with transfers is a test; if in principle, what supports it?
- Does the argument at p. 222 prove too much? National populations are also heterogeneous; Hayek's ch. 5 says agreement fails nationally too. Why is the national minimum feasible (p. 222) but any international redistribution not?
- How "very powerful" (p. 231) must the enforcing authority be, and does it need armed force of its own? Hayek gives no account of its composition, its legislature or its control of force; "democracy with definitely limited powers" (p. 229) is the only description.
- Compare the actual post-war settlement (UN, Bretton Woods institutions, GATT, European Communities) with Hayek's categories: which were "negative" rule-enforcing bodies and which "economic authorities"? This tests whether his dichotomy sorts real institutions.
- Is the regional preference (p. 232) for federating countries "more similar in their civilization, outlook, and standards" in tension with the universalism of "nations of free men"?
- How does the international argument relate to the book's main thesis: is international order an application of the argument, or does federation function as the positive programme the earlier chapters lack?
