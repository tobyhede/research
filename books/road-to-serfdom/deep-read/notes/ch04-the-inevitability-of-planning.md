# ch04: The "Inevitability" of Planning (pp. 88–97)

Scope note: the chapter text runs from the heading on p. 88 to p. 96; footnotes run pp. 96-97. Unbracketed footnotes are Hayek's; bracketed ones are the editor's (Caldwell).

## Question this chapter answers

Is the replacement of competition by central planning forced on us by technological change and the complexity of modern industry, or is it the result of opinion and deliberate policy?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| inevitability (of planning) | p. 88 | The planners' claim that circumstances beyond our control compel the substitution of planning for competition. Called a deliberately cultivated 'myth'; Hayek's counter-thesis is that the trend is the product of opinions propagated for half a century. |
| planning / central planning | p. 88 | As fixed in ch. 3: central direction substituted for competition. Qualified here as 'comprehensive economic planning' (p. 94) and 'conscious central planning' (p. 92). Also used loosely for German cartel policy, 'the ideal which we now call planning' (p. 91). |
| monopoly | p. 88 | Control of an industry by one or a few firms, cartels or syndicates; treated as paired with planning ('the tendency toward monopoly and planning'). Its growth is admitted as fact; its cause is said to be collusion and public policy, protection above all. |
| concentration of industry | p. 88 | The Marxist doctrine that large-scale production must drive out small firms; the source, at third or fourth hand, of the inevitability belief. |
| competition | p. 91 | The only method able to coordinate a complex division of labour; a condition in which the individual producer must adapt to prices and cannot control them (p. 92). |
| price system | p. 92 | An 'apparatus of registration' that automatically records the relevant effects of individual actions and whose indications are both the resultant of and the guide for individual decisions; an impersonal mechanism for transmitting information. Works only under competition. |
| division of knowledge | p. 92 | The dispersal among individuals of the facts 'which only they can know'; the reason decentralisation is imperative and the counterpart of the division of labour. |
| decentralization plus automatic coordination | p. 92 | Hayek's description of the competitive method of solving the economic problem, set against 'central direction'. |
| conscious control | p. 92 | Coordination by a mind or board that surveys all the facts; possible only in simple conditions. |
| freedom / liberty | pp. 93-94 | Here freedom of choice and variety, and room for 'unforeseeable free growth'; 'our most precious inheritance'. An instrumental, progress-oriented justification is added to the ch. 2 definition (absence of coercion). |
| specialist | p. 94 | The technical expert whose ideal could be realised if it were humanity's sole aim; widened to everyone with a predominant single aim (p. 95). |
| coordination | p. 96 | Balancing competing ends against each other; cannot be a new specialism, and requires a method that works without an 'omniscient dictator'. |

## Leading propositions

- Few planners say merely that planning is desirable; most say it is forced on us by circumstances (p. 88).
- The claim that technology spontaneously eliminates competition is a myth accepted by iteration and is devoid of foundation (p. 88).
- The tendency toward monopoly and planning is the product of opinions fostered for half a century, not of objective facts beyond our control (p. 88).
- The belief in technological necessity derives mainly from the Marxist doctrine of the concentration of industry (p. 88).
- The growth of monopoly over the last fifty years is not disputed, though its extent is often greatly exaggerated (p. 88).
- The argument from economies of scale picks out one effect of technical progress, ignores opposite effects and gets little support from the facts (p. 89).
- The Temporary National Economic Committee found scant support for the view that the efficiency of large-scale production causes the disappearance of competition; monopoly is frequently the product of collusive agreement and public policy (p. 89).
- Aspiring monopolists regularly seek and often get the help of state power (p. 89).
- Monopoly appeared first not in the most advanced economy but in the young industrial countries, the United States and Germany, in the last third of the nineteenth century (p. 90).
- In Germany cartels and syndicates were systematically fostered by deliberate policy from 1878, by protection, inducements and finally compulsion (p. 90).
- The doctrine of inevitable 'monopoly capitalism' spread largely through German socialist theoreticians, Sombart in particular, generalising from their own country (p. 90).
- British industry was on the whole competitive until 1931; monopolies grew at an amazing rate only after the turn to general protection (p. 90).
- In Germany, the prototype, the suppression of competition was deliberate policy in the service of the ideal now called planning (p. 91).
- In social evolution nothing is inevitable but thinking makes it so (p. 91).
- Complexity does create some problems competition cannot solve, such as those of a modern town and of public utilities, but these are not what the planners have in mind (p. 91).
- It is the very complexity of the modern division of labour that makes competition the only adequate method of coordination (p. 91).
- Central planning would be easy if conditions were simple enough for one person or board to survey all the relevant facts; decentralisation becomes imperative when they are not (p. 91).
- Coordination among decentralised agents can be effected only by arrangements that convey to each the information he needs; no single centre can know or collect the details of constantly changing supply and demand (p. 92).
- The price system under competition is such an apparatus and no other system even promises to do the same (p. 92).
- The price system does this only if competition prevails, that is, if producers adapt to prices and cannot control them (p. 92).
- The industrial system could never have reached its present differentiation and complexity under conscious central planning; further complexity makes non-conscious coordination more important, not less (p. 92).
- A third argument holds that new technical possibilities can be used only if protection against competition, a monopoly, is granted; it is often special pleading or a confusion of engineering excellence with social desirability (pp. 92-93).
- In a residual group of cases that argument has some force: compulsory standardisation might raise abundance enough to compensate for lost choice (p. 93).
- Such cases still do not make central direction inevitable; they present a choice between gaining an advantage by compulsion and forgoing or postponing it (p. 93).
- Forgoing such gains may be the price of freedom, and it preserves a stimulus to progress, since we cannot predict from which variant something better will develop (pp. 93-94).
- The argument for freedom is to leave room for unforeseeable growth, and it holds even where compulsion would seem to bring only advantages (p. 94).
- Nothing in modern technology forces comprehensive planning, but much in it makes a planning authority's power far more dangerous (p. 94).
- Technical experts lead the planners because almost any one technical ideal could be realised quickly if it were made humanity's sole aim, and its frustration makes the specialist revolt against the existing order (p. 94).
- A planned society would certainly further some objectives more than at present, and existing planned societies show good things owed entirely to planning (p. 95).
- Technical excellence out of line with general conditions is evidence of misdirected resources, not of the superiority of planning; the German motor roads are the example (p. 95).
- Everyone is in some measure a specialist who believes his own scale of values would prevail; each wants planning for his own aim, and adopting planning would only expose the conflict between the aims (pp. 95-96).
- The single-minded idealists who most want to plan would be the most dangerous planners and the most intolerant of the planning of others (p. 96).
- Coordination cannot become a specialism; the economist does not claim the knowledge a coordinator would need, and asks for a method that coordinates without an omniscient dictator (p. 96).

## Arguments

- **Against technological determinism of monopoly (empirical).**
  **Premises**: (p. 89) the scale argument predicts large firms driving out small in most industries; (p. 89) the TNEC final report and the Wilcox monograph find the superior efficiency of large establishments undemonstrated and monopoly often due to collusion and public policy; (p. 89) monopolists regularly rely on state help; (unstated) what holds for American industry around 1940 holds generally, and (p. 89) 'an investigation of conditions in England would lead to very similar results', asserted without the investigation.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 89) there is nothing inevitable about the growth of monopoly.

- **Argument from historical order.**
  **Premises**: (p. 90) if monopoly came from technology or the evolution of capitalism it would appear first in the most advanced economy; (p. 90) it appeared first in the then young industrial countries, the United States and Germany, both protectionist; (p. 90) in Britain it grew fast only after protection in 1931; (p. 90) it is absurd to suppose technological necessities that operated in Germany in the 1880s reached Britain only in the 1930s; (unstated) Britain was the most advanced economy throughout, and no difference other than policy explains the timing.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 90-91) monopoly follows policy, and policy follows ideas; 'nothing is inevitable but thinking makes it so'.

- **The complexity (knowledge) argument: the chapter's main positive argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 91) central direction requires that one person or board survey all the relevant facts; (p. 91) in a complex division of labour the factors are too numerous for any synoptic view; (pp. 91-92) therefore decisions must be decentralised to agents who adjust to facts only they can know; (p. 92) decentralised decisions still have to be mutually adjusted; (p. 92) no centre can know, or collect and disseminate fast enough, the details of constantly changing supply and demand; (p. 92) competitive prices automatically record the effects of individual actions and guide each decision; (unstated) the knowledge in question cannot be gathered or computed centrally in principle, not just with present means; (unstated) actual prices under actual competition transmit that information well enough to coordinate.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 91-92) the more complex the economy, the more competition is the only adequate method of coordination and the less central planning can work; complexity is an argument against planning.

- **The fork on the planners' argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 88) one version says technology destroys competition; (pp. 92-93) another says technology cannot be used unless competition is suspended; Hayek notes these are 'almost opposite'.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 88) (p. 93) neither shows necessity; at most the second shows a choice.

- **Freedom over calculable gain.**
  **Premises**: (p. 93) in some cases compulsory uniformity might make everyone better off by present lights; (p. 93) enforcing it makes future development depend on the knowledge particular people now possess; (p. 94) we cannot predict which variant will lead to something better; (p. 94) long-run material progress depends on variety; (unstated) the expected long-run gain from open-ended variety outweighs the forgone present gain, or else freedom is to be preferred even where it does not.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 94) the case for freedom applies even where compulsion would seem to bring only advantages and may in the instance do no harm. Hayek explicitly declines to claim the sacrifice will be repaid in every case (p. 94), so the conclusion rests on a rule-level, not case-level, justification.

- **The specialist argument (why planning is popular and why that is no recommendation).**
  **Premises**: (p. 94) any one technical ideal could be realised if made the sole aim; (p. 94) they cannot all be realised at once, and seeing this requires stepping outside one's specialism; (pp. 95-96) each enthusiast expects the planners to share his own priorities; (p. 96) the aims conflict; (unstated) there is no agreed scale of values by which a planning authority could rank the aims.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 96) the coalition for planning rests on an illusion that dissolves as soon as planning is adopted, and rule by the best specialists would be unbearable and irrational. The unstated premise is the explicit subject of ch. 5.

## Evidence offered

- Epigraph from Mussolini on complexity restricting freedom (p. 88). Kind: quotation from an opponent; the editor gives the source as a 1929 Grand Fascist Council report quoted at second hand in Ashton (p. 96).
- Lionel Robbins's essay 'The Inevitability of Monopoly' for the claim that the extent of monopoly is exaggerated in Hayek's note 2 (p. 96). Kind: citation of an ally.
- TNEC Final Report, p. 89 of that report: the scale-efficiency view 'finds scant support' (p. 89), cited in Hayek's note 3 (p. 96). Kind: official inquiry, the nearest thing to data in these five chapters. Hayek stresses the committee 'cannot be accused of an undue liberal bias' (p. 89). Editor adds that Roosevelt's 1938 message, the source of the ch. 1 epigraph, was the impetus for the committee (p. 96).
- Clair Wilcox, TNEC Monograph No. 21, p. 314 (p. 89), cited in Hayek's note 4 (p. 96). Kind: quotation from an official monograph. **Editor's correction**: Hayek gave the publication date as 1940; it is 1941 (p. 96). Hayek's block quotation contains an elision, so its wording is not continuous in the original.
- German cartel policy since 1878: protection, inducements, compulsion (p. 90). Kind: historical example, no source.
- Sombart as populariser of 'monopoly capitalism' (p. 90). Kind: attribution, no citation; editor cross-refers to ch. 1 note 13 (p. 96).
- Reinhold Niebuhr on Germany as the most advanced form of modern civilisation (p. 90), cited in Hayek's note 6 (p. 96). Kind: quotation illustrating a commonplace.
- British industry competitive until 1931, monopolised after (p. 90). Kind: historical assertion from recent memory ('only a dozen years since'), no figures. The editor's note 7 explains the 1931 crisis and the general tariff (pp. 96-97). Hayek concedes the 1920s 'suffered severely' but says they compare 'not unfavorably' with the 1930s (p. 90).
- The engineer watching a few dials (p. 92). Kind: analogy for how prices economise on knowledge.
- The price mechanism as register and guide (p. 92). Kind: mechanism argument, stated in a page with no empirical support; referred to nothing.
- British car industry and universal electricity (p. 93). Kind: hypothetical examples; Hayek says he does not know whether they are true.
- German and Italian motor roads (p. 95). Kind: example conceded and then reinterpreted; rests on personal observation of light traffic. Hayek's note 8 adds, as he corrects proofs, that maintenance on them has been suspended (p. 97).
- The countryside lover, health enthusiast, motorist, efficiency fanatic and craft idealist (p. 95). Kind: illustrative types.
- Where language outruns evidence: 'devoid of foundation' (p. 88) against a single American inquiry; 'there can be no doubt' about German policy (p. 91); 'the only method' and 'no other system even promises' (pp. 91-92); 'It is no exaggeration to say' that industry would never have developed under planning (p. 92), a counterfactual; 'incredibly clumsy, primitive, and limited in scope' (p. 92). The historical-order argument assumes away other causes of German and American cartelisation (scale of new heavy industries, banking structure, railways).

Concessions and qualifications recorded:
- The growth of monopoly and the shrinking field of competition are 'not disputed' as historical fact (p. 88).
- 'We cannot here investigate this question in detail and must be content to accept the best evidence available' (p. 89).
- Economies of size do exist in some fields ('where they exist', in the monograph Hayek quotes) (p. 89).
- Britain in the 1920s suffered from 'incompatible policies' on wages and money (p. 90).
- For England the view that policy merely follows facts has 'a certain appearance of truth' (p. 91).
- That complexity creates problems needing central planning is true 'in a sense': the problems of a modern town, of close contiguity in space, and of public utilities are not adequately solved by competition (p. 91). State action accepted: town planning and public-utility regulation or provision.
- The monopoly-for-new-technique argument is 'not necessarily fraudulent' and the stock reply does not dispose of all cases (p. 93).
- Compulsory standardisation or prohibition of variety might raise abundance more than enough to compensate for restricted choice; an invention might some day be usable only if all adopt it at once (p. 93).
- Preserving freedom may cost 'a possible immediate gain', and the price of variety 'may sometimes be high' in the short run (pp. 93-94).
- It cannot be asserted that the sacrifice will be rewarded in all instances; compulsion 'in a particular instance' may do no harm (p. 94).
- Inventions have given us tremendous power (p. 94).
- Almost every technical ideal of the experts could be realised quickly if made the sole aim (p. 94).
- A planned society 'would certainly further some objectives more than is the case at present'; there are 'good things which the people of these countries owe entirely to planning' (p. 95).
- The motor roads do not represent a kind of planning impossible in a liberal society (p. 95): state road-building is again accepted.
- Single-minded idealists have 'great pragmatic value' in a free society and deserve 'just admiration' (p. 96).
- The market's checks on individual effort are 'impersonal, and often unintelligible' (p. 96): an admission that market outcomes cannot always be explained or justified to those affected.

Editor versus author in the notes:
- Notes 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 are Hayek's (with editorial additions in 3 and 4); notes 1, 5 and 7 are the editor's (pp. 96-97).
- The only correction is the Wilcox date (p. 96).

## Quotes

- "Far from being appropriate only to comparatively simple conditions, it is the very complexity of the division of labor under modern conditions which makes competition the only method by which such coordination can be adequately brought about." (p. 91)
- "It enables entrepreneurs, by watching the movement of comparatively few prices, as an engineer watches the hands of a few dials, to adjust their activities to those of their fellows." (p. 92)
- "The more complicated the whole, the more dependent we become on that division of knowledge between individuals whose separate efforts are coordinated by the impersonal mechanism for transmitting the relevant information known by us as the price system." (p. 92)
- "But the argument for freedom is precisely that we ought to leave room for the unforeseeable free growth." (p. 94)
- "While there is nothing in modern technological developments which forces us toward comprehensive economic planning, there is a great deal in them which makes infinitely more dangerous the power a planning authority would possess." (p. 94)
- "It would be foolish to deny that the instances of planned or semiplanned societies which we know do furnish illustrations in point, good things which the people of these countries owe entirely to planning." (p. 95)
- "His plea is for a method which effects such coordination without the need for an omniscient dictator." (p. 96)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Redeems the Introduction's promise to deal with the 'alleged inevitability of planning' and its claim that no objective facts make socialism inevitable (p. 59); restates ch. 1's thesis that ideas, not facts, changed the world (p. 64).
- The price system as register (p. 92) completes ch. 3's remark that controlled prices cease to register changes in circumstances (pp. 83-84). This is the book's short statement of the knowledge problem; note that it is an efficiency argument about coordination, whereas the book's announced theme is freedom. Synthesis should track which later arguments depend on it.
- The requirement that producers 'cannot control' prices (p. 92) means monopoly, whether private or state-fostered, disables the mechanism; this ties the anti-monopoly tasks of ch. 1 (p. 68) and ch. 3 (p. 84) (p. 86) to the knowledge argument.
- The concession about towns and public utilities (p. 91) extends ch. 3's list of cases where competition must be supplemented (pp. 84-85).
- Germany as prototype where cartelisation was policy (pp. 90-91) continues the German-provenance thesis of the Introduction (pp. 57-58) and ch. 1 (pp. 70-71). Sombart reappears from ch. 1 (p. 70).
- The specialist and the conflict of ends (pp. 94-96) lead directly into ch. 5's argument that no agreed scale of values exists; 'omniscient dictator' (p. 96) anticipates chs. 5 and 10.
- The engineering cast of mind (p. 93) picks up ch. 1's remark on habits of thought of the natural scientist and engineer (p. 69).
- My observation, a tension: Hayek rejects historical inevitability (p. 91) while relying, in ch. 3, on quasi-inevitable sequences from partial to total control (p. 86). Both are consistent only if the latter are conditional on opinion staying as it is.
- My observation, a second tension: the freedom-over-gain argument (pp. 93-94) justifies freedom by long-run progress, but then says freedom should prevail even when no such reward follows. Whether freedom is for Hayek an instrumental or a final value is left open here; compare ch. 1 (pp. 66-67), where freedom is praised for its fruits, and ch. 2 (p. 74), where it is defined without reference to them.
- 'Planning' is used on p. 91 for German cartel and tariff policy, which is narrower than ch. 3's 'central direction of all economic activity according to a single plan' (p. 82). The term stretches when Hayek needs historical instances.

## Open questions

- Does the knowledge argument (pp. 91-92) show that central planning cannot work, or only that it works worse than competition? The text says 'clumsy, primitive, and limited in scope' (p. 92), which is a comparative claim, yet the surrounding language ('only method') is absolute.
- Is the argument addressed to market socialism, where a centre sets or simulates prices? Ch. 3's footnote says no fundamental difference (p. 88); does the book ever argue this on knowledge grounds?
- How much of the complexity argument survives if the relevant comparison is not competition versus total planning but competition plus the wide range of state action Hayek himself accepts in ch. 3 (pp. 83-85) and here (p. 91)?
- The evidence against technological concentration is one American inquiry of 1941 and an unperformed English one (p. 89). Is the claim that monopoly is mainly policy-made sustained anywhere else in the book, and does Hayek consider natural monopoly beyond the passing reference to public utilities (p. 91)?
- Where compulsory coordination would benefit everyone (p. 93), Hayek prefers freedom on the ground of unforeseeable growth. By what principle are the accepted cases of compulsion in ch. 3, such as sanitary rules and limits on hours (p. 84), distinguished from the rejected ones?
- The motor roads are both 'owed entirely to planning' and 'not a kind of planning not equally possible in a liberal society' (p. 95). What then distinguishes large state investment decisions in a liberal society from planning?
- If the market's checks are 'often unintelligible' (p. 96), what does Hayek say later (chs. 8, 14) about why people should submit to them?
