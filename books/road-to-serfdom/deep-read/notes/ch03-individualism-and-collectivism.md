# ch03: Individualism and Collectivism (pp. 80–88)

Scope note: the chapter text runs from the heading on p. 80 to p. 87; footnotes run pp. 87-88. Unbracketed footnotes are Hayek's; bracketed ones are the editor's (Caldwell). This is the definitional chapter of the book: it fixes what is attacked and, at more length than anywhere else in these opening chapters, what state activity is accepted.

## Question this chapter answers

What exactly is meant by socialism, collectivism and planning, and what is the liberal alternative, so that the dispute between planners and liberals can be stated precisely?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| socialism (as ends) | p. 80 | The ideals of social justice, greater equality and security: the 'ultimate aims'. Hayek does not attack these as such. |
| socialism (as method) | p. 80 | Abolition of private enterprise and of private ownership of the means of production, and creation of a 'planned economy' in which a central planning body replaces the entrepreneur working for profit. This is the sense the book uses. |
| collectivism | p. 81 | The genus: the methods of central economic direction, usable for any distributive end (equalitarian, racial, party, aristocratic). Socialism is one species, so whatever is true of collectivism is true of socialism. Includes 'all types of planned economy, whatever the end of planning' (p. 82). |
| planning (general sense) | p. 82 | Handling common problems rationally and with foresight. In this sense everyone who is not a fatalist is a planner and Hayek has no objection. |
| planning (the planners' sense; the book's target) | p. 82 | Central direction of all economic activity according to a single plan, laying down how society's resources are to be consciously directed to serve particular ends in a definite way. Equivalently, the planning needed to realise any given distributive ideal (p. 82), and 'planning against competition' (p. 87). |
| 'the liberal plan' | p. 82 | Designing the most rational permanent framework within which different persons conduct their activities according to their own plans. Not a plan about who should have what. |
| planning for competition | p. 87 | The 'very necessary planning' required to make competition as effective and beneficial as possible; explicitly outside the book's criticism and outside its scope. |
| liberalism / economic liberalism | p. 83 | The case for making the best possible use of competition as the means of coordinating human efforts; not laissez faire, not leaving things as they are. Opposed to competition being supplanted by inferior methods of coordination. |
| competition | p. 83 | A method of coordinating individual efforts, a 'principle of social organization'; superior because usually most efficient and, more importantly, because it is the only method that adjusts activities to each other without coercive or arbitrary intervention of authority. Requires free prices, freedom to produce and trade, and open entry (p. 83). |
| laissez faire | p. 83 | A 'dogmatic' attitude with which opposition to planning must not be confused. |
| legal framework | p. 83 | The carefully thought-out, continuously adjusted body of law (property, contract, corporations, patents, fraud) that competition needs in order to work beneficially (pp. 83-85). |
| 'socialists of all parties' / 'socialists of the Left and the Right' | pp. 82-83, p. 85 | All who want competition replaced by a directed economy, whatever their party; united by hostility to competition. |
| competitive socialism | p. 87 | The academic proposal to abolish private property while retaining competition; dismissed in Hayek's note 4 as resting on a delusion (pp. 87-88). |
| individualism | title | Appears in the title only; the chapter's working contrast is competition versus central direction. The ch. 1 definition (p. 65) is not restated. |

Shifts to note: 'socialism' was a climate of opinion in the Introduction (p. 59) and a historical movement in ch. 2 (p. 74); here it is narrowed to a method (p. 80). 'Planning' is split into a harmless and a condemned sense (p. 82) and Hayek warns he will use the bare word for the condemned sense (p. 87).

## Leading propositions

- 'Socialism' names both a set of ultimate ends (social justice, equality, security) and a particular method (abolition of private enterprise and private ownership of the means of production, central planning) (p. 80).
- Many who value the ends of socialism as much as socialists do reject it because of the danger its methods pose to other values; the dispute is largely about means, not ends, though whether the ends can all be achieved together is also at issue (p. 81).
- Central direction of economic activity is necessary if the distribution of income is to conform to current ideas of social justice (p. 81).
- The same method is equally necessary for, and equally serviceable to, any other distributive aim, including rule by a racial elite, a party or an aristocracy (p. 81).
- The method is better called collectivism, with socialism a species of it; all the consequences examined in the book follow from the methods of collectivism irrespective of the ends (p. 81).
- Socialism is the most important species of collectivism and the one that persuaded liberal-minded people to accept regimentation of economic life again (p. 81).
- Central planning owes its appeal largely to the vagueness of the word (p. 82).
- In the general sense of rational foresight everyone is a planner; the planners mean something else: central direction of all economic activity according to a single plan (p. 82).
- The real dispute is whether the holder of coercive power should confine himself to creating conditions in which individuals can plan best, or whether resources must be centrally directed according to a blueprint (p. 82).
- Opposition to that kind of planning is not dogmatic laissez faire (p. 83).
- Competition needs a carefully thought-out legal framework, and neither existing nor past legal rules are free from grave defects (p. 83).
- Where conditions for effective competition cannot be created, other methods of guiding economic activity must be used (p. 83).
- Competition is superior chiefly because it is the only method of coordination that needs no coercive or arbitrary intervention of authority (p. 83).
- Competition requires that parties be free to buy and sell at any price, that anyone be free to produce and trade, and that entry to trades be open to all on equal terms (p. 83).
- Any control of prices or quantities of particular commodities deprives competition of its coordinating power, because prices then cease to register changes in circumstances (pp. 83-84).
- Restrictions on methods of production that bind all producers equally, such as bans on poisonous substances, limits on working hours and sanitary requirements, are fully compatible with competition; the only question is whether benefits exceed costs (p. 84).
- An extensive system of social services is compatible with competition so long as it is not organised so as to make competition ineffective over wide fields (p. 84).
- The positive requirements of competition, including money, markets, channels of information and above all an appropriate legal system, have been neglected; defects in corporation and patent law have destroyed competition in many spheres (p. 84).
- Where owners cannot be charged for damage or paid for services (roads, signposts, deforestation, harmful farming methods, factory smoke and noise), the price system fails and direct regulation by authority must substitute for it (pp. 84-85).
- These tasks give the state 'a wide and unquestioned field' of activity; in no rationally defensible system would the state do nothing (p. 85).
- The modern movement for planning is a movement against competition as such (p. 85).
- The first result of general hostility to competition is not central planning but a syndicalist or corporative organisation of industry run by independent monopolies of capital and labour, which is worse than either system (p. 86).
- From that stage the only alternative to restoring competition is state control of the monopolies, which must become progressively more complete and detailed (p. 86).
- There is no stable middle way: competition and central direction are alternative principles, and a mixture works worse than either consistently applied (pp. 86-87).
- Planning and competition can be combined only by planning for competition, never by planning against it (p. 87).
- The book's criticism is directed solely at planning against competition (p. 87).

## Arguments

- **Means-neutrality argument (why the target is collectivism, not socialist ends).**
  **Premises**: (p. 81) making income distribution conform to any chosen standard requires central direction of economic activity; (p. 81) the method is the same whether the standard is equalitarian or favours a racial elite or a party; (unstated) the political and moral consequences of an economic system depend on its method of coordination and not on the intentions of those who operate it.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 81) everything true of collectivism as such applies to socialism, and the consequences examined in the book follow from the method irrespective of the ends. This is the hinge that lets evidence from Nazi Germany count against democratic socialists.

- **Definition-of-the-dispute argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 82) everyone favours rational foresight; (p. 82) the planners reject the liberal framework as 'no plan' because it does not settle who gets what; (p. 82) they demand central direction of all economic activity under a single plan; (pp. 82-83) their appropriation of the word 'planning' suggests but does not prove that theirs is the only rational method.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 82) the dispute is not about whether to plan but about who plans: individuals within a framework, or a central authority.

- **Case for competition.**
  **Premises**: (p. 83) where effective competition can be created it guides individual efforts better than any other method; (p. 83) it is in most circumstances the most efficient method known; (p. 83) it is the only method that adjusts activities to one another without coercive or arbitrary intervention of authority; (p. 83) it lets individuals decide for themselves whether an occupation's prospects compensate for its risks; (unstated) being subject to impersonal market outcomes is not being coerced, whereas being subject to an authority's allocation is.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 83) economic liberalism opposes the replacement of competition by inferior methods of coordination.

- **The criterion for permissible intervention (price-signal argument).**
  **Premises**: (pp. 83-84) prices coordinate only if they register all relevant changes in circumstances; (p. 83) control of the price or quantity of a particular commodity stops them doing so; (p. 84) rules about methods of production that apply equally to all producers raise costs but leave prices free to adjust; (p. 84) social services need not impair competition if suitably organised.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 84) general, equal rules and services are compatible with competition and are to be judged case by case on costs and benefits; direct control of particular prices and quantities is not compatible. This is the nearest thing in these chapters to a demarcation test.

- **Market-failure argument for state provision and regulation.**
  **Premises**: (p. 84) competition and private property are useful only on condition that owners gain from all benefits and bear all damage their property causes; (pp. 84-85) for some services payment cannot be exacted and for some harms the cost cannot be charged; (pp. 84-85) there private calculation and social welfare diverge.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 85) in such cases some substitute for the price mechanism must be found, including direct regulation by authority; but (p. 85) this does not show that competition should be suppressed where it can be made to work.

- **No-middle-way argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 85) the planning movement aims at displacing competition, not supplementing it; (p. 86) partial suppression yields industry monopolies that exploit consumers and defeat the planners' own aims; (p. 86) once there, the only choices are restoring competition or ever more detailed state control; (pp. 86-87) competition and central direction are alternative tools for the same problem and each is a poor tool when incomplete; (unstated) a mixed system cannot be held in equilibrium by political choice, because each control creates the need for the next.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 87) a mixture of the two means neither will work and the result is worse than either consistently applied; the only coherent combination is planning for competition. My observation: the premise that 'competition can bear some admixture of regulation' (p. 86) and the long list of accepted interventions (pp. 83-85) show that Hayek's own position is a mixture in the everyday sense; what he denies is a mixture in which authority sets particular prices and quantities or allocates resources. The stronger claim that any such mixture is unstable is asserted here, with the corporatist stage as its only support.

- **Against competitive socialism (Hayek's note 4).**
  **Premises**: (pp. 87-88) control over all productive resources cannot be assumed without deciding for whom and by whom they are used; (p. 88) under such schemes central planning only takes more roundabout forms.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 88) the competitive element would be 'little more than a sham'. The argument is referred to Hayek's 1940 Economica paper, not made here.

## Evidence offered

- Epigraph from Élie Halévy on socialists believing in freedom and organisation (p. 80). Kind: motto; editor supplies the source (p. 87).
- Adam Smith's 1755 memorandum, via Dugald Stewart, on governments obliged to be 'oppressive and tyrannical' (pp. 81-82). Kind: appeal to authority. Hayek's note 2 gives the source; the editor adds the reprint and page (p. 87).
- Adam Smith on public works whose profit could never repay any individual (p. 85). Kind: appeal to authority, used here to support state activity. Uncited by Hayek; the editor supplies the reference to *Wealth of Nations*, book 5, chapter 1, part 3 (p. 87).
- Examples of compatible regulation: poisonous substances, working hours, sanitary arrangements (p. 84). Kind: illustrative cases.
- Examples of price-system failure: signposts, roads, deforestation, some methods of farming, factory smoke and noise (p. 85). Kind: illustrative cases supporting a mechanism argument about uncompensated costs and benefits.
- Corporation and patent law as causes of the destruction of competition (p. 84). Kind: assertion, hedged ('strong arguments can be advanced'), no cases given.
- Corporative organisation 'in wide fields has already existed for some time' (p. 86). Kind: appeal to current observation, no cases named.
- A weekly magazine on British leaders thinking in terms of 'national development by controlled monopolies' (p. 86). Kind: press quotation, unnamed by Hayek; the editor identifies it as The Spectator, 3 March 1939, p. 337 (p. 88).
- The price-signal mechanism (pp. 83-84) and the monopoly-to-state-control sequence (p. 86). Kind: mechanism arguments; the central support of the chapter.
- Where language outruns evidence: 'the only method' of non-coercive coordination (p. 83); 'the inevitable first result' (p. 86), an inevitability claim in a book that denies inevitabilities; 'neither will really work' (p. 87), offered with no worked case; 'It is this stage we are rapidly approaching' (p. 86). The dismissal of competitive socialism as unlikely 'to recommend itself to practical politicians' (p. 87) is a prediction, not an argument.

Concessions and qualifications recorded (state activity explicitly accepted):
- Many opponents of socialism value its ultimate ends no less than socialists do (p. 81); Hayek places himself with them by implication.
- It 'may, perhaps, seem unfair' to use 'socialism' for the method when for many it names an ideal (p. 81).
- An economist is 'the last person who could object to planning' in the general sense (p. 82).
- The liberal position is not laissez faire and is not 'an argument for leaving things just as they are' (p. 83).
- Existing and past legal rules have 'grave defects' (p. 83).
- Where competition cannot be made effective 'we must resort to other methods of guiding economic activity' (p. 83).
- The use of competition 'admits of' some kinds of coercive interference and 'even requires certain kinds of government action' (p. 83).
- Regulation of production methods applying equally to all: prohibition or control of poisonous substances, limits on working hours, sanitary requirements. They impose costs but 'may be well worth while' (p. 84).
- 'An extensive system of social services', on condition that it does not make competition ineffective over wide fields (p. 84).
- State organisation of money, markets and channels of information, 'some of which can never be adequately provided by private enterprise' (p. 84).
- A legal system designed both to preserve competition and to make it work beneficially; reform of property rights, corporation law and patent law (p. 84).
- Provision of roads and signposts; regulation of deforestation, farming methods, smoke and noise; in general 'direct regulation by authority' where costs and benefits cannot be priced (pp. 84-85).
- Smith's public works and institutions (p. 85).
- Prevention of fraud and deception, 'including exploitation of ignorance', called a great and not yet accomplished object of legislation (p. 85).
- 'A wide and unquestioned field for state activity' (p. 85).
- Competition 'can bear some admixture of regulation' (p. 86).
- Complete centralisation 'still appalls most people' (p. 86): Hayek grants that few actually want the endpoint.
- The planning needed to make competition effective is 'very necessary' but is not discussed in the book (p. 87).
- 'Planning' is 'a very good word meriting a better fate' (p. 87).
- Some academic socialists are 'animated by the same fear of the extinction of freedom in a centrally planned society' (p. 87).

Editor versus author in the notes:
- Note 2 is Hayek's (source of the Smith memorandum), with an editorial addition (p. 87). Note 3 is wholly the editor's, supplying a reference Hayek omitted (p. 87).
- Note 4 is Hayek's dismissal of competitive socialism; the editor identifies the paper as 'Socialist Calculation: The Competitive Solution' and the targets as Dickinson and Lange and Taylor, and points to his own introduction for its significance (p. 88).
- Note 5 is the editor's, sourcing the magazine quotation Hayek left anonymous (p. 88).

## Quotes

- "Nearly all the points which are disputed between socialists and liberals concern the methods common to all forms of collectivism and not the particular ends for which socialists want to use them; and all the consequences with which we shall be concerned in this book follow from the methods of collectivism irrespective of the ends for which they are used." (p. 81)
- "The liberal argument is in favor of making the best possible use of the forces of competition as a means of coordinating human efforts, not an argument for leaving things just as they are." (p. 83)
- "And it regards competition as superior not only because it is in most circumstances the most efficient method known but even more because it is the only method by which our activities can be adjusted to each other without coercive or arbitrary intervention of authority." (p. 83)
- "To prohibit the use of certain poisonous substances or to require special precautions in their use, to limit working hours or to require certain sanitary arrangements, is fully compatible with the preservation of competition." (p. 84)
- "Nor is the preservation of competition incompatible with an extensive system of social services—so long as the organization of these services is not designed in such a way as to make competition ineffective over wide fields." (p. 84)
- "In no system that could be rationally defended would the state just do nothing." (p. 85)
- "Or, to express it differently, planning and competition can be combined only by planning for competition but not by planning against competition." (p. 87)
- "It is of the utmost importance to the argument of this book for the reader to keep in mind that the planning against which all our criticism is directed is solely the planning against competition—the planning which is to be substituted for competition." (p. 87)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Fulfils ch. 1's programme: the framework-building liberalism of pp. 67-68 is here given content (pp. 83-85), and 'conscious direction' (p. 69) becomes the definition of planning (p. 82).
- Narrows the 'socialism' of the Introduction (p. 59) and ch. 2 (p. 74). My observation: the Introduction's claim that all opinion-formers are socialists (p. 59) and ch. 2's claim that democratic socialism is unachievable (p. 78) are only supported by later chapters if 'socialism' there means central direction of all economic activity under a single plan (p. 82). A socialist who seeks equality through taxation and social services within competition falls outside the definition, and by p. 84 inside what Hayek accepts.
- Scope of the target: the definition says 'all economic activity according to a single plan' (p. 82), a total system. The no-middle-way argument (pp. 86-87) is what extends the critique from the total system to partial measures. If that argument fails, the book's later chapters apply only to comprehensive planning. This is the main load-bearing joint for synthesis.
- The means-neutrality premise (p. 81) underwrites the use of German and Russian evidence in chs. 10-13.
- The demarcation test, general equal rules versus control of particular prices and quantities (pp. 83-84), anticipates the Rule of Law distinction of ch. 6 between formal rules and substantive commands.
- 'Extensive system of social services' (p. 84) should be read with ch. 9 (Security and Freedom) for how much Hayek actually concedes.
- The corporatist or syndicalist stage (p. 86) links to ch. 4's account of monopoly as a product of policy (pp. 89-91) and to ch. 13.
- The role of prices as registers of changing circumstances (pp. 83-84) is developed in ch. 4 (pp. 91-92).
- Tension with the anti-inevitability stance of the Introduction (p. 56) (p. 58): 'inevitable first result' (p. 86) and 'must become progressively more complete' (p. 86) are conditional inevitabilities (given continued hostility to competition).
- 'Coercion' is used for the state's interference and for authority's allocation (p. 83) but market outcomes are treated as non-coercive by definition; this rests on the ch. 2 definition of freedom (p. 74).
- The individualism of the chapter title is never defined here; the reader must carry the definition from p. 65.

## Open questions

- Is the demarcation between planning for and against competition a difference of kind, as claimed (p. 87), or of degree? The accepted list (limits on hours, social services, regulation of externalities, state provision of services) already alters prices and quantities indirectly; Hayek's own test asks only that restrictions not be used 'as an indirect way of controlling prices and quantities' (p. 84). How is that intent to be identified?
- What supports the claim that a mixture is worse than either pure system (p. 87)? Does any later chapter give more than the corporatist example? The post-war mixed economies are the obvious test case for synthesis.
- Is competition really 'the only method' of non-coercive coordination (p. 83)? What of voluntary cooperation, custom, professional norms? Hayek's 'spontaneous forces' in ch. 1 (p. 67) seem wider than market competition.
- The accepted cost-benefit test for regulation (p. 84): who applies it, and by what procedure, if not a planning authority making discretionary judgments? Check ch. 6.
- How 'extensive' may social services be before they 'make competition ineffective over wide fields' (p. 84)? No measure is given.
- Hayek admits the book does not discuss the positive programme of planning for competition (p. 87). How much of the practical question (what should the state do?) is therefore left unanswered by the book as a whole?
- Competitive (market) socialism is dismissed in a footnote (pp. 87-88) by reference to another paper. Since it is the one socialist proposal that claims to keep prices and choice, does the book's argument reach it at all?
