# ch11: The End of Truth (pp. 168–178)

## Question this chapter answers

Why must a totalitarian (planned) system control not only action but belief, and not only beliefs about values but beliefs about facts? And what does that do to truth, language, science and the growth of reason? Continues from ch10's closing remark that selection by conformity bears on all the virtues grouped under truthfulness (p. 165).

Epigraph: E. H. Carr, that the nationalization of thought has gone pari passu with the nationalization of industry (p. 168). **Editor's correction**: Carr actually wrote nationalization of opinion, not of thought (p. 176, note 1).

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Propaganda | p. 168 | Not objectionable or distinctive in itself; neither propaganda nor its techniques are peculiar to totalitarianism. What changes its nature is that all of it serves one goal under a single control, making its effect different in kind, not just magnitude, from propaganda by independent competing agencies (p. 168). At p. 176 used neutrally: new ideas are put to the test of argument and propaganda. |
| Gleichschaltung | p. 168 | The coordination of all minds in one direction (p. 168). Editor: usually translated coordination; Nazi coordination of all activities in support of the state (pp. 176-177, note 2). |
| Totalitarian propaganda (object and scope) | p. 169 | Propaganda that cannot confine itself to values but must extend to questions of fact (p. 169). |
| Myth / official doctrine | p. 170 | A theory, i.e. assertions about connections between facts, constructed (not necessarily consciously) to rationalize the planner's likes and dislikes, which becomes part of the governing creed and must not be questioned (p. 170). Examples: racial doctrine, Blut und Boden, Plato's noble lies, Sorel's myths, Mussolini's corporative state (pp. 170-171). |
| Perversion of language | p. 171 | Keeping old words while changing their meaning so that allegiance transfers from old values to new; a continuous process that ends with words as empty shells used only for emotional association (pp. 171-172). |
| Liberty / freedom (the perverted sense) | p. 171 | "Collective freedom", "planned freedom": not the freedom of members of society but the unlimited freedom of the planner; the confusion of freedom with power carried to the extreme (p. 171). Hayek's own sense is left as "liberty as we understand it" (p. 171). |
| Truth | p. 174 | Old meaning: something to be found, with the individual conscience the sole arbiter of whether evidence (or the standing of those proclaiming it) warrants belief. Totalitarian meaning: something laid down by authority, believed in the interest of unity, alterable as the organized effort requires (pp. 174-175). |
| Intellectual freedom / freedom of thought | p. 175 | Its value is not that everyone thinks independently but that any cause or idea may be argued by somebody; the prime mover of intellectual progress (p. 176). |
| Growth of reason | p. 176 | A social, interpersonal process arising from the interaction of individuals with different knowledge and views; unpredictable in its results, hence unplannable (p. 176). |
| Individualism | p. 176 | Here an epistemic attitude: humility before the social process and tolerance of other opinions; opposite of the intellectual hubris behind comprehensive direction (p. 176). |
| "Conscious" control | p. 176 | The collectivist demand; said to confuse individual reason, which alone can consciously control anything, with the interpersonal process by which reason grows (p. 176). |

## Leading propositions

- To make everyone serve a single system of ends efficiently, it is not enough to force them; they must be made to believe in those ends as their own (p. 168).
- The feeling of oppression in totalitarian countries is much less acute than outsiders imagine, because governments largely succeed in making people think as they want (p. 168).
- Propaganda is not peculiar to totalitarianism; monopoly and coordination of it are, and they change its effect in kind (p. 168).
- If propaganda only taught a comprehensive moral code, the sole problem would be whether the code is good or bad; such a code, though repellent, is not necessarily entirely antimoral and might even appeal to stern conservative moralists (p. 169).
- The deeper damage is to truth: totalitarian propaganda must extend from values to facts, and so undermines a foundation of all morals (p. 169).
- It must extend to facts for two reasons: official values must be justified by causal claims linking them to values already held; and ends and means are never cleanly separable, so people must agree with the factual views on which particular measures rest (p. 169).
- The planner's code of values does not exist in advance; it is created with the particular decisions, which must then be publicly rationalized through theories that become official doctrine (p. 170).
- Myth-making need not be conscious; the leader embraces theories that rationalize prejudices he shares with many (p. 170).
- The most effective technique for getting new values accepted is to keep the old words and change their meaning; liberty is the worst sufferer (p. 171).
- Wherever liberty as we understand it has been destroyed, this has almost always been done in the name of some new freedom (p. 171).
- Mannheim's planned or collective freedom is the unlimited freedom of the planner (p. 171).
- Because much of the plan's value code is only implicit in it, the plan in every detail and every government act must be exempt from criticism; doubt is treated as disloyalty or sabotage (p. 172).
- All information is then selected by its probable effect on loyalty; the situation is permanently and universally what wartime censorship is partially and temporarily (pp. 172-173).
- Control extends to all sciences, including the most abstract; the human sciences become factories of official myths (p. 173).
- Totalitarian systems share a dislike of abstract thought and condemn any activity pursued for its own sake (pp. 173-174).
- These are not accidental by-products but direct results of the wish to direct everything by a unitary conception (p. 174).
- The word truth itself changes meaning (pp. 174-175).
- Contempt for intellectual liberty is already found among collectivist intellectuals in still-liberal countries (p. 175).
- The argument that mass opinion is shaped anyway, so should be shaped deliberately, is confused: it makes a difference whether everyone must follow the same lead (pp. 175-176).
- The growth of reason is an unpredictable social process; to plan it is a contradiction in terms; attempting it produces stagnation of thought and decline of reason (p. 176).
- Collectivism sets out to make reason supreme and ends by destroying it; individualism is humility before the social process (p. 176).

## Concessions and qualifications

- Propaganda and its techniques are not peculiar to totalitarianism (p. 168).
- People in totalitarian countries feel much less oppressed than liberals imagine (p. 168).
- A totalitarian moral code is not necessarily entirely antimoral; some features may appeal to stern conservative moralists (p. 169).
- On values and moral convictions the individual always will conform more or less to the views ruling his community (p. 169). So conformity of values is treated as normal; it is the extension to facts that is distinctive.
- Myth creation need not be conscious (p. 170); the change of word meanings may be employed consciously or unconsciously (p. 172).
- Mannheim "at least warns us" about his redefinition (p. 171).
- Wartime in liberal countries already involves the same control in some fields (p. 173).
- It is sometimes difficult to explain why particular doctrines are proscribed (p. 173).
- The desire to impose a salutary creed is not new or peculiar to our time (p. 175).
- The large elitist concession: it is probably true that the great majority are rarely capable of independent thought, accept ready-made views, and that freedom of thought is of direct significance only to a small minority (p. 175). He denies only the inference that anyone may select that minority or determine what others should believe (pp. 175-176).
- Standing of the person asserting something is admitted, in a parenthesis, as a legitimate ground of belief alongside evidence (p. 174).

## Arguments

- **A1. From single plan to official creed about facts.**
  **Premises**: (p. 168) a plan serving a single system of ends works efficiently only if people adopt those ends as their own + (p. 169) acceptance of ends requires justification through causal claims + (p. 169) ends and means are not cleanly separable + (p. 170) the planner's value scale is created ad hoc with particular decisions and must be rationalized publicly + (p. 172) much of the value code is only implicit in the plan, so the whole plan must be shielded.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 172) the enforced creed must comprise all the views about facts on which the plan rests; criticism and doubt must be suppressed.
  **Unstated premises**: that active, unhesitating support (not mere compliance or majority consent) is a functional requirement of planning; that the plan is comprehensive, covering the whole of social activity (the argument is stated for "a totalitarian system", p. 168, and assumes ch05's claim that a plan implies a complete ethical code, recalled at p. 169); that criticism weakens rather than improves execution.

- **A2. From official creed to the end of truth.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 172-173) information is published or suppressed solely by its effect on loyalty + (p. 173) no field is exempt, since any field may yield unfavorable comparisons or doubts + (p. 174) once science serves a class, community or state, argument only vindicates received beliefs.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 174-175) truth ceases to mean something found by inquiry and becomes something laid down by authority; cynicism about truth and loss of independent inquiry follow.

- **A3. Language.**
  **Premises**: (p. 171) new values are most easily accepted if presented as what the people always believed + (p. 171) this is done by keeping old words with new meanings + (p. 172) the process is continuous.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 172) political and moral language is despoiled until words can denote a thing or its opposite; rational discussion across the divide becomes impossible.

- **A4. Against deliberate shaping of opinion.**
  **Premises**: (p. 175) granted that most people take their opinions ready-made + (p. 176) the value of intellectual freedom lies in the possibility that any idea may be argued by someone, not in universal independent thought + (p. 176) nobody is competent to select who shall have that freedom.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 176) it makes all the difference whether people follow many leads or must all follow one; the manipulability of opinion does not justify directing it.

- **A5. The epistemic argument (the chapter's philosophical core).**
  **Premises**: (p. 176) reason grows through interaction of individuals with different knowledge and views + (p. 176) the results of that process cannot be predicted, so we cannot know which views will assist it + (p. 176) to govern it by views now held is to limit it to them.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 176) planning the growth of mind is a contradiction in terms and must produce stagnation; collectivism, demanding conscious control, entails rule by some individual mind and so destroys the reason it meant to enthrone.
  **Unstated premise**: that "conscious control" of social development must mean control by one mind or one doctrine, rather than, say, collective deliberation that itself preserves dissent.

## Evidence offered

- **Mechanism argument**: A1 (pp. 168-172). No empirical support at the point of argument; the examples come after.
- **Illustrative psychological sketch**: the leader who dislikes Jews and admires the tall blond figure of the novels of his youth and so embraces a pseudoscientific theory (p. 170). A transparent portrait of Hitler; conjectural psychology, not evidence.
- **Historical example**: Blut und Boden as a myth combining values with causal beliefs, including a "probably erroneous" idea about country people as soldiers (p. 170). Editor's note explains the doctrine and attributes the term's introduction to Spengler (p. 177, note 3).
- **Appeal to theorists**: Plato's noble lies, Sorel's myths, Nazi racial doctrine, Mussolini's corporative state as serving the same purpose (p. 171). Editor glosses Plato and Sorel (p. 177, note 4). The assimilation of Plato to modern totalitarian theorists is asserted.
- **Quotation from an opponent**: Mannheim on planned freedom and on older conceptions of freedom as an obstacle (p. 171). **Editor's correction**: Hayek gave p. 377; the passage is on p. 379 (p. 177, note 6). The quoted sentence carries a bracketed sic after the word all (p. 171). My observation: Mannheim's sentence says earlier freedoms need not all be abolished; Hayek reads the reassurance as an admission. This is interpretation, and a hostile one.
- **Quotation from an ally**: Drucker on the new freedom (p. 177, note 7, Hayek's note). **Editor's correction**: the first part is on p. 79 and the last on p. 80 of Drucker, not p. 74 (p. 177, note 7). Note the Drucker quotation as Hayek gives it contains an ellipsis.
- **Title allusion**: Becker's New Liberties for Old (p. 171; p. 177, note 5).
- **Personal testimony**: one must have experienced it; the two brothers who can no longer communicate (p. 172); the climate is something one must personally experience (p. 175). Anecdote from his Austrian experience; not checkable.
- **Quotation from sympathizers reporting on Russia**: the Webbs on public expression of doubt as disloyalty in Russian enterprises (p. 172; p. 177, note 8). Strong evidence of its kind because the witnesses were admirers. But note it concerns doubts voiced while work is in progress within an enterprise; Hayek extends it to the whole social plan by inference (p. 172).
- **Assertion with appeal to experience**: history, law and economics have become factories of official myth in all totalitarian countries; "amply confirmed by experience" (p. 173). No instance cited.
- **Paired quotations, unsourced by Hayek**: attacks on relativity as Semitic and as contrary to dialectical materialism; attacks on mathematical statistics; continuity and bourgeois prejudice (pp. 173-174). No citations are given for these, by Hayek or the editor.
- **Quotation via the Webbs**: Soviet journal slogans, party in mathematics and purity of theory in surgery (p. 174; the editor supplies the page reference, p. 177, note 9).
- **German parallel**: the journal of the National-Socialist mathematicians; Lenard's German Physics (p. 174). Editor identifies Lenard and the work (p. 177, note 10).
- **Quotation, deliberately unattributed**: the chess exhortation, with the reader left to guess Germany or Russia (p. 174). **Editor's identification**: Krylenko, Soviet Commissar for Justice, 1932, cited in Souvarine (p. 177, note 11). The rhetorical device presumes interchangeability, which is the thing to be shown.
- **Quotation, attribution uncertain**: the Nazi minister of justice on scientific theories serving National Socialism (p. 174). **Editor's note**: it is not clear which of three ministers said it (pp. 177-178, note 12). Hayek gives no source.
- **Quotation from a British opponent**: J. G. Crowther defending the Inquisition as beneficial to science when it protects a rising class (p. 175; p. 178, note 13). Hayek calls this practically indistinguishable from the views that led to Nazi persecution of scientists (p. 175); a strong equation resting on one sentence.
- **Confident language outrunning evidence**: "everywhere among intellectuals who have embraced a collectivist faith" (p. 175); "equally abhorrent to the Nazis, our socialist intellectuals, and the communists" (p. 174); the oppression "openly advocated by people who pretend to speak for the scientists of liberal countries" (p. 175), with Crowther the only name.
- **Conceptual argument**: A5 on the growth of reason (p. 176). No evidence needed or offered; it is a philosophical claim.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch05**: the plan implies a complete ethical code that does not exist and cannot be settled democratically (recalled at pp. 169-170, "we have seen").
- **Depends on ch02**: freedom confused with power (p. 171) is the "new freedom" argument of The Great Utopia; here it is recast as a propaganda technique rather than an intellectual mistake. Shift: in ch02 the redefinition is a seductive error of socialists; here it is "perversion" used as an instrument (p. 171).
- **Depends on ch06 and ch08**: justice, law, right and equality are said to have already been shown to suffer the same reversal (p. 172).
- **Depends on ch10**: collectivist morals (p. 169) and the "not accidental by-products" formula (p. 174; ch10 p. 154). The parallel between individual conscience as arbiter of moral rules (ch10 p. 161) and as arbiter of evidence (p. 174) is deliberate.
- **Extends ch04 and ch07**: the knowledge argument for the market (dispersed knowledge, unpredictable results, competition as discovery) is here transposed to intellectual life (p. 176). The final paragraph ties the whole book's individualism to an epistemic stance, not only a moral one. This is the bridge to Hayek's later work; my observation.
- **Feeds ch12**: German philosophers and theoreticians of socialism prepared the perversion of freedom (p. 171).
- **Feeds ch13**: collectivists among our scientists and British intellectuals (pp. 173, 175).
- **Tension** (mine): Hayek concedes that most people take their views ready-made and that free thought matters directly only to a small minority (p. 175), which sits beside ch10's lowest-common-denominator passage (p. 156). His liberalism is not grounded in confidence in mass judgement but in the unplannability of progress. Compare with any democratic-theory material in ch05.
- **Tension** (mine): he says conformity on values is normal in any community (p. 169), yet ch05 and ch10 rest on the claim that no agreed value scale exists in a free society. The two are consistent only if communities share general moral rules but not a ranking of concrete ends.
- **Shift in "propaganda"**: neutral in principle at p. 168 and p. 176 (ideas are tested by argument and propaganda), pejorative when monopolized. Compare ch09 p. 151, where anticapitalist propaganda in a free society is blamed for changing the values of the young; there plural propaganda is already treated as harmful.
- **Slide of scope** (mine): the argument is framed for a totalitarian system (p. 168) but the conclusion is applied to "a planned or totalitarian system" (p. 174). As in ch10, the force against democratic planning depends on the earlier chapters' claim that comprehensive planning becomes totalitarian.

## Open questions

- Does A1 require comprehensive planning, or would it apply to partial planning and wartime mobilization in democracies? Hayek's wartime analogy (p. 173) suggests degrees; synthesis should ask where he thinks the threshold lies.
- How well does the prediction of control over abstract science hold up historically (Lysenko, Deutsche Physik) versus counter-cases (Soviet mathematics and physics flourishing under planning)? Hayek's evidence is selective quotation.
- Is the charge against Mannheim fair to Mannheim's actual argument? Later synthesis comparing books should check Man and Society directly.
- Hayek's own book redefines contested words (liberalism, planning, socialism, freedom). By what criterion is his usage restoration and his opponents' usage perversion? He appeals to what Europe always understood (via Drucker, p. 177, note 7) and to "liberty as we understand it" (p. 171), without a criterion.
- The concluding epistemic argument (p. 176): is it independent of the rest of the book? If reason's growth is unplannable, that is an argument against any enforced orthodoxy, including a liberal one. Does Hayek accept that implication elsewhere?
- What is the status of "the standing of those proclaiming it" as a warrant for belief (p. 174), and how does it differ from authority laying down truth?
- Editor's corrections accumulate in this chapter (Carr epigraph wording, Mannheim page, Drucker pages, one unattributable quotation). Worth noting for the overall assessment of Hayek's handling of sources.
