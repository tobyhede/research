Scope note: the unit file opens with the last footnotes of chapter 15 (p. 234) and ends with the heading of the appendix (p. 237); neither is covered here. The Conclusion runs pp. 234-236 with one editor's note (p. 236); the Bibliographical Note runs pp. 236-237 with one editor's note (p. 237). All propositions are Hayek's, 1944.

## Question this chapter answers

What has the book tried to do and deliberately not tried to do, and what attitude should readers take to the post-war task? In the Bibliographical Note: where can a reader find the wider literature, including the constructive side this book leaves out?

## Leading propositions

**Conclusion**

- The book's purpose was not to sketch a detailed program for a desirable future society (p. 234).
- Only on international affairs did it go a little beyond its essentially critical task, because a framework for long-term growth may soon have to be created there (pp. 234-235).
- Whatever is done after the war is only the start of a long and arduous process (p. 235).
- A detailed blueprint of a desirable internal order would probably be of little use at this stage, and it is doubtful anyone is competent to furnish one (p. 235).
- What matters now is agreement on certain principles and freedom from the errors of the recent past (p. 235).
- It is more important to clear away obstacles and release the creative energy of individuals than to devise more machinery for guiding and directing them (p. 235).
- The first need is to drop the belief that recent policy was all either wise or inevitable; much of it was very foolish (p. 235).
- Courage to make a new start, even at the cost of stepping back first, belongs to those who reject inevitable tendencies, not to preachers of a New Order (p. 235).
- Those loudest for the New Order are the most under the sway of the ideas that produced the war (p. 235).
- The young are right to distrust the ideas ruling their elders but wrong to think those are still nineteenth-century liberal ideas, which they hardly know (p. 235).
- There is neither the wish nor the power to return to the reality of the nineteenth century, but there is the opportunity to realise its ideals (p. 235).
- The twentieth century, not the nineteenth, made a mess of things (p. 235).
- If the first attempt to create a world of free men failed, it must be tried again; freedom for the individual is the only truly progressive policy (pp. 235-236).

**Bibliographical Note**

- A few chapters can discuss only some aspects of a long-unfashionable view, and that is hardly enough common ground for readers formed by the dominant views of the last twenty years (p. 236).
- The author's views are not singular; a steadily growing number of writers in many countries reached similar conclusions independently (p. 236).
- The list includes several works that supplement this essay's critical character with a fuller discussion of a desirable future society (p. 236).
- The earliest and most important is Mises's work, first published in 1922 (p. 236).
- Important German and Italian works exist that cannot safely be named, out of consideration for their authors (p. 237).
- Three books (Ashton, Foerster, Kantorowicz) are singled out as best for understanding the ideas ruling the enemy, plus Schnabel's history of nineteenth-century Germany (p. 237).
- The best guides may still be the political philosophers of the liberal age: Tocqueville, Acton, Constant, Burke and The Federalist (p. 237).
- The present generation takes liberty for granted, does not see where the danger comes from, and lacks the courage to free itself from the doctrines that endanger it (p. 237).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: the international framework will have to be built soon and will shape growth for a long time (pp. 234-235) + a blueprint for internal order is of doubtful use and nobody is clearly competent to write one (p. 235) + (unstated) a critical work can establish principles without a positive design.
  **Conclusion**: the book rightly confined itself to criticism, except on international order (pp. 234-235).
- **Premises**: before the war the path was encumbered by obstacles of human folly (p. 235) + individuals' creative energy is what produces progress (p. 235) + (unstated) removing controls releases that energy without further positive direction.
  **Conclusion**: the task is to create conditions favourable to progress, not to plan it (p. 235).
- **Premises**: the New Order merely projects forward the tendencies of the last forty years (p. 235) + those tendencies produced the war and most present evils (p. 235) + believing in inevitable tendencies forecloses a new start (p. 235).
  **Conclusion**: the truly progressive course is a new start on the nineteenth-century ideal of individual freedom (pp. 235-236).
- **Premises**: the nineteenth century had not yet fully learned what its ideals required (p. 235) + experience since then should have equipped the present better (p. 235).
  **Conclusion**: a first failure is a reason to try again, not to abandon the ideal (pp. 235-236).

## Evidence offered

- None in the Conclusion. It is exhortation and summary; the claims that recent policy was foolish, that the New Order imitates Hitler, and that the young do not know liberal ideas are asserted (p. 235).
- The Bibliographical Note offers a reading list as evidence that the view is shared: Cassel, Chamberlin, Graham, Gregory, Halevy, Hayek's own Collectivist Economic Planning, Hutt, Lippmann, Mises (two titles), Muir, Polanyi, Queeny, Rappard, Robbins (three titles), Roepke (two titles), Rougier, Voigt, and five Chicago Public Policy Pamphlets by Simons, Gideonse, Hermens, Sulzbach and Heilperin (pp. 236-237). This is evidence of company, not of truth. The phrase that these writers reached their conclusions independently (p. 236) is not supported; my observation is that several are close associates of Hayek (Mises, Robbins, Roepke).
- The list includes titles dated 1944 (Mises's Omnipotent Government, Roepke's Civitas Humana) (pp. 236-237), so it was current to the year of publication.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Self-description as critical.** The Conclusion's admission that the book is essentially critical (p. 234) is the passage Marschak points to in his reader's report (ap01, p. 243), matches Knight's complaint that it hardly considers alternatives (ap01, p. 242), and is conceded again by Hayek in 1976 (fm02, p. 54).
- **Against inevitability.** The Conclusion attacks belief in inevitable tendencies and the idea that past policy was inevitable (p. 235). This supports Hayek's 1976 denial that he argued inevitability (fm02, p. 55) and sits awkwardly with his own 1956 phrase about the inevitable consequences of socialist planning (fm02, p. 48) and Knight's summary of the thesis (ap01, p. 241). My observation: Hayek denies historical inevitability of the trend while asserting a necessary connection between a policy and its results; the two uses of the word should be kept apart in synthesis.
- **Not a return to the past.** The disclaimer about the reality of the nineteenth century (p. 235) is the book's answer to the charge of nostalgia or laissez-faire; compare Chamberlain's remark that Hayek is no devotee of laissez faire (ap01, p. 245).
- **Chapter 15.** The one exception to the critical task is international order (p. 234), which refers back to the federation proposals of the preceding chapter.
- **Principles, not a programme.** The 1956 foreword says the tests for reform are in the Rule of Law chapter (fm02, p. 43); the Conclusion names no such test and speaks only of certain principles (p. 235).
- **Bibliography as a map for cross-book work.** Mises's Socialism is ranked first (p. 236); Lippmann, Polanyi, Robbins, Roepke and Simons are the constructive supplements. Simons's title, A Positive Program for Laissez Faire (p. 237), names what this book declines to give.
- **Canon.** The closing list of Tocqueville, Acton, Constant, Burke and The Federalist (p. 237) matches the epigraphs and the 1956 use of Tocqueville and Acton (fm02, pp. 37, 41, 47). Burke's presence is worth noting against the 1956 distinction between liberalism and conservatism (fm02, p. 44).

## Open questions

- What are the certain principles (p. 235)? Synthesis should assemble them from the main chapters, since the Conclusion does not list them.
- Is the unstated premise sound, that clearing obstacles suffices to release creative energy (p. 235), given the positive state functions the main chapters allow?
- Does the refusal to offer a blueprint follow from Hayek's own argument about the limits of knowledge, or is it a limitation of the book, as Knight and Marschak took it (ap01, pp. 242-243)?
- How does the attack on inevitable tendencies (p. 235) fit with any passages in the main text that describe planning's consequences as necessary?
- Which works in the list did the reviewers and Hayek's critics regard as doing the constructive work, and do they agree with one another?
