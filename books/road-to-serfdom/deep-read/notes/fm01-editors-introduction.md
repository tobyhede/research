## Question this chapter answers

How did The Road to Serfdom come to be written and published, how was it received, which of the standing criticisms of it are fair, and what did Hayek actually claim (in particular, did he claim that any planning leads inevitably to totalitarianism)?

## Leading propositions

### Editorial Foreword: what this text is

- Caldwell: the Collected Works policy (stated by Bartley) is to publish texts in corrected, revised and annotated form, in their most complete version (p. 3).
- Caldwell: the British edition appeared in March 1944 and the American in September 1944; the American text was reset mainly to replace "this country" with "England"; because it is clearer for a non-British reader it was chosen as the text of this edition, and American spelling is used (p. 3), repeated in the body (p. 16) and in footnote 58 (p. 33).
- Caldwell: typographical errors are silently corrected; wrong citations by Hayek are corrected and noted; Hayek's inexact quotations of others are noted only where meaning might be affected, and Hayek's wording stands (p. 3).
- Caldwell: the book is the only work of Hayek's to go through numerous editions: 1944, 1956 (new foreword), 1976 (new preface), 1994 (Friedman's introduction) (p. 3).
- Caldwell: the Tocqueville title-page quotation was dropped from the first American edition and the Hume quotation was moved in some reprints; both are restored to the title page here (p. 16).
- Caldwell: Hayek refused the Press's proposal to drop the chapter epigraphs, saying (letter to Scoon, 26 June 1944) that a chapter's main conclusion is sometimes stated only in its epigraph (p. 16), with the letter in footnote 59 (p. 33).
- Caldwell: the Press also proposed the title "Socialism: The Road to Serfdom"; Hayek and Machlup rejected it as misleading because socialism was only one of the doctrines criticized and central planning could come from the right as well as the left (p. 16).
- Caldwell: an abridged British edition was produced by a Conservative MP on paper transferred from the Conservative Party's allocation, with a Churchill quotation in place of Hume and Tocqueville (p. 34), in footnote 61 citing Shearmur.

### Origins: memo, article, pamphlet, Abuse of Reason

- Caldwell: the book began as a memo to Beveridge disputing the claim that fascism was the dying gasp of a failed capitalism (p. 4).
- Caldwell: Hayek's 1933 inaugural lecture blamed the German Historical School for discrediting economic theory and so clearing the ground for utopian schemes, including socialist planning in Britain (p. 6).
- Caldwell: many in Britain read Naziism as capitalist-inspired or as the bourgeoisie's last stand, because some industrialists backed Hitler and the Nazis persecuted the left (p. 7).
- Hayek (as quoted from Hayek on Hayek): Beveridge was the main exponent he met of the view that National Socialism was a capitalist reaction against socialism, so he wrote a memorandum for him (p. 7).
- Caldwell: Hayek misdated the memo to 1939; it was probably written in May or early June 1933 (p. 7), argued in footnote 14 (p. 29).
- Caldwell, summarizing the memo: Hayek called National Socialism a "genuine socialist movement", citing its antagonism to liberalism, restrictive economic policy, the socialist background of some leaders and its antirationalism; socialism and Naziism both grew from antiliberal soil tended by the Historical School; other countries were following at a distance (p. 8).
- Caldwell: the memo already asserted that collectivism cannot be confined to a limited sphere and hinted that collective action leads to coercion, but did not develop the idea (p. 8).
- Caldwell: the 1938 article added the claim that planning presupposes more agreement on ends than exists, so the planner must impose a code of values, and that even democratic planning ends in propaganda and coercion (p. 8).
- Caldwell: the 1939 pamphlet added the contrast between central planning and planning a general system of rules, the price system as a mechanism for coordinating knowledge, and remarks on liberal economic policy; all went into the book (p. 8).
- Caldwell: some of these arguments descend from Mises and the post-1918 socialist calculation debate, which Hayek brought to English readers in Collectivist Economic Planning (1935); Lange replied, and Hayek answered Lange and Dickinson in a 1940 review (pp. 8-9).
- Caldwell: the book continues that academic work but also goes beyond it, answering a much wider chorus calling for scientific planning (p. 9).
- Caldwell: "By 1939, in short, most of the elements for Hayek's book were present", but not its form (p. 10).
- Caldwell: in August 1939 Hayek described to Machlup a project on scientific method and social problems tracing a century of intellectual history from Saint-Simon to Hitler; this became the Abuse of Reason project (p. 10).
- Caldwell: Hayek told Machlup the second part would elaborate the central argument of the 1939 pamphlet; the parts were to be called Hubris and Nemesis (p. 11).
- Caldwell: late in 1940 Hayek began turning the last part into The Road to Serfdom, first imagined as a sixpence Penguin; he never finished the larger project (p. 11).
- Caldwell: why Hayek switched is not definitively known; the plausible reason is fear that wartime controls would be kept in peacetime (p. 11).

### British context

- Caldwell: Hayek, an émigré naturalized in 1938, was offered no war post, was refused by the Ministry of Information in December 1939, and spent the war teaching and writing (pp. 10-11).
- Caldwell: war mobilization means central direction of production, price fixing and rationing, so the market system is more or less abandoned (p. 11).
- Caldwell: there was precedent for fearing that planners would treat this as a peacetime model, namely Neurath's war-economy doctrine, which had provoked Mises's original critique (p. 12).
- Caldwell: British intelligentsia in the 1930s nearly universally saw a planned system as the middle way; the Webbs, Mannheim, Nature's editorialists and Wootton wanted science enlisted to reconstruct society (pp. 9-10) (p. 12).
- Caldwell: Mannheim held planning to be inevitable, the only question being whether it would be totalitarian or democratic (p. 10).
- Caldwell: the Labour Party's 1942 pamphlet and Laski's conference resolution called for a planned society, nationalization of essential instruments of production, and keeping wartime controls (pp. 12-13).
- Caldwell: the Beveridge Report (December 1942) laid the foundations of the postwar welfare state and was an immediate popular success; the war itself was changing the climate of opinion (pp. 13-14).
- Caldwell: Beveridge privately wanted far more than the Report said, including public ownership of up to 75 per cent of industrial production (pp. 31-32), in footnote 45 quoting Harris.
- Caldwell: Hayek's aims were to remind the British of their liberal democratic heritage, contrast it with collectivist and corporativist authoritarianism, and show that implementing a centrally planned society would be inimical to liberty; the economic case alone was not enough (p. 14).

### Publication and reception

- Caldwell: Routledge published without difficulty; three American houses (Macmillan, Little Brown, Harper) declined before Chicago accepted (p. 4) (pp. 14-15).
- Caldwell: Aaron Director brought the book to Chicago; Knight's report was lukewarm, Marschak's (a socialist's) was warmer; acceptance was dated 28 December 1943 (p. 15).
- Caldwell: British publication 10 March 1944, 2,000 copies, sold out in about a month; paper rationing limited further printings (p. 16).
- Caldwell: American publication 18 September 1944, 2,000 copies; 17,000 ordered by 28 September after Hazlitt's front-page review (p. 16).
- Caldwell: the Reader's Digest condensation (April 1945, circulation about 8,750,000, over a million reprints) made the book a cultural icon and also a symbol; without it the book would hardly be so widely remembered (pp. 16-17) (p. 19).
- Caldwell: Chicago estimates more than 350,000 copies sold; Routledge and translation figures are unknown (p. 5).
- Caldwell: Hayek's 1945 American tour drew large popular audiences; he was embarrassed by adulation from people who knew only the condensation or the Look cartoon and worried about being misread (p. 17).
- Hayek (quoted, Chicago Sun): he was puzzled and alarmed that a book written in no party spirit was welcomed by one party and excoriated by the other (p. 17).
- Hayek (quoted, Detroit): he is not against government activity as such; what is needed is principles separating legitimate from illegitimate fields (p. 17).
- Hayek (quoted second-hand, Washington): above all he stands for free trade throughout the world (p. 17).
- Caldwell: the tour led to the Luhnow-funded Chicago project, which helped assemble the Chicago School, to the Mont Pèlerin Society (1947), and to Hayek's later move to Chicago (pp. 17-18).
- Caldwell: British critics (Wootton is the model) mostly took Hayek seriously; American reception was harsher (Finer, Soule, the PM exposé) (p. 18).
- Caldwell: Finer read Hayek's constitutionalism as antidemocratic; Caldwell answers that Hayek opposed planning, not democracy, and wanted constitutional limits against majority tyranny (p. 18), with the reply in footnote 77 (p. 35).
- Caldwell: Hayek's 1956 foreword explained the transatlantic difference by the two countries' different experience of socialism instead of attacking his critics; the 1948 draft postscript was less measured (p. 19), with footnote 82 (p. 35).

### The four criticisms and Caldwell's assessment

- Criticism 1, historical accuracy (Knight): German history was more complicated than Hayek's account; Bismarck-era socialist policy was only one element. Caldwell concedes the point and says Hayek would be on shaky ground to deny it, but pleads that the book was the conclusion of a larger unwritten work and that its target was the claim that Naziism was a phase of collapsing capitalism (pp. 19-20).
- Caldwell: Hayek's main point, that fascism and communism are both totalitarian and have more in common with each other than with liberal democracies, is one most would now accept as evident (p. 20).
- Criticism 2, no positive programme (Hansen, Keynes, and earlier Tead): Hayek does not say where to draw the line between good and bad planning. Caldwell does not dispute it; he says Hayek evidently took it to heart and answered in The Constitution of Liberty and Law, Legislation, and Liberty (p. 20); Tead is in footnote 52 (pp. 32-33).
- Keynes (quoted): morally and philosophically in deeply moved agreement with virtually the whole book, but Hayek gives no guidance on where to draw the line (p. 20).
- Criticism 3, market socialism ignored (Durbin): Caldwell rejects it. Hayek had already answered Lange in 1940 and cited that review in a note; he thought market socialism a blackboard exercise; Lange's 1940 letter seemed to concede the practical point; and the book was aimed at vague popular planning sentiment, not at theoretical economists (pp. 20-22).
- Hayek (unpublished 1948 postscript, quoted): he will examine competitive socialism further when a socialist party takes it seriously; none has (pp. 22-23).
- Caldwell: until a real-world example exists, market socialism is of interest only to specialists; later information-economics and public-choice arguments have strengthened Hayek's critique (p. 23).
- Criticism 4, inevitability (Durbin, Wootton, Stigler, Samuelson): Caldwell rejects it as a misreading (pp. 23-26). See Arguments.
- Caldwell: the condensed version omitted Hayek's statement that he was not describing inevitable tendencies, which may partly explain the misreading (p. 36), in footnote 101.

### Continuing relevance

- Caldwell: the book is a lightning rod and a Rorschach test that shows the reader's prior commitments (p. 26).
- Caldwell: it is both a tract for the times, needing annotation, and a carrier of timeless ideas (p. 26).
- Caldwell: three lasting messages: liberal democracy under the rule of law is a heritage worth keeping (p. 26); wartime, real or metaphorical, is when liberties are most easily surrendered and state power grows and is not given back (p. 27); ideas have power (p. 27).
- Caldwell admits the war theme is more explicit in his introduction than in particular passages of Hayek's text (pp. 26-27).
- Caldwell: readers on the left should look at chapter 3 for the intervention Hayek accepted in 1944; readers on the right at the liberal/conservative distinction in the 1956 foreword (p. 5).

## Arguments

### A. The inevitability charge is a misreading (Caldwell's central defence)

- **Premises**:
  1. [p. 24] Hayek denied the charge in public (1976 preface) and in private (letter to Samuelson), and pointed to a disclaimer present since the first edition: these developments are not inevitable and can be prevented.
  2. [pp. 24-25] A historical-inevitability claim is the kind of historicism Hayek attacked in "Scientism and the Study of Society"; so he cannot have meant it.
  3. [p. 25] The book is partly a warning: unless we change course we are on the road, and may blunder onto it without deciding to.
  4. [p. 25] Hayek's opponents (Mannheim-style "planning for freedom") were the ones claiming inevitability; Hayek was reversing their argument.
  5. [p. 25] The core is a logical claim: full-scale central planning requires the authorities to take all production decisions, and to decide at all they need growing political control; economic and political control cannot be separated. The 1939 pamphlet states it.
  6. [pp. 25-26] Western democracies never went to complete planning or full nationalization; Britain peaked at about 20 per cent of industry nationalized.
  7. [p. 26] A welfare state is not socialism.
  **Conclusion**: [p. 26] The postwar history of Western Europe does not test Hayek's thesis; the right test is the record of regimes that fully nationalized, and on that test Hayek is "fully vindicated".
- **Unstated premises (my reconstruction)**:
  - (U1) The thesis of the book is about *full* socialism (complete nationalization and comprehensive planning), not about partial planning, mixed economies or redistribution. Caldwell needs this for premise 7 to do any work. He elsewhere says the book's target was the vague, widespread enthusiasm for planning held by people who had not thought it through (p. 22) and that planning was advocated across the spectrum (pp. 9-10) (p. 16).
  - (U2) An author's later statements of intent, plus one disclaimer sentence, settle what a book argues, even where many careful readers on both sides (Stigler as well as Wootton) (p. 24) read it otherwise.
  - (U3) The logical argument carries no implication about intermediate cases; that is, there is no claim that partial planning creates pressure toward more planning.
  - (U4) In the regimes that fully nationalized, the loss of liberty was caused by planning and not by how those regimes came to power.
- **[My observation]** U1 and U3 are the weak points. Hayek's own words quoted in this unit cut against U3: the 1933 memo says the logic of collectivism makes it impossible to confine it to a limited sphere (p. 8), and the 1976 denial itself concedes "this danger exists" (p. 24). A tendency claim (planning generates pressure for more planning unless resisted) sits between "inevitable" and "purely logical", and Caldwell's three-way division of readings (prediction, warning, logic) does not give it a separate place. Synthesis should treat the presence of a dynamic or tendency claim in the book as the live question, in place of the inevitable-or-not framing.
- **[My observation]** On U4, Caldwell himself notes in passing that no real-world case began as a liberal socialist experiment (p. 25). That concession means his "count them up" test compares regimes that were mostly illiberal from the start, so the count cannot show that planning produced the illiberalism. The test as framed is close to unfalsifiable by democratic experience: any democracy that stops short of full nationalization is ruled out of the sample.
- **[My observation]** The defence buys immunity from refutation at the price of practical bite. If the thesis concerns only full socialism, it says little about the mixed economies and welfare states that were the actual postwar outcome, which is the ground on which Keynes's and Hansen's where-to-draw-the-line objection (p. 20) stands.

### B. The market-socialism omission is excusable

- **Premises**: [p. 21] Hayek had answered Lange in 1940 and referred to that review in a note. [p. 22] Lange's letter of 31 July 1940 called central price fixing a methodological device, not a practical proposal. [p. 22] The book addressed the general public's vague planning sentiment, not theorists. [p. 23] No socialist party proposed competition as an organizing method.
  **Conclusion**: [pp. 22-23] The details did not belong in a general book, and market socialism remains a theoretical construct without relevance to the actual world.
- **Unstated premises**: that an argument's relevance is measured by its uptake in party programmes; that Hayek's 1940 critique had in fact succeeded (Caldwell reports only that Hayek felt it had) (p. 22).
- **[My observation]** Hayek's 1948 rejoinder as quoted (p. 23) ends in an ad hominem against Lange and Durbin's political careers; Caldwell reports it sympathetically and adds Lange's later record, which is biography, not an answer to the scheme.

### C. The historical overstatement is excusable

- **Premises**: [p. 19] Knight is right that German history was more complex. [p. 19] The book was meant as the last section of a larger comparative history of liberalism's decline. [pp. 19-20] Its target was the capitalism-in-decay theory of Naziism.
  **Conclusion**: [pp. 19-20] The German claims make more sense in the intended setting; the main point (shared totalitarian character of fascism and communism) stands.
- **Unstated premise**: that a claim is to be judged by the unwritten work that would have supported it. **[My observation]** This is an explanation of the weakness, not a rebuttal; Caldwell effectively concedes criticism 1 while defending a weaker thesis (commonality) than the one in the 1933 memo (Naziism as a genuine socialist movement with shared intellectual roots) (p. 8).

### D. Why the book was written when it was

- **Premises**: [p. 11] War replaces the price system with central control. [p. 12] Neurath's precedent shows planners treat war economy as a peacetime model. [pp. 12-13] Scientists, Labour and Beveridge explicitly wanted wartime arrangements carried forward.
  **Conclusion**: [pp. 11, 14] Hayek set aside his scholarly project to write a popular political book aimed at reversing these trends.
- Caldwell flags this as plausible, not established (p. 11).

## Evidence offered

- **Archival documents, strong**: the "Nazi-Socialism" memo in the Hayek Papers, with an identical copy found by Susan Howson in Beveridge's papers (p. 29), in footnote 14. The identification as the Beveridge memo rests on that match; the 1933 dating rests on the manuscript's own "Spring 1933" date and its reference to the Berlin book-burning (p. 7). The correction of Hayek's "1939" is an inference, labelled by Caldwell as a best guess (p. 29).
- **Published texts**: the 1938 and 1939 versions of Freedom and the Economic System, compared directly (p. 8), sources in footnote 17 (p. 29); the 1935 volume and 1940 review (p. 9); the Labour pamphlet and Laski's speech (pp. 12-13); Nature editorial and Wootton review (p. 12); the Beveridge Report (p. 14).
- **Letters**: Hayek–Machlup correspondence for the Abuse of Reason outline, the Penguin plan and the publisher search (pp. 10-11) (pp. 14-15), sources in footnotes 30-35 (pp. 30-31); Lange to Hayek, 31 July 1940 (p. 22); Keynes to Hayek, 28 June 1944 (p. 20); Hayek to Samuelson (p. 24), source in a footnote outside this unit; Hayek to Scoon on epigraphs (p. 33); Berlin, Means, Carnap letters for hostile reception (p. 5).
- **Readers' reports**: Knight (10 December 1943) and Marschak (20 December 1943), University of Chicago Press collection (p. 15), sources in footnotes 55-56 (p. 33).
- **Sales and circulation figures**: British and American print runs (p. 16); 350,000 is a Press estimate from 2005, Friedman's 1994 figure was about 250,000 (p. 28), in footnote 2; Routledge has no reliable numbers (p. 5); Reader's Digest circulation from the magazine, readership of 10 million and "over a million" reprints from the hostile PM article, against Scoon's and Friedman's 600,000 (p. 34), in footnote 65. The larger reprint figure rests on a single journalistic source.
- **Second-hand anecdote, flagged by Caldwell**: the mile-long queue for the Beveridge Report comes from Beveridge's wife repeating hearsay (p. 31), in footnote 43; Scoon's account of how the book reached Chicago is second hand because he joined the Press in January 1944 (p. 33), in footnote 54; Bevin's motive for appointing Beveridge is "according to one account" (p. 13); the free-trade remark and the room cooling come from a newspaper columnist (p. 17), source in footnote 70 (p. 35).
- **Hayek's late reminiscences**: the student who said Hayek had destroyed his reputation is from a file card written for Bartley (p. 7), source in footnote 12 (pp. 28-29); the Beveridge story is from Hayek on Hayek and contains a dating error Caldwell himself corrects (p. 7). These are recollections decades after the events.
- **Historical generalization without source**: about 20 per cent of British industry nationalized at the postwar peak (p. 26); no citation in this unit.
- **Where confident language outruns the evidence** (note-taker's flags):
  - "Hayek evidently took these criticisms to heart" (p. 20): inferred only from the fact that later books addressed the gap; no document cited.
  - "Hayek doubtless experienced" déjà vu on reading Beveridge (p. 14) and "doubtless presumed" his economist readers knew the 1940 arguments (p. 22): psychological conjecture.
  - "Hayek probably hoped" his 1933 audience would see the Bismarck–Hitler parallel (p. 6): conjecture.
  - "Hayek might be forgiven if he were to infer" that Lange had conceded (p. 22): no evidence that Hayek drew that inference; and Lange's letter as quoted only says he prefers market pricing where feasible.
  - "fully vindicated" (p. 26): rests on an uncounted count ("Count them up") and on accepting Caldwell's choice of test.
  - "one that most would accept today as evident" (p. 20): appeal to present consensus.
  - The claim that the condensation caused the inevitability misreading is hedged (Caldwell says only that Hayek may have been implying it) (p. 36), yet Durbin and Wootton, British reviewers of the full text, held the same reading (pp. 23-24), which the hypothesis does not explain. **[My observation]**
  - The war-metaphor passage (war on drugs, poverty, terror; politicians love power) (p. 27) is Caldwell's own 2005 application, asserted without evidence and, by his admission, not explicit in Hayek's text (pp. 26-27).

### Closing footnotes (pp. 36-37), added after the first pass

These sit past the unit boundary, on the page where Hayek's own preface begins. All are Caldwell's.

- **Footnote 101** (pp. 36-37): the 1976 denial of the slippery-slope reading is in this volume's 1976 preface. Caldwell suggests Hayek may have been blaming the condensed and cartoon versions for the misreading. In the Reader's Digest condensation the denial of inevitability is left out, and the sentence calling fascism and naziism a necessary outcome of earlier socialist trends is set partly in italics that the original does not have. The IEA reprint of the condensation wrongly prints Marxism for naziism in that sentence (p. 37).
- **Footnote 102** (p. 37): source for the letter to Samuelson: Hayek to Paul Samuelson, 18 December 1980, Hayek Papers, box 48, folder 5, Hoover Institution Archives. Caldwell adds that Hayek was wrong to imply Samuelson originated the misreading, since it was a common one, and that the archive holds Samuelson's reply apologising and promising to represent Hayek more accurately in future (p. 37).
- **Footnote 103** (p. 37): source for the remark that Hayek was not against government activity as such: his address to the Economic Club of Detroit, p. 4 of the printed address.
- **Footnote 104** (p. 37): the line about methods most socialists would disapprove of is cross-referenced to chapter 10 of this volume.
- **Footnote 105** (p. 37): source for the statement of the logical argument: the 1939 pamphlet version of Freedom and the Economic System, p. 205. Caldwell reports that seminar audiences have more than once noted a similarity between Hayek's argument and Arrow's Impossibility Theorem in welfare economics. He reports the comparison and does not develop it.
- **Footnote 106** (p. 37): the tract-for-the-times phrase is Hayek's own, from the 1976 preface in this volume.
- **Footnote 107** (p. 37): the war-metaphor examples (wars on drugs, poverty, terror) are credited to Steven Horwitz, from a 2004 History of Economics Society session marking the book's sixtieth anniversary.
- **Footnote 108** (p. 37): for the claim that crises expand state power that is not given back, Caldwell cites Robert Higgs, Crisis and Leviathan (1987), and says the introduction is being written during George W. Bush's presidency, which he thinks supplies further evidence.
- **Footnote 109** (p. 37): source for the Keynes passage on the power of ideas: The General Theory, p. 383 of the Collected Writings reprint.

**[My observation]** Footnotes 107 and 108 confirm that the war-and-state-power theme is Caldwell's 2005 application, drawn from Horwitz and Higgs, not from Hayek's text. Footnote 105's Arrow comparison is the one substantive idea here: it bears on the chapter 5 claim that no agreed ranking of ends exists, and is worth following up in a cross-book synthesis. Footnote 102 weakens the use of the Samuelson letter as evidence that the inevitability reading was a careless one, since Caldwell concedes the reading was widespread.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Inevitability.** Caldwell cites the 1976 preface and a disclaimer he says stands "right at the beginning" of the book (p. 24) and the title of chapter 4 (p. 25). Check Hayek's Introduction and chapter 4 for the disclaimer's exact wording and for how it sits beside the stronger "inherent logic" language of the 1933 memo (p. 8); the memo is reproduced in the appendix.
- **Scope of the target.** Caldwell says the dedication to socialists of all parties shows planning from right and left alike was the target (p. 16), and that the book answered diffuse planning sentiment (p. 22); but his final defence restricts the thesis to full nationalization and says a welfare state is not socialism (p. 26). These two descriptions of the target pull apart. **[My observation]**
- **Intervention Hayek accepts.** Caldwell points to chapter 3 for accepted government intervention "at least in 1944" (p. 5) and notes that the 1976 preface amended some of these views (p. 28), in footnote 6. This links chapter 3, chapter 9 (security) and the 1976 preface.
- **Liberal versus conservative.** Caldwell points to the 1956 foreword for the distinction (p. 5).
- **Market socialism.** Hayek's only treatment is chapter 3, note 4 (p. 36), in footnote 92; Caldwell's defence of the omission depends on the 1940 review, which is outside this book.
- **Epigraphs carry conclusions.** Hayek's letter says a chapter's main conclusion may be stated only in its epigraph (p. 33); chapter notes should record the epigraphs.
- **German intellectual history.** Caldwell concedes the German argument is overstated and incomplete as it stands (p. 19); this bears on chapter 12 (socialist roots of Naziism) and on the 1933 memo in the appendix.
- **Rule of law and democracy.** Caldwell's reply to Finer (p. 35) depends on chapters 5 and 6.
- **Knowledge and prices.** Caldwell says the price system as a coordinator of knowledge entered in 1939 and went into the book (p. 8) and calls the dispersed-knowledge theme one of Hayek's central contributions (pp. 21-22); check how much of it actually appears in chapter 4 and elsewhere.
- **Appendix documents.** Knight's and Marschak's reports, the Scoon letter, Chamberlain's foreword and Friedman's introduction are referred to here and printed in the appendix (p. 15) (p. 33).
- **War.** Caldwell's wartime-liberties theme is acknowledged to be more his than Hayek's text's (pp. 26-27); compare chapters 13-15 and Hayek's own remarks on wartime controls.

## Open questions

- Does Hayek's text contain a tendency or dynamic claim (each step of planning creating pressure for the next) that is weaker than inevitability but stronger than Caldwell's "logical argument"? Chapters 5, 7 and 9 are the places to check prompted by the memo (p. 8) and the inevitability section (pp. 24-25).
- Is Hayek's target in the book full central planning only, or also piecemeal planning and the redistributive state? Does he himself distinguish the welfare state from socialism in 1944, or is that a later distinction that Caldwell reads back (p. 26)?
- How specific is Hayek about the line between acceptable and unacceptable state action in chapters 3, 6 and 9? Is the Keynes/Hansen objection (p. 20) fair to the 1944 text?
- How much of the German history in chapter 12 survives Knight's objection (p. 19)? Does Hayek claim socialism was the cause, or one cause, of Naziism?
- What exactly did the 1976 preface retract from the 1944 position on intervention (p. 28)?
- Does the full text support Caldwell's claim that most elements were in place by 1939 (p. 10)? What is new in the book relative to the 1939 pamphlet (for instance chapters 10 and 11)?
- Caldwell's "count them up" test (p. 26): is there any counter-case, and does Hayek himself propose an empirical test anywhere?
- The 20 per cent nationalization figure (p. 26) is unsourced in this unit; verify before relying on it.
- Footnote 105 (p. 37): is the similarity to Arrow's Impossibility Theorem real? Compare with the chapter 5 argument on the absence of an agreed scale of ends.
