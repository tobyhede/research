# ch05: Planning and Democracy (pp. 97–108)

## Question this chapter answers

Can a democracy direct the whole of economic life by a single plan and remain a democracy? Hayek's answer: no. Comprehensive planning needs agreement on a complete ranking of ends that does not exist and cannot be produced by majority vote, so the task is handed to experts and finally to a dictator; and democracy is in any case only a means, so its formal survival would not protect freedom.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Collectivism | p. 97 | Any system (communism, fascism, etc.) that wants "the deliberate organization of the labors of society for a definite social goal"; the systems differ in the goal, not in the method. Defined by organising the whole of society for one end and refusing to recognise autonomous spheres where individual ends are supreme. |
| Totalitarian | p. 97 | "In the true sense": organising the whole of society and all its resources for a unitary end. Used as the practical name for what "in theory we call collectivism"; the two are treated as inseparable. |
| Social goal / common good / general welfare | p. 97 | Phrases with "no sufficiently definite meaning to determine a particular course of action" (p. 97); they hide the absence of agreement on ends (p. 100). |
| Complete ethical code | p. 98 | A comprehensive scale of values in which every need of every person is ranked, complete enough to decide among all the courses open to a planner. Planning presupposes it; it does not exist (p. 98) and no mind could hold it (pp. 98-99). |
| Individualism | p. 99 | Not a claim that man is or should be selfish. The position that, since scales of value exist only in individual minds and are partial, the individual should be "the ultimate judge of his ends" within defined limits. |
| Social ends | p. 99 | Merely "identical ends of many individuals", or ends individuals will contribute to in return for help with their own. Most often means that serve many purposes, not ultimate ends. |
| The state | pp. 99-100 | One organisation ("person") among others, far more powerful, but with a "separate and limited sphere" set by the extent of agreement among individuals. |
| Planning | p. 98, p. 100 | Here: direction of all economic activity "according to a single plan" (p. 98) / "a system of directed economy" (p. 100); a plan "to deserve the name, must have a unitary conception" (p. 102). Always the comprehensive, central sense; the chapter never discusses partial planning except as "many separate plans" that "may be worse than no plan" (p. 104). |
| Democracy | pp. 105-106 | A procedure of majority decision; "essentially a means, a utilitarian device for safeguarding internal peace and individual freedom" (p. 106). Not an end in itself, not infallible. Distinguished from liberty, which Acton calls the highest political end. |
| Delegation (of legislative power) | pp. 101-103 | Handing law-making to departments or expert bodies. Unobjectionable when the power delegated is to make general rules; objectionable when it is power to decide particular cases by discretion (p. 103). |
| Arbitrary power | p. 103, p. 106 | Power to make particular decisions with the force of law that are not guided by fixed rules; "power limited by no fixed principles" (p. 108), in Hayek's note 5. What makes power non-arbitrary is its limitation, not its source (p. 106). |
| Capitalism | p. 105 | Stipulated as "a competitive system based on free disposal over private property". |
| Plebiscitarian dictatorship | p. 105 | A head of government periodically confirmed by popular vote while holding the powers to make sure the vote goes his way. |

## Leading propositions

- All collectivist systems share one feature: organising the whole of society for a single end and denying autonomous individual spheres; in that sense they are all totalitarian (p. 97).
- "Common good" and similar terms cannot determine a course of action, because welfare cannot be measured on a single scale; a single plan presupposes a complete ranking of all ends, that is, a complete ethical code (pp. 97-98).
- No such code exists, and morals have historically moved the other way, towards fewer and more general rules that only mark the limits of individual choice (p. 98).
- No mind could survey all needs and weigh them; this limit of knowledge and imagination, not egoism, is the foundation of individualism (pp. 98-99).
- Common action is legitimate where individual ends coincide; the state's sphere is set by the extent of agreement, and agreement becomes less likely as the scope of action widens (pp. 99-100).
- Once the state sector passes a certain share of the whole it indirectly controls almost everything, so common action cannot be extended indefinitely while leaving the individual free (p. 100).
- A democracy that adopts planning has agreed only on the mechanism, not on the ends; the absence of agreement shows as soon as a particular plan must be drawn up (pp. 100-101).
- Majority rule cannot produce a comprehensive plan: there need be no majority for any one of a legion of possible plans, and a plan cannot be assembled by voting clause by clause (p. 102).
- The work is therefore delegated to experts, who must impose their own scale of preferences, because the ends cannot be specified apart from the plan (pp. 102-103).
- Delegation is the first step only; separate delegated plans do not make a whole, and the demand grows for a government or individual freed from democratic procedure (pp. 103-104).
- Parliamentary "ultimate control" over a planned economy is illusory: parliament can accept or reject the plan as a whole, criticise, and choose who holds near-absolute power, but cannot direct (p. 105).
- Democracy is possible only within a competitive system based on private property; under a collectivist creed it destroys itself (p. 105).
- Democracy is a means, not an end; it can be oppressive, and autocracy has sometimes allowed more cultural freedom than some democracies (p. 106).
- Planning leads to dictatorship because dictatorship is the most effective instrument of coercion, and therefore essential to large-scale central planning (p. 106).
- Power is made non-arbitrary by its limitation, not by its democratic source (p. 106).

## Arguments

**A. The complete-code argument (pp. 97-99)**

- **Premises**: (p. 97) Welfare depends on many things in endless combinations and cannot be put on one scale. (pp. 97-98) To direct all activity by one plan the planner must be able to decide among all alternatives, so needs a complete ranking of every need of every person. (p. 98) No such ranking exists in our morals; people have no views or conflicting views on these questions. (pp. 98-99) No single mind could comprehend and weigh all needs. (p. 99) Scales of value exist only in individual minds and are partial and mutually inconsistent.
- **Unstated premises**: that a plan must be *fully* determinate and consistent across all decisions to count as a plan (no room for a plan that fixes a few priorities and leaves the rest); that there is no acceptable procedural way to generate a social ranking where none pre-exists (voting, bargaining and so on are treated as failing, argued separately in C).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 99) Individuals should be allowed, within defined limits, to follow their own values; a single plan can only be someone's partial scale imposed on everyone else (p. 103).

**B. The scope-of-agreement argument (pp. 99-100)**

- **Premises**: (p. 99) Social ends are only coinciding individual ends. (p. 100) The state's legitimate sphere is bounded by agreement. (p. 100) The probability of agreement "necessarily decreases as the scope of such action extends". (p. 100) Beyond a certain share of resources, state decisions dominate the rest of the system.
- **Unstated premises**: that state action outside agreement is by that fact a suppression of freedom (he says the state "is bound to suppress individual freedom" there, p. 100, without separate argument); that there is a threshold share rather than a gradual slope, and that roughly half of national income is past it.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 100) The "social scale of values" guiding a large state must cover practically all individual ends, so the sphere of common action cannot be widened indefinitely without ending individual freedom.

**C. The parliamentary-incapacity argument (pp. 100-105)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 100-101) Agreement to plan is agreement on a mechanism only. (p. 102) Majorities form between limited alternatives; with legion alternatives no plan need command a majority, even if every member prefers some plan to none. (p. 102) A plan needs a unitary conception and cannot be built by compromise or clause-by-clause voting. (pp. 102-103) Unlike a general, a planner has no single given end; which ends conflict is known only to those who know all the facts. (p. 103) So the experts must choose between ends and impose their preferences. (pp. 103-104) Delegation to separate bodies leaves the problem of integration, and the legislature's reluctance to give up vital decisions blocks anyone else from providing the whole plan. (p. 104) Belief that planning is necessary plus failure to deliver produces a demand for someone freed from democratic procedure.
- **Unstated premises**: that the public commitment to planning persists through the failure (people blame parliament, not planning); that there is no stable stopping point between an assembly that cannot plan and a single authority that can; that the dynamic observed in Weimar Germany was caused by the attempt to plan and not by other factors.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 105) Democracy that depends on central planning tends towards plebiscitarian dictatorship; parliament is reduced to a safety valve and to choosing those who hold practically absolute power.

**D. Reply to Mannheim: many controls are not like few (pp. 104-105)**

- **Premises**: (p. 104) Mannheim: if a few controls can be checked by parliamentary sovereignty, so can many. (p. 105) Parliament controls execution where it has agreed the aim and delegates only detail. (p. 105) In planning the delegation happens *because* there is no agreement on ends, and the planning body chooses between ends whose conflict parliament does not even know of.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 105) Parliamentary control of a comprehensive plan is not control; a plan can only be taken or left as a whole.

**E. Democracy as means (pp. 105-106)**

- **Premises**: (p. 106) Democracy is a device for peace and individual freedom, not the highest political end. (p. 106) Autocracies have sometimes permitted more cultural and spiritual freedom than some democracies; a homogeneous doctrinaire majority could be as oppressive as a dictatorship. (p. 106) What prevents arbitrariness is limitation of power, not its source. (p. 106) A task that cannot be guided by fixed rules requires arbitrary power whoever authorises it.
- **Unstated premise**: that individual freedom (as Hayek defines it) is the value by which political forms are to be judged; democracy has no independent claim, for example as an expression of equal standing.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 106) The real danger is not the loss of democratic form but the loss of freedom; a democratically conferred planning power would still be arbitrary, and a democratic "dictatorship of the proletariat" that planned centrally "would probably destroy personal freedom as completely as any autocracy has ever done".

**Position versus argument.** What he holds: democracy is possible only under competitive capitalism (p. 105). Why he says he holds it: only the liberal creed keeps the range of matters needing agreement small enough for majorities to form by free discussion (p. 105). Note that this is a claim about a necessary condition; he does not claim capitalism is sufficient for democracy.

## Evidence offered

- **Mechanism argument** (the main support): the logic of what a single plan requires, and of how majorities form (pp. 97-103). No empirical test is offered for the claim that no majority can agree on a plan.
- **Statistic**: German central and local authorities directly controlling "more than half the national income (according to an official German estimate then, 53 per cent)" in 1928 (p. 100). Hayek names no document; the editor's note 2 only explains that Hayek collected such data as director of the Austrian business-cycle institute (pp. 106-107). The inference from 53 per cent of income passing through public hands to indirect control of "almost the whole economic life" (p. 100) is asserted, not shown.
- **Quotation from opponents**: the Webbs on the incapacity of the House of Commons (p. 101); Laski's New Statesman article on delegated legislation and his unanswered question about a Labour government risking overthrow at the next election (pp. 101-102), reinforced in Hayek's note 4 with Laski's Democracy in Crisis (p. 107); Mannheim on boundless plenary powers (pp. 104-105). These show that some socialists accepted reduced parliamentary control; they do not show that planning requires it.
- **Official document**: the Donoughmore Committee report (1932), read against its own intent in Hayek's note 5: the phrase that laws affect lives so closely "that elasticity is essential" is taken to reveal a conferment of arbitrary power (pp. 107-108).
- **Testimony of an observer**: Halévy's "composite photograph" of Percy, Mosley and Cripps all calling for dictatorial leadership (p. 104).
- **Historical example**: Germany before 1933 "had, in effect, had to be governed dictatorially"; Hitler "did not have to destroy democracy; he merely took advantage of the decay of democracy" (p. 104). Offered as illustration of the mechanism. The text does not show that the decay was caused by planning commitments; that link is assumed.
- **Analogy**: travellers who commit to a journey without agreeing on a destination (p. 101); the military campaign, used both for the need to delegate and to mark the difference that a general has one end (pp. 102-103); civil law reform as a technical field nobody proposes to delegate (p. 103).
- **Historical generalisation, bare**: civilisation has been accompanied by "a steady diminution of the sphere in which individual actions are bound by fixed rules" (p. 98); "there has often been much more cultural and spiritual freedom under an autocratic rule than under some democracies" (p. 106). No instances are given for either.
- **Confident language outrunning evidence**: "inevitably cause dissatisfaction" (p. 101); "It is inevitable that they should impose their scale of preferences" (p. 103); "democracy will inevitably destroy itself" (p. 105); "only within this system is democracy possible" (p. 105). Elsewhere the wording is weaker: "will tend toward" (p. 105), "would probably destroy" (p. 106).

**Editorial corrections and additions (Caldwell, in square brackets)**

- Note 3: the Webbs wrote "increasing incapacity", not "increased incapacity" as Hayek quotes them (p. 107).
- Note 8: the second half of the Mannheim quotation is from p. 341, not p. 340 as Hayek cites (p. 108). The Mannheim passage carries "[sic]" after "only" (p. 104); it is not signed by the editor, so it appears to be Hayek's own. The passage also contains an ellipsis, so it joins two separate places in Mannheim.
- Note 4 (Hayek's own voice): Laski was a member of the Donoughmore Committee whose authority he invokes (p. 107). The editor adds what the committee was (p. 107).
- Note 6: editor identifies Percy, Mosley and Cripps as from different ends of the spectrum (p. 108). Note 7: editor identifies Brüning, von Papen and Schleicher (p. 108).

## Concessions and qualifications

- Individualism "does not, of course, exclude the recognition of social ends"; men may combine to pursue coinciding ends, and the state is such an organisation (pp. 99-100).
- There are state functions on which there is "practical unanimity" and others with "agreement of a substantial majority" (p. 100). He does not list them here.
- Delegation of law-making "as such" is not objectionable; there may be "very good reasons" for local bodies to lay down general rules (p. 103). Repeated in note 5: "delegation as such need not be a danger to freedom" (p. 107).
- Parliamentary discussion under planning may still prevent "some flagrant abuses" and get particular shortcomings remedied (p. 105).
- The fault lies "neither with the individual representatives nor with parliamentary institutions as such" but with the task (p. 102).
- "We have no intention, however, of making a fetish of democracy" (p. 105): democracy is fallible, can be oppressive, and autocracy is not necessarily fatal to freedom (p. 106).
- "Democratic control may prevent power from becoming arbitrary, but it does not do so by its mere existence" (p. 106).
- It is "the price of democracy" that "in some fields things must be left to chance" (p. 105): he admits a cost.
- He sets aside, without answering, whether a complete ethical code would be desirable (p. 98).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch03-ch04**: "planning" here is the comprehensive central direction defined earlier; the argument has no purchase on planning for competition or on partial intervention. The chapter itself never restates that restriction, so a reader of ch05 alone could take it as an argument against any extension of state action.
- **Feeds ch06**: the closing sentence (power not guided by fixed rules "must become arbitrary power", p. 106) and the general-rules versus discretion distinction in the delegation passage (p. 103) are the bridge to the Rule of Law chapter, which supplies the criterion (formal rules) that ch05 only gestures at.
- **Feeds ch07**: the claim that controlling over half of resources means indirectly controlling nearly all ends (p. 100) is the quantitative version of ch07's claim that control of means is control of ends (ch07, p. 123).
- **Feeds ch10**: "the largest group able to agree among themselves" being a minority whose will is imposed (p. 105) anticipates the selection argument about who rises under totalitarianism.
- **Internal tension (my observation)**: the scope argument says agreement falls as scope widens (p. 100), a matter of degree; the conclusions are stated as thresholds and inevitabilities (pp. 100, 105). The chapter gives no way to locate the threshold other than the single German figure.
- **Internal tension (my observation)**: agreement is the test of legitimate state action (pp. 99-100), yet he also says majority agreement confers no protection against arbitrariness (p. 106). The two fit together only if "agreement" in the first sense means agreement on general rules or shared means, which is ch06's contribution.
- **Tension with later concessions (my observation)**: ch09's acceptance of a guaranteed minimum and social insurance (not read in this pass; check against the ch09 note) involves state action on which views differ; whether those meet the ch05 agreement test is not addressed here.
- **Democracy's status**: democracy is instrumental to freedom (p. 106). Compare the 1956 and 1976 prefaces and ch15 for whether he later treats democratic limits differently.

## Open questions

- Is the "complete ethical code" requirement a fair description of what real planners (wartime planners, the post-1945 Labour government, indicative planners) needed? Mixed economies that passed the 50 per cent mark in public spending did not become dictatorships; synthesis should test whether Hayek's claim is restricted to *comprehensive central direction* tightly enough to escape that counter-example, or whether p. 100 commits him to more.
- Does the argument about majorities (p. 102) prove too much? Budgets are complex wholes voted by parliaments. What distinguishes a budget from a plan on his account?
- The Weimar example (p. 104): is the causal claim (planning commitments caused democratic breakdown) argued anywhere in the book, or only assumed? Check ch12.
- How does "the limitation of power" (p. 106) get enforced if not by democratic means? ch06 answers with the Rule of Law, but who binds the legislature? (See ch06, pp. 118-119 on constitutional limits.)
- He does not say whether the desirable state would be a complete ethical code if we could have it (p. 98). Is his objection epistemic, moral, or both?
- "In some fields things must be left to chance" (p. 105): how does this admitted cost square with ch08's treatment of chance and impersonal forces as preferable to deliberate distribution?
