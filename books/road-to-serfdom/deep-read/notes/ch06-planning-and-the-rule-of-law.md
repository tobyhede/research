# ch06: Planning and the Rule of Law (pp. 108–121)

## Question this chapter answers

What separates a free country from one under arbitrary government, and can collectivist economic planning be carried on within that limit? Hayek's answer: the Rule of Law, meaning government bound by general rules fixed in advance, is the mark of a free country; planning needs discretionary decisions between particular people and so cannot observe it; and any policy aiming at substantive equality or distributive justice is incompatible with it.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rule of Law | p. 109 | Government "in all its actions is bound by rules fixed and announced beforehand" (p. 109), so individuals can foresee how coercive power will be used and plan accordingly. Later tightened: a limit on the *scope of legislation* itself, restricting it to formal law (p. 116). Not the same as legality: an act can be fully legal and still violate it (pp. 115-116). Hayek's note 2 says he uses the "wider and older" meaning, not the narrower technical English sense that grew out of Dicey (p. 118). |
| Arbitrary government | p. 109 | The opposite of the Rule of Law: coercive power used ad hoc, by discretion, not determined by pre-established rules. Central direction of economic activity is "a particular case" of it (p. 109). |
| Formal rules / formal law | pp. 109-110 | General rules, set in advance for long periods, defined "without reference to time and place or particular people", instrumental to whatever ends individuals choose. The decisive criterion: we do not know their concrete effects or whom they will help (p. 110). Likened to the Highway Code and to signposts (p. 110). |
| Substantive rules | p. 110 | Rules or orders that aim at particular ends or particular people and decide whose interests count for more; like "ordering people where to go" (p. 110). Hayek admits the line is "most difficult to draw precisely in practice" (p. 110). |
| Planning (of the collectivist kind) | p. 109 | Government directing "the use of the means of production to particular ends" (p. 109); deciding how many pigs, which mines, what price for shoes (p. 110). Contrasted with fixing a permanent framework of laws. |
| Impartiality | p. 111 | Having "no answer to certain questions"; possible for a legislator only because the effects of general rules on particular people cannot be foreseen. |
| "Moral" state | p. 112 | A state that imposes its views on all moral questions on its members; in this sense the Nazi state is "moral" and the liberal state is not. A deliberately non-evaluative use. |
| Fairness / reasonableness | pp. 112-113 | Vague formulas that cannot decide between competing claims once gains and losses are both visible; their spread in legislation marks the decline of the Rule of Law. |
| Rule of status | p. 113 | Law fixing how well off particular people shall be and what they may do; planning is a return to it. The Rule of Law, more than contract, is its true opposite. |
| Formal equality (equality before the law) | p. 113 | Absence of legal privileges for people designated by authority; the same rules for all. |
| Substantive / material equality | p. 113 | Equal results or equal subjective chances for different people; requires treating people differently and so conflicts with formal equality. |
| Distributive justice | p. 113 | A "substantive ideal" of who should have what; any policy "aiming directly" at it destroys the Rule of Law. |
| Privilege | p. 114 | A right reserved to particular people designated by authority (land reserved to nobility; licences to produce or sell). Property that all can acquire under the same rules is not privilege. |
| Laissez faire | p. 114 | "A highly ambiguous and misleading description" of liberal policy. The real question is not action versus inaction but whether state action is foreseeable. |
| Rights of man / individual rights | p. 116 | What recognised limits on legislation amount to: "the inalienable right of the individual, inviolable rights of man". |
| Delegation | p. 116 | Reused from ch05: under planning, law must give boards power to decide "on principles which cannot be stated in generic form". |

## Leading propositions

- The Rule of Law means government bound by rules fixed and announced beforehand; the ideal is never perfectly achieved, but executive discretion should be reduced as far as possible (p. 109).
- Every law restricts freedom to some extent; the Rule of Law prevents government from frustrating individual efforts by ad hoc action (p. 109).
- The distinction between a permanent legal framework and central direction of the economy is a particular case of the distinction between the Rule of Law and arbitrary government (p. 109).
- Collectivist planning cannot bind itself by general rules: it must decide concrete questions by the circumstances of the moment and so rank the interests of persons and groups (pp. 109-110).
- The mark of a formal rule is that its effect on particular people and ends is unknown in advance (p. 110).
- Economic argument: individuals can use their local knowledge only if state action is predictable, and state action is predictable only if it follows rules fixed independently of circumstances; so "the more the state “plans,” the more difficult planning becomes for the individual" (p. 111).
- Moral or political argument: where the state can foresee the effects of its action on particular people, the state is choosing between their ends and cannot be impartial (pp. 111-112).
- "Fairness" cannot settle planning questions; agreement inside one industry proves nothing because the losers (consumers) are left out (p. 112).
- Growth of planning brings growth of vague standards and judicial or administrative discretion; in Germany this decline preceded Hitler (pp. 112-113).
- Planning means deliberate discrimination between people and a return to the rule of status (p. 113).
- Formal equality before the law is incompatible with government action aimed at material equality; a policy aiming directly at distributive justice must destroy the Rule of Law (p. 113).
- The Rule of Law produces economic inequality; the only claim for it is that the inequality is not designed to affect particular people (p. 113).
- That a rule be applied without exception can matter more than its content (p. 114).
- Private property open to all on the same rules is not privilege (p. 114).
- The liberal position is not state inaction; the question is whether state action is foreseeable. General permanent regulation of production is compatible with liberal principles (pp. 114-115).
- Legality is not the Rule of Law: a democracy can legally set up complete despotism by granting unlimited powers (pp. 115-116).
- The Rule of Law limits what legislation may do and so implies inviolable individual rights; planners such as Wells who also want rights of man are inconsistent (pp. 116-117).
- The view that government may always hold plenary powers is the view that all who want government to direct economic life must hold, and it leads "straight to the totalitarian state" (p. 118).
- Formal protection of minority rights is worthless where the state controls economic life (p. 118).

## Arguments

**A. Planning cannot be done by formal rules (pp. 109-110)**

- **Premises**: (p. 109) Formal rules are set in advance for long periods and do not aim at particular people's needs. (pp. 109-110) A planning authority must meet actual needs as they arise and choose between them. (p. 110) Decisions on quantities, prices and which plants operate depend on the circumstances of the moment and require balancing the interests of persons and groups.
- **Unstated premise**: that there is no intermediate form, such as general rules that set planning targets or criteria in advance and are applied by formula. He treats every allocation decision as a fresh act of discretion.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 110) Someone's view of whose interests matter more becomes "part of the law of the land", a new rank order imposed by coercion.

**B. The economic (knowledge) argument for general rules (p. 111)**

- **Premises**: Only the individuals concerned know the circumstances of time and place. To use that knowledge in plans they must be able to predict state action. Predictable state action must follow rules fixed independently of concrete circumstances, so its particular effects are unpredictable. State action aimed at particular ends must respond to the full circumstances of the moment and is therefore itself unpredictable.
- **Unstated premise**: that predictability of state action and predictability of its effects on particular people cannot both be had; and that the use of dispersed individual knowledge is the dominant economic consideration (the full argument is "only briefly" stated here, p. 111).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 111) The state should confine itself to rules for general types of situation.

**C. The moral (impartiality) argument (pp. 111-112)**

- **Premises**: (p. 111) If the state can foresee exactly who is affected and how, it leaves those affected no choice and is itself choosing between ends. (p. 111) General rules operate in unforeseeable circumstances, so their effect on particular people is unknown. (p. 111) Impartiality just is having no answer to such questions. (pp. 111-112) Where effects on particular people are known and aimed at, government "must, of necessity, take sides".
- **Unstated premises**: that legislators really are ignorant of who will gain from general rules (as opposed to being able to predict, statistically, which classes gain); that knowing an effect and intending it are morally equivalent for a legislator; that impartiality in this sense is the relevant standard of justice.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 112) Law whose particular effects are foreseen becomes "an instrument used by the lawgiver upon the people and for his ends".

**D. Formal versus substantive equality (p. 113)**

- **Premises**: The Rule of Law is the absence of legal privileges for designated people. To give different people the same result they must be treated differently. A law that prescribes how well off particular people shall be is a rule of status.
- **Unstated premise**: that general rules cannot themselves aim at a distributive pattern. A rule defined in general terms (for instance a tax schedule or a benefit entitlement by category) is not considered here as a possible vehicle; "deliberately aiming" and "aiming directly" carry the weight.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 113) Formal equality before the law is "in conflict, and in fact incompatible" with government activity deliberately aiming at material equality; pursuit of distributive justice destroys the Rule of Law.

**E. Legality is not the Rule of Law (pp. 115-116)**

- **Premises**: (p. 115) Full legal authority to act says nothing about whether the law prescribes how to act or leaves it to discretion. (p. 115) Hitler may have gained unlimited powers constitutionally. (p. 116) If the law says a board may do as it pleases, its acts are legal but not under the Rule of Law.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 116) The Rule of Law is a limit on legislation, and a particular enactment can infringe it; (p. 116) such limits amount to recognising inviolable individual rights, whether in a bill of rights or by tradition.
- **Unstated premise**: that some standard above the legislature exists and can be made effective; he says the form "matters comparatively little" (p. 116) but gives no account of enforcement.

**F. Rights and planning cannot be combined (pp. 116-118)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 116-117) Individual rights obstruct planning; Wells therefore hedges each right with "common welfare" qualifications that empty it. (p. 117) Freedom of movement and of the press cannot be secured when the authority controls transport, currency, location of industry, paper and distribution. (p. 118) Central European states discriminated against national minorities through ordinary economic policy without breaching statutory minority protections.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 118) Formal recognition of rights "loses all significance" under complete control of economic life.

**Position versus argument.** He holds that the liberal state should not pursue distributive justice (p. 113). The reason given here is formal and legal, that doing so requires treating persons differently by authority, and not that existing distributions are just; he concedes outright that the Rule of Law "produces economic inequality" (p. 113).

## Evidence offered

- **Conceptual analysis and definition** carry most of the chapter: the Rule of Law and formal rules are defined (pp. 109-110), and the incompatibility with planning follows largely from the definitions. The claim that planning "necessarily involves the very opposite" (p. 109) is a deduction from how planning is defined, not an observation.
- **Analogy**: Highway Code versus ordering people where to go; signposts versus commanding which road (p. 110); driving on the left or right (p. 114); rules as "a kind of instrument of production" (p. 109); tossing a coin (p. 111).
- **Illustrative examples of planning decisions**: pigs, buses, coal mines, shoe prices (p. 110); nurses' and doctors' wages against services for the sick, milk for children against farm wages, jobs for the unemployed against wages for the employed (p. 112). Hypothetical, not documented cases.
- **Mechanism argument against "fairness"**: capital and labour in one industry agreeing to restrict output and share the spoils at consumers' expense (p. 112).
- **Historical assertion**: the decline of the Rule of Law in Germany "had been going on steadily" before Hitler, and planning "had already done a great deal of the work which Hitler completed" (p. 113). No instances are cited in the text; the editor points to The Constitution of Liberty ch. 16 (pp. 118-119).
- **Authority**: Dicey's definition (Hayek's note 2, p. 118); Maine's status to contract (p. 113); Kant and Voltaire (p. 115); Locke (Hayek's note 7, p. 119).
- **Quotation from opponents**: Mannheim epigraph conceding that formal law belongs only to the liberal competitive phase (pp. 108-109); Carl Schmitt's "just state" against the Rechtsstaat (Hayek's note 5, p. 119); Wells's Declaration of the Rights of Man, clauses quoted and criticised (pp. 116-117); The Economist leader of 1939 on plenary powers (pp. 117-118); E. B. Ashton on totalitarian criminal law (Hayek's note 9, p. 120).
- **Legal cases**: Mr. Justice Darling on the Board of Agriculture being "no more impeachable than Parliament itself", via Lord Hewart (p. 116); the bill of attainder against Richard Rose, the Bishop of Rochester's cook, under Henry VIII (Hayek's note 9, p. 120).
- **Historical example**: discrimination against national minorities in Central European countries through economic policy (p. 118). No country, measure or source is named.
- **Bare assertion / language outrunning evidence**: "There can be no doubt that planning necessarily involves deliberate discrimination" (p. 113); socialists "(and Nazis) have always protested" against formal justice and "always demanded" socialisation of the law (p. 113); the plenary-powers view "leads straight to the totalitarian state" and is the view "all those who want the government to direct economic life must hold" (p. 118); "has been amply demonstrated" used twice of the unnamed Central European cases (p. 118).

**Editorial corrections and additions (Caldwell)**

- Note 6: the editor "was unable to locate the quotation attributed to Kant"; he supplies only the Voltaire source and its French wording (p. 119). The Kant quotation on p. 115 is therefore unverified.
- Note 9: Hayek gave the Ashton quotation as p. 119; the editor corrects it to p. 127 (p. 120).
- Note 7: the editor glosses Hayek's appeal to Locke by noting Locke described the state of nature as "a state of perfect freedom" before men submit to law to preserve liberty and property (pp. 119-120); a mild qualification of "no liberty without law".
- Note 5: editor identifies Schmitt and glosses the Freirechtsschule as the German term for legal realism (p. 119). Notes 2 and 3: cross-references to The Constitution of Liberty chs. 13 and 16 (pp. 118-119). Note 10: background on Wells's Declaration and its later influence on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (p. 120).
- The Economist quotation carries "[sic]" after "always" (p. 117); it is not signed by the editor, so it appears to be Hayek's own, and he returns to the word on p. 118.

## Concessions and qualifications

- The Rule of Law "can never be perfectly achieved"; the aim is to reduce executive discretion "as much as possible" (p. 109).
- "Every law restricts individual freedom to some extent" (p. 109).
- The formal/substantive distinction is "most difficult to draw precisely in practice" (p. 110), and at the border "the distinction, however clear in principle, becomes blurred in practice" (p. 115).
- The Rule of Law "produces economic inequality" (p. 113), and giving people the same objective opportunities does not give them the same subjective chance (p. 113).
- A rule applied without exception may be felt "to be unjust" in a particular instance (p. 114).
- **State activity explicitly accepted**: "every state must act and every action of the state interferes with something or other" (p. 114); controlling weights and measures and preventing fraud and deception (p. 114); suppressing violence, with strike pickets as the example of culpable inaction (p. 114); "building regulations or factory laws", which "may be wise or unwise" but do not conflict with liberal principles if permanent and not used to favour or harm particular people (p. 115).
- He admits such regulations have known short-run effects on particular people, and says only that these "are in general not (or at least ought not to be) the guiding consideration" (p. 115).
- "Laissez faire" is rejected as a description of liberal principles (p. 114).
- A planned society need not be lawless or illegal; its acts may all be legal (p. 115).
- Plenary powers "may be inevitable in wartime" (p. 118).
- The constitutional form in which limits on legislation are held (bill of rights, code, tradition) "matters comparatively little" (p. 116).
- The economic argument is "only briefly" stated (p. 111).
- Carl Schmitt's contrast between the Rechtsstaat and the "just state" is "not altogether false" (Hayek's note 5, p. 119).

## Quotes

- "Stripped of all technicalities, this means that government in all its actions is bound by rules fixed and announced beforehand—rules which make it possible to foresee with fair certainty how the authority will use its coercive powers in given circumstances and to plan one’s individual affairs on the basis of this knowledge." (p. 109)
- "They do not involve a choice between particular ends or particular people, because we just cannot know beforehand by whom and in what way they will be used." (p. 110)
- "To be impartial means to have no answer to certain questions—to the kind of questions which, if we have to decide them, we decide by tossing a coin." (p. 111)
- "It cannot be denied that the Rule of Law produces economic inequality—all that can be claimed for it is that this inequality is not designed to affect particular people in a particular way." (p. 113)
- "But to call private property as such, which all can acquire under the same rules, a privilege, because only some succeed in acquiring it, is depriving the word “privilege” of its meaning." (p. 114)
- "these may be wise or unwise in the particular instance, but they do not conflict with liberal principles so long as they are intended to be permanent and are not used to favor or harm particular people." (p. 115)
- "By giving the government unlimited powers, the most arbitrary rule can be made legal; and in this way a democracy may set up the most complete despotism imaginable." (p. 116)
- "The Rule of Law thus implies limits to the scope of legislation: it restricts it to the kind of general rules known as formal law and excludes legislation either directly aimed at particular people or at enabling anybody to use the coercive power of the state for the purpose of such discrimination." (p. 116)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch03**: p. 109 says the earlier distinction between a legal framework for competition and central direction is "really a particular case" of Rule of Law versus arbitrary government. This re-grounds the book's central distinction in legal form instead of in economic mechanism.
- **Completes ch05**: supplies the criterion for ch05's claims that delegation is harmful only when it confers discretion (ch05, p. 103) and that limitation, not source, makes power non-arbitrary (ch05, p. 106). The "complete system of values" needed to decide between nurses and patients (p. 112) repeats ch05's complete ethical code (ch05, p. 98).
- **Feeds ch08**: the claim that inequality under general rules "is not designed" (p. 113) is the basis for ch08's argument that impersonal inequality is easier to bear than inequality by design (ch08, p. 134). "Rule of status" (p. 113) is developed there.
- **Feeds ch07**: the remarks on freedom of movement and the press under control of paper, transport and currency (p. 117) anticipate ch07's argument that economic control is control of all ends.
- **Shift in meaning (my observation)**: the Rule of Law begins as predictability of *executive* action (p. 109) and ends as a substantive limit on *legislation* tied to inalienable rights (p. 116). The first is a formal property; the second needs a doctrine of rights that the chapter invokes but does not supply.
- **Shift in the criterion of formality (my observation)**: three tests are used as if equivalent: generality of form (no reference to time, place, persons, p. 110); ignorance of who will benefit (p. 110); and permanence plus absence of intent to favour or harm (p. 115). They come apart. A general, permanent rule can have well-known distributive effects, and he admits known short-run effects on p. 115, falling back on intent ("guiding consideration").
- **Tension with ch09 (my observation)**: ch09 accepts a guaranteed minimum income and social insurance (not read in this pass; check against the ch09 note). Whether those "aiming directly at a substantive ideal of distributive justice" (p. 113) or count as general rules is the pressure point; the p. 113 claim, read strictly, seems to exclude any redistributive purpose, read loosely excludes only person-by-person allocation.
- **Tension (my observation)**: "it is more important that there should be a rule applied always without exceptions than what this rule is" (p. 114) sits uneasily with the appeal to "inviolable rights of man" (p. 116), since a general, exceptionless rule can be oppressive in content. He does not address general rules that are bad.
- **Link to ch10-ch11**: the "moral" state that imposes views on all moral questions (p. 112) is the premise for the later chapters on collectivist morality and control of opinion.

## Open questions

- Can general, prospective rules have a distributive aim (progressive taxation, universal benefits defined by category) without violating the Rule of Law as defined on pp. 110 and 116? Synthesis should decide whether Hayek's criterion is form, ignorance of beneficiaries, or legislative intent, and test each against his own accepted examples (factory laws, p. 115).
- Is the ignorance claim true? Legislators often know which groups gain from a general rule (property law, inheritance). If so, is the liberal legislator "impartial" in his sense (p. 111)?
- Who enforces the limit on legislation (p. 116)? The chapter names bills of rights and tradition but not courts or constitutional design; check whether ch15 or the conclusion addresses this.
- The German decline of the Rechtsstaat before Hitler (p. 113) is asserted. Does ch12 give the evidence?
- Does the Rule of Law argument reach non-comprehensive intervention (price controls in one market, industrial licensing), or only the "collectivist kind" of planning (p. 109)? The licensing example of privilege (p. 114) suggests it reaches further than central planning.
- The Kant quotation (p. 115) is unlocated by the editor (p. 119); treat it as unverified in any later citation.
- The picketing example (p. 114) shows the "inaction can be illiberal" point being used against trade-union practice; compare ch08 and ch09 on organised labour.
