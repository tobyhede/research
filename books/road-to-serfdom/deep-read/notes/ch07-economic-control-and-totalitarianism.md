# ch07: Economic Control and Totalitarianism (pp. 121–130)

## Question this chapter answers

Can authoritarian direction be confined to "merely" economic matters, leaving political and personal freedom intact? Hayek's answer: no. There are no separate economic ends; whoever controls all economic activity controls the means for all ends and so decides which ends are served. Economic control is therefore control of the whole of life, as consumer and still more as producer.

## Leading propositions

- Most planners accept that a directed economy must be run on more or less dictatorial lines, and offer the consolation that this will apply only to economic matters (p. 121).
- That consolation rests on the false belief that there are purely economic ends separate from the other ends of life (p. 121).
- Money is an instrument of freedom; replacing money rewards with rewards in kind (distinctions, privileges, housing, travel) means whoever fixes the reward also fixes the form in which it is enjoyed (p. 122).
- Economic matters seem unimportant only because, and only where, we are free to let economic losses fall on our least important wants (pp. 122-123).
- To be controlled in economic pursuits is to be controlled in everything, since any purpose would have to be declared and approved (p. 123).
- Whoever has sole control of the means must determine which ends are served, "in short, what men should believe and strive for" (p. 123).
- The planner's power over private life does not depend on rationing; it comes from control of production, and would be nearly as complete if consumers were nominally free to spend (p. 124).
- An authority directing the whole economy would be the most powerful monopolist conceivable, able to discriminate between persons to any degree (p. 124).
- In a competitive society obstacles come from others wanting the same means, not from anyone's disapproval of our ends; in a directed economy the authority would certainly favour some ends and block others (p. 124).
- Control over us as producers matters even more than as consumers; some freedom to choose work is probably more important than freedom to spend (pp. 124-125).
- Planning must control entry to occupations or terms of remuneration or both, so "free choice of occupation" under planning is fictitious (pp. 125-126).
- Fixing remuneration by category excludes people as effectively as direct exclusion and removes the ability of atypical people to make special arrangements (p. 126).
- Under planning the individual becomes more than ever a mere means to abstractions such as "social welfare" (p. 126).
- The alternative to things being available at a price is not free choice but orders, prohibitions, and "the favor of the mighty" (p. 126).
- The claim of potential plenty is false; the productivity claim for planning is being abandoned by serious students; the only serious argument left for planning is distributive (pp. 127-128).
- Securing a predetermined distribution requires planning the whole economic system (p. 128).
- Central planning today would not be a return to pre-liberal regulation; because of the division of labour it would require direction of a far greater part of life than ever before (p. 128).
- Political freedom is meaningless without economic freedom, but in the sense of freedom of economic activity, not freedom from economic care (p. 129).

## Arguments

**A. Means and ends: the core argument (pp. 121-123)**

- **Premises**: (p. 121) No ends are purely economic; "economic" names the general means (money, opportunity) to unspecified ends. (p. 122) In a free economy a person decides which of their wants bears an economic loss, so such losses fall on the margin. (p. 123) That marginality is a product of the individual's power to choose, not a property of economic matters. (p. 123) An authority directing all economic activity allocates the limited means among all ends.
- **Unstated premises**: that control is *sole* and *total* ("whoever has sole control of the means", p. 123), so the argument applies in full only to complete central direction; that controlling means entails ranking ends in detail and not merely setting aggregate constraints (for example an income) within which individuals still choose; that the authority will use its ability and not abstain.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 123) Economic control is control of the means for all our ends, and so of which ends are pursued; the planner, not the individual, decides what is marginal.

**B. The monopolist argument for consumption (p. 124)**

- **Premises**: Freedom of choice in competition rests on being able to turn to another supplier. A single authority is a universal monopolist. It decides what is available, in what quantity, to which districts and groups. Planning is advocated precisely so that approved ends can be favoured.
- **Unstated premise**: that there is no functioning mechanism (consumer sovereignty through prices set to clear markets under public ownership, as market socialists proposed) by which the authority would be bound to follow consumer demand. He grants nominal freedom to spend but treats production decisions as unresponsive to it.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 124) The authority "would control what we consume almost as effectively as if it directly told us how to spend our income".

**C. The occupation argument (pp. 124-126)**

- **Premises**: (p. 125) Work takes up a large part of life and determines where and among whom we live. (p. 125) What matters is having some choice and a possible way out. (p. 125) To plan, the authority must control entry into trades, or remuneration, or both. (p. 126) Fixing pay for whole categories and selecting by objective test removes the weight of how strongly someone wants a job and removes special arrangements. (p. 126) To make its task manageable the authority must reduce human diversity to "a few categories of readily interchangeable units".
- **Unstated premise**: that a single employer or wage-setter cannot offer varied contracts; that the escape routes in a competitive economy (accepting lower pay to get a start) are really open to the poor and "apparently less able".
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 125-126) Free choice of occupation under planning is "purely fictitious"; conditions become those of one large organisation with no exit.

**D. The price-of-values argument (pp. 126-127)**

- **Premises**: (p. 127) Higher values (life, health, honour) can often be preserved only at material cost. (p. 127) All ends compete for the same means, so someone must choose. (p. 127) We could abolish road deaths by abolishing cars and do not.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 126-127) Objecting to the cash nexus amounts to demanding that the choice be made for us; the real alternative to price is command and favour.

**E. Only distribution is left as a reason to plan, and it requires total planning (pp. 127-128)**

- **Premises**: (p. 127) Potential plenty is untrue. (p. 128) Serious socialist economists now hope only that planning will equal competitive efficiency. (p. 128) A distribution matching a predetermined standard requires consciously deciding who gets what.
- **Unstated premise**: that distributive aims come only in the strong form of a complete "predetermined standard", so that weaker aims (a floor, reduced dispersion) are not separately considered here.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 128) "we must plan the whole economic system" to achieve it, and the price is likely more discontent and oppression than the free play of economic forces caused. The last clause is put as a question, not asserted.

**F. No return to the past (p. 128)**

- **Premises**: Pre-liberal regulation amounted to a general semi-permanent framework; the apparatus could impose only very general directions; it reached only activities within the social division of labour, and people then lived largely on their own products. The division of labour now makes almost every activity part of a social process and cannot be reversed without failing to maintain the population.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 128) Central planning now would direct far more of life than any earlier regime.

**Position versus argument.** He holds that freedom of economic activity is the prerequisite of every other freedom (p. 129). His stated reason is the means-ends structure (A), not a claim that economic liberty is intrinsically more valuable than other liberties; indeed he grants that economic matters are rightly felt as less important within a market economy (p. 122).

## Evidence offered

- **Conceptual / mechanism argument** carries nearly the whole chapter (pp. 121-126). The conclusions about what a planner "would" do are derived, not observed.
- **Quotation from opponents**: Stuart Chase, that political democracy can remain "if it confines itself to all but economic matters" (p. 121). The editor notes Hayek gives no source and that the line can be found, also without its original source, in a Lippmann article of 1936 (p. 129), so it is second-hand.
- **Authority**: Robbins on the meaning of economic causation (Hayek's note 3, p. 129); Colin Clark, quoted at length in Hayek's note 5 against "poverty in the midst of plenty" (p. 130). The Clark passage is the only quantitatively grounded evidence in the chapter, and it supports the denial of potential plenty, not the claims about control. Hayek stresses Clark's "undoubted progressive views" to make it testimony against interest (p. 130).
- **Historical example**: foreign-exchange control in "most Continental countries" as "the decisive advance on the path to totalitarianism", closing all means of escape and foreign contact (Hayek's note 4, pp. 129-130). No particular country or measure is cited.
- **Historical example**: organisation of leisure in Russia, Germany and Italy; the German word Freizeitgestaltung (Hayek's note 6, p. 130).
- **Historical generalisation, unsupported**: "In almost all known instances of planning" controls on entry to occupations were among the first measures (p. 125); pre-liberal economic regimentation "amounted to little more than" a general framework of rules (p. 128). No instances are given.
- **Hypothetical illustrations**: the plain girl who wants to be a saleswoman, the weakly boy (p. 126); the person who prefers irregular hours and uncertain income (p. 126); abolishing automobiles to end accidents (p. 127).
- **Bare assertion / language outrunning evidence**: "it is certain that it would use its powers to assist some ends and to prevent the realization of others" (p. 124); "whoever talks about potential plenty is either dishonest or does not know what he is talking about" (p. 127); "too obvious a consequence" that planning needs a commander-in-chief unfettered by democratic procedure (p. 121); the claim that money gives the poor man today "a range greater than that which not many generations ago was open to the wealthy" (p. 122).
- **Report of the state of expert opinion** without names: most students are abandoning the productivity claim for planning (pp. 127-128).

**Editorial additions (Caldwell)**

- Note 1: source of the Belloc epigraph (p. 129). Note 2: the Chase quotation traced only to Lippmann, original source not given (p. 129). Note 3: editor quotes Robbins's definition of economic versus non-economic causes (p. 129). Note 5: editor identifies Clark and explains "progressive views" by his Labour activity (p. 130). ## Concessions and qualifications

- The planners' argument appeals "to our best instincts and often attract the finest minds"; if economic activity really concerned only the inferior side of life, relieving us of it would be right (p. 121).
- There is an "important kernel of truth" in the belief that economic matters affect only the less important ends, "quite justified in a market economy" (p. 122).
- A planning authority would probably *not* exploit its monopoly for maximum financial gain as a private monopolist would (p. 124).
- "No doubt it is true that even in the best of worlds this freedom will be very limited. Few people ever have an abundance of choice of occupation" (p. 125).
- Not all is for the best in the present world "or has been so in the most liberal past"; much could be done to improve opportunities of choice (p. 125).
- **State activity explicitly accepted**: "the state can do a great deal to help the spreading of knowledge and information and to assist mobility" (p. 125). He adds that this is "almost precisely the opposite" of planning as advocated.
- The price paid in a competitive society is "often a cruelly high price" (p. 126).
- It "is not surprising" that people wish to be relieved of bitter choices (p. 127).
- Distribution is "the only argument for planning which can be seriously pressed" (p. 128), and it is "indisputable" that a predetermined distribution requires planning the whole system (p. 128): he grants the planners' logic on this point.
- Under-used capacity is a real question in the U.S.A. and sometimes in Britain, Germany and France (Clark, quoted with approval in Hayek's note 5, p. 130).
- The claim about more oppression under planning is framed as "the question remains whether" (p. 128), not as settled.
- He grants that political freedom is meaningless without economic freedom is "true enough" (p. 129), accepting the socialists' slogan while reversing its sense.

## Quotes

- "What in ordinary language is misleadingly called the “economic motive” means merely the desire for general opportunity, the desire for power to achieve unspecified ends." (p. 121)
- "It would be much truer to say that money is one of the greatest instruments of freedom ever invented by man." (p. 122)
- "Economic control is not merely control of a sector of human life which can be separated from the rest; it is the control of the means for all our ends. And whoever has sole control of the means must also determine which ends are to be served, which values are to be rated higher and which lower—in short, what men should believe and strive for." (p. 123)
- "Our freedom of choice in a competitive society rests on the fact that, if one person refuses to satisfy our wishes, we can turn to another. But if we face a monopolist we are at his mercy. And an authority directing the whole economic system would be the most powerful monopolist conceivable." (p. 124)
- "Here as elsewhere the state can do a great deal to help the spreading of knowledge and information and to assist mobility." (p. 125)
- "The alternative is not, however, complete freedom of choice, but orders and prohibitions which must be obeyed and, in the last resort, the favor of the mighty." (p. 126)
- "It is indisputable that if we want to secure a distribution of wealth which conforms to some predetermined standard, if we want consciously to decide who is to have what, we must plan the whole economic system." (p. 128)
- "The economic freedom which is the prerequisite of any other freedom cannot be the freedom from economic care which the socialists promise us and which can be obtained only by relieving the individual at the same time of the necessity and of the power of choice; it must be the freedom of our economic activity which, with the right of choice, inevitably also carries the risk and the responsibility of that right." (p. 129)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch05**: opens from ch05's conclusion that a directed economy needs a commander not fettered by democratic procedure (p. 121; ch05, pp. 104-105) and turns to the fallback claim that dictatorship can be confined to economics. The requirement that the planner rank "the relative importance of the different needs" (p. 123) is ch05's complete ethical code (ch05, p. 98) seen from the individual's side.
- **Depends on ch02**: the two senses of economic freedom (p. 129) restate what I take to be ch02's distinction between freedom from coercion and the socialist "new freedom" as power or wealth (not read in this pass; check against the ch02 note). Synthesis should read p. 129 with that chapter.
- **Depends on ch06**: price "not determined by the conscious will of anybody" (p. 124) parallels the formal rule whose effects on particular people are unknown (ch06, p. 110); both make impersonality the mark of freedom. The planner's ability to "discriminate between persons to any degree" (p. 124) is the ch06 violation of formal equality.
- **Feeds ch08**: the admission that distribution is the one serious argument for planning (p. 128) sets ch08's agenda; the monopolist argument (p. 124) becomes ch08's claim that a single owner of all means has complete power over us (ch08, p. 132).
- **Feeds ch09**: the closing line that freedom of economic activity "carries the risk and the responsibility" (p. 129) is the premise of ch09 on security and freedom. The remark that knowing escape is possible makes hard positions bearable (p. 125) recurs in the discussion of security.
- **Feeds ch11**: "what men should believe and strive for" (p. 123) and the note on exchange control as control of opinion (pp. 129-130) anticipate The End of Truth.
- **Tension (my observation)**: "Power" is used in two ways. The "economic motive" is "the desire for power to achieve unspecified ends" (p. 121), where power is general ability or opportunity, the very sense of freedom-as-power that ch02 attacks socialists for using. Elsewhere power is one agent's ability to decide for another (p. 124). Hayek's defence of money as freedom (p. 122) leans on the first sense.
- **Tension (my observation)**: the argument needs "sole control" (p. 123) and "the whole economic system" (p. 124), but the footnote on exchange control treats a single partial control as "the complete delivery of the individual to the tyranny of the state" (p. 129). The chapter moves between total and partial control without marking the step.
- **Tension (my observation)**: the claim that in a market "economic loss will always deprive us only of what we regard as the least important of the desires we were able to satisfy" (p. 122) holds for those with a margin. For the very poor the marginal want may be a necessity; he concedes a "cruelly high price" (p. 126) but not that this weakens the marginality argument. ch09's guaranteed minimum may be the implicit answer.
- **Tension (my observation)**: p. 128 says pre-liberal regimentation was "a general and semipermanent framework of rules", which by ch06's test is close to the Rule of Law, yet ch01, as I recall it, presents the pre-liberal order as the unfreedom from which liberalism released Europe (not read in this pass; check against the ch01 note).

## Open questions

- Does the means-ends argument survive when control is partial? What share or kind of economic control produces control of ends? ch05's 53 per cent figure (ch05, p. 100) and the exchange-control note (pp. 129-130) are the only markers offered.
- Market socialism (Lange, Lerner, Dickinson) proposed consumer choice and free choice of occupation with planned investment and public ownership. Hayek asserts the planner "would" not be bound by demand (p. 124) and must control entry or pay (p. 125). Is this argued anywhere in the book, or only in his technical writings? Check ch03 and the bibliographical note.
- Is the distributive aim only ever "a predetermined standard" of who gets what (p. 128)? Synthesis should test whether the book has an argument against patterned redistribution through general rules, as distinct from allocation by authority (compare ch06, p. 113 and ch09).
- The escape-by-sacrifice claim (pp. 125-126) is an empirical claim about mobility in competitive societies; no evidence is offered. How does it sit with ch08's concession about the poor man's chances?
- The monopolist would "presumably not" seek maximum gain (p. 124) but would "certainly" favour approved ends (p. 124). Why is benevolence presumed for one and not the other? ch10 supplies the selection argument; test whether ch07 depends on it.
- The state role in "the spreading of knowledge and information" and assisting mobility (p. 125) is unspecified. Does it include public education, labour exchanges, retraining? Collect with the other concessions (ch03, ch06 p. 115, ch09).
