## Question this chapter answers

How did Hayek first state the thesis in 1933, how did the first American readers and publishers assess the finished book in 1943-45, how did the book come to be published and sell, and what did Friedman think experience had shown by 1971 and 1994?

## Leading propositions

**D1. Hayek, Nazi-Socialism (1933)**

- Events in Germany are the culmination of tendencies dating from long before the Great War (p. 238).
- It is superficial to call the forces now dominant reactionary in the sense of wanting a return to the order of 1914 (p. 238).
- The persecution of Marxists and democrats obscures the fact that National Socialism is a genuine socialist movement (p. 238).
- Its alliance with industrial and landowning nationalists hid its socialist character; those groups misjudged it, and many German capitalists were themselves influenced by socialist ideas (p. 238).
- The Nazis objected to the older socialist parties' internationalism and liberal cultural programme, hardly at all to their economic policy (p. 238).
- The party programme (Feder's 25 points) is a cruder, less rational socialism dominated by hatred of anything capitalistic (pp. 238-239).
- The anti-capitalism is genuine, not propaganda: many leaders are former communists or socialists and the intellectual milieu was uniformly anti-liberal and anti-capitalist (p. 239).
- The movement's anti-rational, mystical strand derives chiefly from Marx's doctrine that thought is class-conditioned, extended from class to nation and race (p. 239).
- Anti-liberalism and anti-rationalism are inevitably bound together, since the superiority of a privileged ruling group cannot be proved and must be accepted (pp. 239-240).
- Anti-liberalism confined to the economic field leads inevitably to universal compulsion, intolerance and the suppression of intellectual freedom, as Germany and Russia illustrate (p. 240).
- Being middle-class, not proletarian, socialism changes little: private property will probably be recognised nominally and hollowed out by guilds, state control and restriction of income (p. 240).
- Forecast: if the radical wing prevails, as seems increasingly probable, Germany will have something differing from communism in little but name (p. 240).
- Other nations should not look down on Germany; they have long been following the same process at a distance, and misjudging Nazism is likely to speed that up (pp. 240-241).
- There seems little prospect that the intellectual reversal elsewhere will come in time to stop other countries following Germany in the last step also (p. 241).

**D2. Knight, reader's report (1943)**

- The book is a masterly performance of the job it sets itself (p. 241).
- That job is to show two things: that socialism or a planned economy will inevitably lead to totalitarianism and dictatorship, and that such an order will inevitably fall to the worst individuals (p. 241).
- The argument is political, not economic; there is little or no economic theory in it (p. 241).
- The argument is well stated, compact and conclusive, but Knight already held the position, so a report from an opponent should be sought (p. 241).
- Doubts about American publication: an English standpoint, a high scholarly level, and much assumed knowledge of central Europe; it is hardly a popular book (pp. 241-242).
- The work is essentially negative: it hardly considers alternatives and inadequately recognises the necessity and political inevitability of wide governmental activity in economic life (p. 242).
- It deals only with the simpler fallacies behind the popular demand for control, and does not discuss the serious shortcomings of the free economy of around 1900 (p. 242).
- The account of Germany is over-simplified: it attributes practically everything to the socialist movement and state paternalism, relegates militarism to a minor role, and neglects late feudalism, delayed unification and industrialisation, the circumstances of post-1918 parliamentarism, and anti-Semitism (p. 242).
- These omissions do not invalidate the general conclusion for Knight but weaken the presentation (p. 242).
- Verdict: able, limited in scope, somewhat one-sided; he doubts it would have a wide American market or change many readers' positions (pp. 242-243).

**D3. Marschak, reader's report (1943)**

- The American debate on free enterprise has not been on a high level; this book may start a more scholarly one (p. 243).
- It will give friends of free enterprise new material: Hayek's reading of the English scene (labour and industrial monopolists driving jointly toward a collective economy) and German support for the contention that socialism is the father of Nazism (p. 243).
- The unconvinced will learn even more: Hayek has a wholesome contempt for the quasi-scientific method of trends, and those who love planning because they love the inevitable may come to think in terms of ends and means (p. 243).
- Hayek gives little food for such concrete thinking; the book is almost exclusively critical, black-and-white, impatient of compromise (p. 243).
- The best chapters are negative or formal; the Rule of Law chapter is excellent, but Hayek says little on how the Rule of Law could shape instruments against unemployment or monopoly, giving only vague hints (p. 243).
- American readers, who include monetary and fiscal policy, social security and progressive income tax under planning and socialism, will want a more concrete demarcation between good planning and planning proper (p. 243).
- The non-economic chapters are more impressive than the economic ones (p. 243).
- Hayek is less concrete than Lippmann or Chase but sharper because more abstract; the book cannot be by-passed (p. 243).

**D4. Chamberlain, foreword (1944)**

- Full employment, planning, social security and freedom from want cannot be had when made conscious objects of government policy (p. 244).
- Italy, Russia and Germany show these slogans leading to disaster; in America only war rescued the politicians of full employment (p. 244).
- Hayek is one of a handful who dared trace a connection between the slogans and the terror, and he sees the English succumbing by degrees to controlled-economy ideas (p. 244).
- The book is a warning to the British and by implication to Americans (p. 244).
- Its logic is incontestable: those goods come only as by-products of a system that releases the free energies of individuals (p. 244).
- When the good of society is the overriding test of state action, planners must be able to move in on any sector, and individual rights that obstruct must go (p. 244).
- State dynamism creates uncertainty: markets obeyed relatively objective laws, governments are subject to whim (pp. 244-245).
- As Hayek demonstrates, the end result of that uncertainty is civil war or the dictatorship that averts it (p. 245).
- The alternative to planning is the rule of law; Hayek is no devotee of laissez faire and his design admits minimum-wage standards, health standards, a minimum of compulsory social insurance and some government investment (p. 245).
- Hayek belongs, with modifications, to the Manchester line, not the school of the Webbs (p. 245).

**D5. Scoon, letter to Grattan (1945)**

- The book reached the Press in December 1943 in Routledge page proofs, was read by two outside scholarly readers and approved by the Publications Committee at the end of that month (p. 245).
- Report A came from a reliable, middle-of-the-road man who was already on Hayek's side and asked for a report from the opposition; report B came from one of the most definitely progressive economists in the country (p. 245).
- A committee of thirteen men of differing opinion approved publication; Scoon holds the trial could not have been more objective (p. 246).
- At first the project looked far from world-shaking (p. 246).
- The Press asked Hayek to be specific about the book's application to the United States; revisions were agreed, with Hayek having the final say (p. 246).
- It was taken on purely as a scholarly work by a foreign author without much of a name in the United States (p. 246).
- Bitterness about the book has grown with its impact, and people go off half-cocked without reading what Hayek actually says (pp. 247-248).
- Hayek regrets that his conclusions are on paper but not the process by which he reached them; an annotated edition is being considered (p. 248).

**D6. Friedman, 1971 introduction (embedded)**

- For years the book was the most frequent answer when he asked individualists how they left the collectivist orthodoxy; it was a revelation especially to those who had served in the armed forces and had seen a collectivist organisation in action (p. 248).
- The message is no less needed, but the immediate issues and jargon have changed from central planning to urban, environmental, consumer and poverty crises, each said to need expanded government programmes (pp. 248-249).
- Promotion of collectivism is still combined with the profession of individualist values; those values can be achieved only in a liberal order (p. 249).
- The relation between ends and means is widely misunderstood; the case for collectivism is simple, emotional and false, the case for individualism subtle, indirect and rational (pp. 249-250).
- In the world of affairs: in 1945 Hayek's sympathisers expected steady growth of the state, yet in practice the movement did not go much farther in Britain, France or the United States, and Germany moved sharply toward a liberal economic policy (p. 250).
- Two forces produced this unexpected check: the conflict between central planning and individual liberty became patent, above all in Britain over the control of engagements order, and liberty was preferred; and collectivism proved inefficient (p. 250).
- The check did not stop the growth of government; it diverted it into indirect regulation and transfer programmes, and government's share of national income kept rising (pp. 250-251).
- In the world of ideas the outcome is worse, although experience has strongly confirmed Hayek's central insight that central direction leads to serfdom and voluntary cooperation to freedom, and a secondary theme that central direction leads to poverty (p. 251).
- After a brief liberal interlude the intellectual climate has again turned against free enterprise, and it is hard to know why (p. 251).
- The battle for freedom must be won repeatedly; the socialists in all parties must be persuaded or defeated (pp. 251-252).

**D6. Friedman, 1994 comments and Note on Publishing History**

- The book is a true classic with a timeless central message, in some ways more relevant to the United States in 1994 than in 1944 (p. 248).
- The 1971 paragraph on growing intellectual support for collectivism no longer rings fully true: the fall of the Berlin Wall and the collapse of communism reduced defenders of Marxian collectivism to a small band, and there is wide agreement that socialism failed (p. 252).
- That conversion is deceptive: most intellectuals still favour any expansion of government advertised as protecting individuals, relieving poverty, protecting the environment or promoting equality; national health care is his example (p. 252).
- In 1944 opinion was more hostile to Hayek's theme but practice conformed to it more; government then was smaller and less intrusive (p. 252).
- Britain shows the same: Labour now defends private markets and Thatcher partly reversed state ownership, but she could not call on the reservoir of liberal sentiment that led to the withdrawal of the control of engagements order, and government spends more and intrudes more than in 1950 (p. 252).
- Summary judgement: individualism is preached and socialism practised, with only slight overstatement (pp. 252-253).
- Hayek's writings, and this book especially, were an important intellectual source of the disintegration of faith in communism on both sides of the Iron Curtain (p. 254).
- Scoon's remark about people not reading what Hayek actually says is still true (p. 254).
- Freedom of the press is more secure than it would otherwise be because of this book (p. 254).

**Acknowledgments (Caldwell)**

## Arguments

**D1. Hayek 1933: Nazism is socialism**

- **Premises**: the Nazi programme's economics differ from the other socialist parties' only in being cruder (p. 238) + its dominant feature is hatred of capitalism (p. 239) + its leaders and intellectual milieu come from socialism and anti-liberalism (p. 239) + its quarrel with the left concerned internationalism and culture, not economics (p. 238) + (unstated) a movement is to be classified by its programme and intellectual pedigree, not by its allies, its victims or what it does in office.
  **Conclusion**: National Socialism is a genuine socialist movement (p. 238).
- My observation: Caldwell's own notes cut against the evidential base. Hitler abandoned Feder's programme to win industrialists (note 2, pp. 254-255), and the state commissioners Hayek cites were self-appointed party radicals rooted out by late 1933 (note 6, p. 255).

**D1. Hayek 1933: collectivism cannot be confined**

- **Premises**: beyond certain limits collective action in the interest of all requires that everyone be coerced into accepting the rulers' view of the common interest (p. 240) + coercion must then reach individuals' ultimate aims and world-view (p. 240) + (unstated) there is no stable stopping point between limited economic collectivism and that threshold.
  **Conclusion**: economic anti-liberalism leads inevitably to universal compulsion and the suppression of intellectual freedom (p. 240).
- **Extension**: other countries show the same tendencies (growing state activity, trade and migration restrictions, sympathy for planning and dictatorship) (p. 241), so Germany's outcome is the ultimate and necessary outcome of a shared process (p. 241). Hayek uses the words inevitably and necessary here in his own voice.

**D2. Knight: sound conclusion, weak case**

- **Premises**: the historical argument attributes Nazism almost wholly to socialism and paternalism (p. 242) + other factors plainly mattered (p. 242) + the book ignores the real defects of the free economy and the necessity of extensive government action (p. 242).
  **Conclusion**: the general conclusion stands, in Knight's view, but the presentation is one-sided and limited (p. 242).
- **Premises**: English standpoint, high level, assumed European knowledge (pp. 241-242).
  **Conclusion**: a narrow American market and few conversions (pp. 242-243). Events falsified this forecast (Scoon, p. 247).

**D3. Marschak: valuable but not constructive**

- **Premises**: the book is critical, not constructive, by its own admission (p. 243) + it gives only vague hints on applying the Rule of Law to unemployment and monopoly (p. 243) + Americans count fiscal policy, social security and progressive taxation as planning (p. 243).
  **Conclusion**: the book needs, and lacks, a concrete line between permissible and impermissible planning; it should still be published because it raises the level of debate (p. 243).

**D4. Chamberlain: from planning to dictatorship via uncertainty**

- **Premises**: planners acting for the good of the whole must be free to intervene anywhere (p. 244) + this makes government action unpredictable to individuals, unlike the market (pp. 244-245) + individuals cannot plan under that uncertainty (p. 245) + (unstated) the frustration of individual plans produces irreconcilable conflict.
  **Conclusion**: the end result is civil war or the dictatorship that averts it (p. 245). Chamberlain credits this to Hayek as demonstrated.
- My observation: this is a compressed and harder version than Hayek's own; it runs the mechanism through uncertainty, and it states flatly that the welfare goals cannot be had as objects of policy (p. 244) while also listing social insurance and minimum wages as compatible with Hayek's design (p. 245).

**D6. Friedman 1971: why collectivism was checked but government grew**

- **Premises**: central planning visibly conflicted with liberty, and where liberal values were strong the planning was dropped (p. 250) + government enterprise proved inefficient (p. 250) + government growth moved into regulation and transfers (p. 250).
  **Conclusion**: Hayek's central insight is confirmed, yet the state's share of income keeps rising and the threat persists in a new form (pp. 250-251).
- **Premises**: paired cases of similar peoples under central direction and under markets show tyranny and misery on one side, freedom and affluence on the other (p. 251) + (unstated) the pairs differ in nothing else that matters.
  **Conclusion**: experience strongly confirms the thesis (p. 251).

**D6. Friedman 1994: more relevant now**

- **Premises**: United States government spending rose from 25 percent of national income in 1950 to nearly 45 percent in 1993 (p. 252) + new programmes and regulations have accumulated (p. 252) + intellectuals favour each new expansion (p. 252) + (unstated) the size of government spending measures the collectivism Hayek warned against.
  **Conclusion**: practice is now further from Hayek's theme than in 1944 even though opinion is nearer (p. 252).

## Evidence offered

**D1. Hayek 1933**

- Documentary: the party programme and its circulation (p. 238). Biographical generalisation about leaders' socialist pasts, without names (p. 239). Intellectual-historical: Die Tat, the Berlin students' protest, the sociology of knowledge (p. 239). Current events: state commissioners in industry (p. 240).
- Much is forecast, hedged with probably and likely (p. 240), set beside unhedged claims of inevitability and necessity (pp. 240-241). The confident language outruns a memorandum written within months of the seizure of power.

**D2. Knight**

- Reasoned assessment by a sympathetic expert; no data. He lists the omitted historical factors but does not argue them (p. 242). He discloses his prior agreement (p. 241).

**D3. Marschak**

- Reasoned assessment with page references to Hayek's manuscript, which Caldwell warns do not match the present pagination (note 9, p. 255).

**D4. Chamberlain**

- Historical illustration by juxtaposition: Italy's African war, the Five-Year Plan and three million kulaks, German full employment and six hundred thousand Jews, American pump-priming (p. 244). These establish coincidence, not causation, and the phrase that the logic is incontestable (p. 244) is promotional. One authority quoted: Drucker on planners as improvisers (p. 245).

**D5. Scoon: publication and sales facts (primary source, publisher's files)**

- Published in England on 10 March 1944 by Routledge; first printing 2,000, sold out in about a month (p. 246).
- American contract signed at the beginning of April 1944 (p. 246).
- American first printing set at 2,000, influenced by competition from Mises's Omnipotent Government; Chamberlain asked to introduce it; title kept (p. 246).
- American publication delayed from July to 18 September 1944, when the English edition was in its third printing (p. 247).
- First review seen: Orville Prescott, New York Times, 20 September, neutral (p. 247). After Henry Hazlitt's front-page review in the Sunday Times Book Review, a second printing of 5,000; on 27 September a third of 5,000, raised to 10,000 the next day (p. 247).
- Requests for German, Spanish, Dutch and other translation rights within days; Reader's Digest made the first and best offer for magazine rights (p. 247).
- Stores out of stock by the first week of October; Routledge arrangement covered Canada (p. 247).
- Hayek's American lecture trip was the Press's idea (Brandt's) to sustain sales, and was handed to the National Concert and Artists Corporation (p. 247).
- By 2 May 1945: seventh printing, nearly 50,000 sold; the Book-of-the-Month Club distribution of the Digest condensation above 600,000 (p. 247).
- The book is used as collateral reading in university courses (p. 248).
- Scoon says the title paraphrases Russell's Roads to Freedom (p. 246); Caldwell says he is wrong and that the title came from Tocqueville (note 18, p. 255).
- Caldwell identifies the unnamed parties: the economics department member who suggested the book was Knight, the friend in Washington was Aaron Director, the colleague who helped with revisions was Machlup (notes 15-17, p. 255); reports A and B are Knight's and Marschak's (notes 7-8, p. 255).
- Scoon cautions that he gathered the material from files and may have lost perspective (p. 248).

**D6. Friedman: publication and sales facts (secondary; his note 20 credits research by Alex Philipson of the Press, pp. 255-256)**

- Hayek began work in September 1940 (p. 253).
- Machlup, then at the Office of Alien Property Custodian in Washington, was authorised to place the book; three American publishers turned it down, at least one as unfit for publication by a reputable house (p. 253). Friedman's source for the last is Hayek's 1956 foreword (note 21, p. 256).
- Machlup showed the proofs to Director; Knight then presented them to the Press with Director's suggestion (p. 253).
- Chicago edition published 18 September 1944, first printing 2,000 (p. 253).
- Reader's Digest condensation April 1945; more than 600,000 copies of it distributed by the Book of the Month Club (p. 254). Caldwell refers to his introduction for further discussion of that figure (note 22, p. 256).
- Seventh printing limited to 10,000 by a paper shortage and reduced to pocket size (p. 254).
- Over fifty years the Press sold over a quarter of a million copies: 81,000 hardback and 175,000 paperback; first Chicago paperback 1956 (p. 254).
- Nearly twenty authorised translations, on Laurence Hayek's report; unauthorised underground translations in Russian, Polish, Czech and possibly others (p. 254).

**D6. Friedman: evidence for the substantive claims**

- Anecdote: his own informal inquiries among individualists (p. 248).
- Natural-experiment comparison: East and West Germany, which he says almost provide a controlled scientific experiment (p. 251). Further pairs by assertion: Israel and Egypt; Malaya, Singapore, Thailand, Formosa, Hong Kong and Japan against India, Indonesia and Communist China (p. 251). No figures; the results are called crystal clear (p. 251). The pairs differ in many respects other than economic organisation, and the comparison supports the efficiency theme more directly than the political one.
- Emigration controls as an index: the wall, and China guarding its borders (p. 251).
- One statistic: total United States government spending from 25 percent of national income in 1950 to nearly 45 percent in 1993 (p. 252). No source given.
- Legislative examples: Great Society, Medicare and Medicaid, Clean Air and Americans with Disabilities acts (p. 252).
- Assertion without evidence: that the book was an important source of the collapse of faith in communism, introduced by the phrase little doubt (p. 254), and that it has made press freedom more secure (p. 254).
- My observation: Friedman's own record sits uneasily with the thesis he says it confirms. He reports that the collectivist advance stopped, that liberty prevailed over planning in Britain, and that by 1993 the state took nearly 45 percent of income with press freedom intact (pp. 250, 252, 254). He does not say what serfdom these economies exhibit.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Who reads the thesis as inevitability.** Knight states it as inevitability twice over (p. 241). Hayek's own 1933 memo uses inevitably and necessary outcome (pp. 240-241). Chamberlain gives a deterministic end result (p. 245). Marschak instead praises Hayek's contempt for trends and for the love of the inevitable (p. 243). Friedman speaks of predictions about the consequences of collectivism (p. 248) and of two roads (p. 251), a directional claim. Hayek in 1976 denies the any-movement version (fm02, p. 55), and the Conclusion attacks belief in inevitable tendencies (ch16, p. 235). So a sympathetic expert reader in 1943 took the inevitability reading as the plain sense, before any hostile reviewer did.
- **Constructive gap.** Knight (p. 242) and Marschak (p. 243) both say the book is negative and does not draw the line between acceptable and unacceptable state action. Hayek says the same of the book in the Conclusion (ch16, p. 234) and in 1976 (fm02, p. 54). Chamberlain alone offers a list of what the design permits (p. 245).
- **Welfare measures.** Chamberlain says social security cannot be had as a conscious object of policy (p. 244) and that a minimum of compulsory social insurance is compatible with Hayek's design (p. 245). Friedman classes transfer programmes as the new channel of collectivism (p. 250), in line with Hayek's 1976 redefinition of socialism (fm02, p. 55) and harder than Hayek's 1956 view that some welfare aims are laudable (fm02, p. 43).
- **Control of engagements order.** Friedman: the order showed liberal values strong enough that planning was sacrificed (p. 250), and its withdrawal showed a reservoir of popular support for liberal values (p. 252). Hayek 1956: the same order shows the logic of planning forcing coercion (fm02, pp. 45-46), and there was not yet much ground to think Britain had changed course (fm02, p. 46). Friedman's reading matches Hayek's escape clause, not his alarm.
- **Tradition as safeguard.** Friedman credits the British tradition of liberty with stopping planning (p. 250). Hayek 1956 says even a strong tradition of political liberty is no safeguard (fm02, p. 46).
- **German history.** Knight's list of neglected factors (p. 242) is the standing objection to the main historical chapters and to the 1933 memo's single-cause account (pp. 238-239). Hayek's 1956 admission that he chose German illustrations for wartime reasons (fm02, pp. 39-40) does not answer it.
- **The 1933 memo and the book.** The memo already contains the pedigree thesis, the claim that economic collectivism cannot be confined, the sociology-of-knowledge point, and the warning that other nations follow at a distance (pp. 238-241). It is the Beveridge memorandum Hayek names in 1976 as the book's first step (fm02, p. 53; Acknowledgments, p. 256).
- **Publication story, small discrepancies.** Hayek 1956 and Friedman both give three rejections (fm02, p. 40; p. 253). Scoon has an economics department member suggesting the book and a friend in Washington supplying proofs (p. 245), whom Caldwell names as Knight and Director (p. 255); Friedman has Machlup in Washington showing proofs to Director and Knight presenting them (p. 253). The roles overlap but do not match exactly.
- **Audience.** Scoon confirms that the American text was revised to be specific about the United States (p. 246); Hayek 1956 says the book was written with only the British public in mind and reappears unchanged (fm02, p. 44). Both can be true, but the American edition is not simply the English text.
- **Title.** Scoon's Russell derivation (p. 246) is corrected by Caldwell (p. 255) and by Hayek's own 1956 note crediting Tocqueville (fm02, p. 52).
- **Forecasts that failed.** Knight predicted a small market (pp. 242-243); the 1933 memo predicted the radical economic wing would probably prevail (p. 240); Friedman's 1971 pessimism about ideas was partly withdrawn in 1994 (p. 252).

## Open questions

- Does the main text warrant Knight's summary (p. 241)? Test his two-part statement against the chapters on planning and democracy and on why the worst get on top.
- Is Knight right that the book neglects the real shortcomings of the pre-1914 free economy (p. 242)? Check what the main chapters concede about monopoly, unemployment and security.
- Where, if anywhere, does the book give the demarcation Marschak asks for between good planning and planning proper (p. 243)?
- Does the mixed-economy record Friedman describes (pp. 250-252) confirm, refute or simply fall outside the thesis? Decide what the thesis predicts for a state taking 45 percent of income without central planning.
