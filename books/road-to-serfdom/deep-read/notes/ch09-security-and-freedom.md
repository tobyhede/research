# ch09: Security and Freedom (pp. 143–153)

## Question this chapter answers

Economic security is widely held to be a precondition of real liberty. In what sense is that true, and at what point does the pursuit of security become a threat to liberty? Hayek's answer turns on splitting "security" into two kinds: one the state may and should provide, one it cannot provide to all without abolishing free choice of occupation.

Epigraphs (chosen by Hayek, sourced by the editor): Lenin 1917 on society as a single office and factory, and Trotsky 1937 on the sole-employer state, where the old rule that who does not work shall not eat becomes who does not obey shall not eat (p. 143; editor's sources at p. 153, notes 1-2).

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic security (general) | p. 143 | A vague, ambiguous term; in one sense a real condition of liberty (independence of mind is rare among those unsure of making their way), in another, "too absolute", sense the gravest threat to it (pp. 143-144). |
| Limited security (first kind) | p. 144 | Security against severe physical privation: a given minimum of sustenance for all; briefly, security of a minimum income. Achievable for all, hence no privilege; provided outside of and supplementary to the market (p. 144). |
| Absolute security (second kind) | p. 144 | Security of a given standard of life or of one's relative position; the particular income a person is thought to deserve. | Privilege | p. 144 | A benefit that cannot be had by all and is granted to some at the expense of others whose security is thereby diminished (pp. 144, 146). Security of the second kind is a privilege by definition; the first kind is not because it is universal. |
| Just remuneration | p. 145 | Pay commensurate with subjective merit (effort, intention) rather than with the objective results, i.e. usefulness to others. The demand for absolute security is another form of this demand (p. 145). |
| Planning "in the good sense" | p. 145 | Planning that does not replace the market, e.g. monetary policy against fluctuations; contrasted with the special kind of planning meant to supersede the market (p. 145). |
| Restrictionism / "control" | p. 149 | Limitation of output or entry so that prices secure an adequate return to insiders; the only way a market economy can guarantee incomes to particular producers; said to cover almost all planning actually practiced (p. 149). |

## Leading propositions

- Economic security is in one sense a true and important condition of liberty, but the term is ambiguous and, understood too absolutely, the striving for it becomes the gravest threat to freedom (pp. 143-144).
- There are two kinds of security: a minimum for all (limited) and a guaranteed particular income or relative position (absolute). The distinction largely coincides with security providable outside the market versus security providable only for some by controlling or abolishing the market (p. 144).
- The first kind can be guaranteed to all in a society as wealthy as ours without endangering general freedom (p. 144).
- The state may help organize comprehensive social insurance against genuinely insurable risks, give disaster relief, and combat general economic fluctuations, none of which is incompatible in principle with individual freedom (pp. 144-145).
- The demand for the second kind of security is a demand for remuneration by subjective merit; it is irreconcilable with freedom to choose one's employment (p. 145).
- Certainty of a given income cannot be given to all if any free choice of occupation is to remain; given to some, it is a privilege at the expense of others (p. 146).
- If remuneration is detached from usefulness, labor must be allocated by direct orders, and the question of incentives is above all one of information, not only of effort (pp. 146-147).
- A planned system needs harsher discipline than a market: for workers something approaching corporal punishment, for managers criminal liability; the manager's economic security is bought with the safety of freedom and life (pp. 147-148).
- There are only two types of social organization, commercial and military; full economic security for all is available only in the second and is the security of the barracks (p. 148).
- Piecemeal grants of security through restriction increase the insecurity of everyone left outside, and are largely responsible for increased unemployment in recent decades (pp. 149-150).
- As security becomes a privilege, social values shift: security rather than independence confers status, and people become willing to trade liberty for it. Germany led this transformation (pp. 150-152).
- Policy conclusion: adequate security against severe privation must be a main goal of policy, but it must be provided outside the market, with competition left unobstructed; and people must relearn that freedom has a material price (pp. 152-153).

## Concessions and qualifications (state provision Hayek explicitly accepts)

Listed separately because these are easily overstated or understated. Each is his position with his own conditions.

1. **Guaranteed minimum** of food, shelter and clothing sufficient to preserve health and the capacity to work, assured to everybody (p. 144). Conditions and caveats: (a) premised on a society that has reached our general level of wealth (p. 144); (b) the precise standard raises difficult questions (p. 144); (c) he flags as an important open question whether those who rely on the community should indefinitely enjoy all the same liberties as the rest (p. 144); (d) incautious handling could cause serious, perhaps dangerous, political problems (p. 144); (e) Hayek's own footnote adds that serious international problems arise if mere citizenship confers a right to a higher standard of living than elsewhere (p. 153, note 3); (f) it must be provided outside the market (pp. 144, 152). He notes this security had long been achieved for a considerable part of the English population (p. 144). Note the level: subsistence adequate to health and working capacity, not a share in general prosperity.
2. **Social insurance** for common hazards against which few can make adequate provision. The condition is that the risk be genuinely insurable: as with sickness and accident, neither the desire to avoid the calamity nor the effort to overcome it is as a rule weakened by assistance. On that condition the case for the state's helping to organize a comprehensive system is "very strong" (pp. 144-145). Caveats: the wording is that the state helps to organize, not that it must be sole provider; details will divide friends and opponents of competition; measures that make competition ineffective can be smuggled in under the name of social insurance (p. 145). Unemployment and old age are not named in the list of examples. (My observation: whether unemployment insurance passes his moral-hazard test is left unaddressed.)
3. **Disaster relief** for victims of acts of God such as earthquakes and floods; wherever communal action can mitigate disasters the individual can neither guard against nor provide for, it should "undoubtedly" be taken (p. 145).
4. **Combating general fluctuations and mass unemployment**, which he calls one of the gravest and most pressing problems of the time. It requires much planning in the good sense but not, or at least need not require, the planning that replaces the market (p. 145). Preferred route: monetary policy, compatible even with nineteenth-century liberalism (p. 145). Public works on a very large scale are acknowledged as the view of others, not ruled out, but said to risk much more serious restriction of the competitive sphere and growing dependence of all activity on government expenditure, so that in experimenting we must watch our step; not in his opinion the most promising way (p. 145). The editor identifies this as what would later be called Keynesian demand management (p. 153, note 4).
5. **Absolute security for a few special offices** such as judges, where complete independence is of paramount importance (p. 144).
6. **Voluntary labor service on military lines** may be the best form in which the state can provide the certainty of an opportunity for work and a minimum income for all; sections of a free society may be organized on the military principle for those who prefer it. Condition: it must be voluntary and not extended to those unwilling to give up their freedom (p. 149).
7. **Mitigation of transitional hardship**: Hayek's own footnote commends W. H. Hutt's Plan for Reconstruction (1943) for suggestions on how the hardships of those displaced by change might be mitigated within a liberal society (p. 153, note 5).
8. **Stated goals of policy**: adequate security against severe privation and reduction of the avoidable causes of misdirected effort and consequent disappointment will have to be among the main goals of policy (p. 152). Some security is essential to preserving freedom, because most men will bear freedom's risk only if it is not too great (p. 152).
9. **Moral concession**: undeserved losses offend our sense of justice; the hardships have no moral justification yet are inseparable from the competitive system (pp. 145-146). He does not claim market outcomes are deserved.

What he refuses: protection of particular incomes, standards of life, fair prices or professional incomes; stabilization of particular prices or wages; restrictions on entry (pp. 149-150).

## Arguments

- **A1. Guaranteed income entails direction of labor.**
  **Premises**: (p. 145) where people choose their own occupations, pay must correspond to usefulness to others, not subjective merit + (p. 146) usefulness changes through unforeseeable circumstances, so results cannot always match effort + (pp. 146-147) if losers were protected and gainers stripped of gains, pay would depend on an authority's view of what one ought to have done, and would no longer induce desirable moves + (p. 147) changes in the distribution of labor are constantly necessary in any society + (p. 147) if not brought about by pecuniary rewards and penalties they must be brought about by orders.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 146-147) security of an invariable income for all is possible only by abolishing free choice of employment.
  **Unstated premises**: that the only two allocation devices are price differentials and command (no mixed case such as partial income insurance that dampens but preserves differentials); that the guarantee is of the full former income, not a fraction.

- **A2. Incentives as information.**
  **Premises**: (p. 147) to choose intelligently, a person needs a readily intelligible yardstick of the social importance of occupations + (p. 147) only remuneration that tracks usefulness provides it.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 147) even with the best will in the world, free choice is impossible if rewards bear no relation to usefulness. This is the more important aspect of the incentive problem; motivation is the second (p. 147).
  **Unstated premise**: that market remuneration is a tolerably accurate measure of usefulness to society (argued for elsewhere in the book, assumed here).

- **A3. Discipline under planning.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 147-148) most men need external pressure to give their best + (p. 148) discipline in an enterprise depends on a surrounding unplanned area into which the dismissed worker vanishes (Coyle) + (p. 148) a planned manager bears neither risk nor gain, so he must be judged by rule, and his avoidable error is a crime against the community.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 148) the planned economy's ultimate sanction is the hangman; the manager's security is bought at the price of the safety of freedom and life.

- **A4. The dichotomy.**
  **Premises**: (p. 148) either both choice and risk rest with the individual or he is relieved of both + (p. 148) the army is the closest familiar approach to the second type.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 148) there is no third possibility; full security for all is the security of the barracks. A partially military society understates the unfreedom because a free sphere remains to exit to (p. 149).
  **My observation**: this conclusion sits awkwardly with the chapter's own first four pages, which describe a third thing: individual choice and risk with a floor under the risk. The dichotomy holds only for "full" security; Hayek's wording at p. 148 ("full economic security") preserves consistency, but the "no third possibility" phrasing is stronger than the argument licenses.

- **A5. The ratchet of privilege.**
  **Premises**: (p. 149) guaranteeing some a fixed part of a variable whole makes the remainder fluctuate proportionally more + (pp. 149-150) within a market, incomes can be secured only by restricting output or entry, which closes opportunities to outsiders + (p. 150) as protected trades multiply, those displaced have nowhere to go, so adjustment falls on employment and output rather than prices and wages + (pp. 150-151) the greater the gap between secure and insecure, the more security is prized.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 150-151) the more we try to provide full security by interfering with the market, the greater insecurity becomes, and the demand for security escalates until, as stated earlier, no price including liberty seems too high (p. 146).
  **Unstated premise**: that the process is cumulative and not self-limiting politically; that outsiders respond by demanding inclusion rather than abolition of privilege.

- **A6. The cultural mechanism (Germany).**
  **Premises**: (p. 151) restriction plus socialist disparagement of risk-taking and profit changes values before institutions + (p. 152) where rank comes almost only from salaried state service and independence is precarious and despised, few will prefer freedom.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 152) liberty becomes almost a mockery and people conclude it is not worth having without security; this is how the spirit of freedom was slowly smothered in Germany, and no people can be confident of withstanding the same process (p. 152).

## Evidence offered

- **Bare assertion / appeal to consensus**: that a minimum can be guaranteed without endangering freedom ("there can be no doubt") (p. 144). No costing or design is given.
- **Empirical generalization, unsourced**: that this security has long been achieved for much of England's population (p. 144).
- **Mechanism argument**: A1 and A2 on remuneration as signal (pp. 145-147). This is the chapter's strongest reasoning and is purely deductive.
- **Historical generalization, unsourced**: technological displacement of skilled workers over the last hundred years, sometimes hundreds of thousands at a time (p. 146). No instance named.
- **Quotation from an authority sympathetic to planning**: David C. Coyle, an American engineer with experience in government planning, on discipline requiring an unplanned reservoir (pp. 147-148; Hayek's cite at p. 153, note 6). Hayek cites p. 558 of the article; the editor adds that the first passage quoted is in fact on p. 559 (p. 153, note 6).
- **Aphorism from an ally**: Roepke's bailiff/hangman contrast (p. 148; p. 153, note 7). Rhetorical, not evidential.
- **Analogy**: the army and the barracks (p. 148); the single great factory (p. 149); the fixed share of a variable cake (p. 149). The cake analogy is an arithmetical truth; the barracks analogy carries the dichotomy.
- **Historical examples by gesture**: ancient Sparta and contemporary Germany as pictures of the fully organized society (p. 149). No detail.
- **Causal claim with confident language outrunning evidence**: "There can be little doubt" that the striving for security by restriction is largely responsible for the rise in unemployment in recent decades (p. 150). No data, no comparison of countries or trades, no engagement with monetary or demand explanations that he himself mentions at p. 145. Likewise the superlative that there has never been a worse and more cruel exploitation of one class by another than insiders over outsiders (p. 150).
- **Personal testimony**: the hopelessness of outsiders can be appreciated only by those who have experienced it (p. 150); "the daily experience of the university teacher" as evidence that values have shifted among the young (p. 151). Anecdotal.
- **Sociological characterization**: Germany as Beamtenstaat (p. 152), supported only by the claim that Germans themselves boasted of it. He explicitly denies that army influence explains it (pp. 151-152), which is itself asserted.
- **Quotation from an opponent**: Laski, that without economic security liberty is not worth having (p. 152; Hayek's note 9 at p. 153 gives the full sentence). Hayek claims this very argument did more than any other to induce Germans to sacrifice liberty (p. 152); no German source is produced for that.
- **Closing authority**: Franklin (p. 153; editor's source at note 10).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch03-ch04** for the claim that competition is the only method of coordination that dispenses with command, and for the distinction between planning for competition and planning against it; "planning in the good sense" at p. 145 reuses that distinction.
- **Depends on ch07** (economic control is control of ends) for why loss of occupational choice is loss of freedom generally, and on **ch08** (Who, Whom?) for the claim that distribution by authority must be arbitrary; p. 146 on an authority judging what one ought to have foreseen restates ch08's point about merit.
- **Links to ch06** (Rule of Law): the minimum is acceptable partly because it is general and not a privilege (p. 144); the objection to the second kind of security is that it is discriminatory. The open question about curtailing the liberties of dependants (p. 144) is in tension with ch06's equality before the law. Hayek leaves it unresolved.
- **Feeds ch10**: the manager judged by rule whose error is a crime (p. 148) anticipates the collectivist ethics of ch10; the shift of values from independence to security (p. 151) is the cultural soil for ch10's account of who rises.
- **Feeds ch12**: the Beamtenstaat passage (pp. 151-152) and "Germany has led" (p. 151) set up the claim that Nazism grew from long-standing German socialist and organizational traditions.
- **Feeds ch13-ch14**: restrictionism by organized capital and labor (pp. 149-150), and the closing demand for material sacrifice (p. 153).
- **Internal tension** (mine): the chapter concedes a wide range of state action (pp. 144-145) and then declares there is no third possibility between commercial and military society (p. 148). Read carefully the two are compatible, since the floor sits outside the market, but the rhetoric of the dichotomy is what later readers remembered, and the concessions are what defenders cite.
- **Internal tension** (mine): he treats large-scale unemployment as partly cyclical and treatable by monetary policy (p. 145) and then as largely a product of restrictionism (p. 150), without relating the two explanations.
- **Shift in "privilege"**: here it means non-universal, state-granted protection (pp. 144, 146); check against ch06 and ch08 where privilege is tied to legal discrimination and property.

## Open questions

- Where exactly is the line between the minimum (acceptable) and the security of a standard of life (unacceptable)? A minimum indexed to rising wealth drifts toward the second. Hayek gives a criterion (outside the market, universal, sufficient for health and work capacity) but no level (p. 144).
- Does unemployment insurance, old-age pension or health provision as actually built after 1945 satisfy his insurable-risk condition (pp. 144-145)? Synthesis should test whether the postwar welfare state falsifies the ratchet (A5) or falls under the accepted first kind.
- What does he intend by suggesting that those who rely on the community may not keep all the same liberties indefinitely (p. 144)? This is the least liberal sentence in the chapter and is not developed.
- Is the unemployment claim at p. 150 defensible on evidence, and how does it relate to his business-cycle views?
- Is the commercial/military dichotomy (p. 148) the same either/or as the competition/central-direction dichotomy of ch03, and does it share that argument's weakness regarding mixed systems?
- How much weight does the Germany-as-Beamtenstaat characterization (p. 152) carry in the book's overall causal story, and is it compatible with ch12's emphasis on ideas rather than social structure?
- The Laski quotation: Laski says liberty without security is not worth having; Hayek himself says some security is essential if freedom is to be preserved (p. 152). How far apart are they once Hayek's concessions are counted?
