# ch02: The Great Utopia (pp. 73–80)

Scope note: the chapter text runs from the heading on p. 73 to p. 78; footnotes run pp. 78-80. Unbracketed footnotes are Hayek's; bracketed ones are the editor's (Caldwell).

## Question this chapter answers

How did socialism, which began as an openly authoritarian reaction against liberalism, come to be accepted as the heir of the liberal tradition and the road to freedom, and is 'democratic socialism' achievable?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| freedom (liberal sense) | p. 74 | Freedom from coercion, from the arbitrary power of other men; release from ties that leave the individual no choice but obedience to a superior. A relation between persons. |
| 'new freedom' / 'economic freedom' (socialist sense) | pp. 74-75 | Freedom from necessity: release from the compulsion of circumstances that limit everyone's range of choice. Hayek says this is merely another name for power or wealth, and in practice another name for the old demand for equal distribution of wealth. |
| economic freedom (shift) | pp. 74-75 | Here the phrase belongs to the socialists and means freedom from want. In ch. 1 the near-identical phrase 'freedom in economic affairs' (p. 65) was Hayek's own and meant freedom of economic activity. Hayek explicitly distinguishes freedom from coercion from freedom from necessity, explaining that socialists and liberals use the word freedom in different senses. |
| socialism | p. 74 | Historically: a doctrine that began as a reaction against the liberalism of the French Revolution, calling for deliberate reorganisation of society on hierarchical lines under a coercive 'spiritual power'. Still not defined analytically see ch. 3 (p. 80). |
| democratic socialism | p. 74, p. 78 | Socialism allied, since before 1848, with the forces of freedom; 'the great utopia of the last few generations', held to be unachievable. |
| liberalism / 'liberal of the old type' | p. 74, p. 77 | The doctrine socialism displaced among progressives; the one who 'really believes in individual freedom' and is the real enemy of both communist and Nazi. |
| collectivism | p. 74 | Used once as the general name for what the liberal thinkers warned against; not defined. |
| democracy | p. 74 | Via Tocqueville: 'an essentially individualist institution' in irreconcilable conflict with socialism. |
| planners / planning boards | p. 74 | Saint-Simon is 'the first of modern planners'; the word is used without definition. |

## Leading propositions

- Socialism displaced liberalism among progressives not because the liberal warnings were forgotten but because people were persuaded of the opposite of what the liberals predicted (p. 74).
- Socialism in its beginnings was frankly authoritarian; its French founders held that their ideas needed a strong dictatorial government and regarded freedom of thought as the root-evil of nineteenth-century society (p. 74).
- Only under the democratic currents before 1848 did socialism ally itself with freedom (p. 74).
- Tocqueville saw that democracy, an individualist institution, is in irreconcilable conflict with socialism (p. 74).
- To allay suspicion and harness the craving for freedom, socialism promised a 'new freedom', economic freedom, without which political freedom was said to be not worth having (p. 74).
- The promise works by a change in the meaning of the word: from freedom from coercion by other men to freedom from necessity (pp. 74-75).
- Freedom in the new sense is another name for power or wealth; the demand for it is the old demand for equal distribution of wealth under a new name (p. 75).
- The shared word let socialists claim the liberals' name; few noticed the difference in sense or asked whether the two freedoms could be combined (p. 75).
- The socialist belief that socialism brings freedom is genuine and sincere (p. 75).
- The promise of more freedom lured liberals along the socialist road and blinded them to the conflict between the basic principles of socialism and liberalism (p. 75).
- Observers who expected otherwise have been struck by the similarity of conditions under fascism and communism, and have begun to ask whether both tyrannies come from the same tendencies (p. 75).
- Many Nazi and Fascist leaders, from Mussolini down, began as socialists; conversion between communist and Nazi was easy and well known in Germany (pp. 76-77).
- Communists and Nazis fought each other most because they competed for the same type of mind; for both the real enemy is the old-type liberal, while each sees the other, and the socialist, as a potential recruit (p. 77).
- Liberalism was already dead in Germany when Hitler came to power, and socialism had killed it (p. 77).
- Most socialists in the democracies still believe profoundly in the liberal ideal of freedom and would recoil if convinced their programme would destroy it (pp. 77-78).
- Democratic socialism is not only unachievable; striving for it produces something so different that few who wish it would accept the consequences (p. 78).
- The reasons why the development is not accidental are not given in this chapter; they need a full examination of the transformation (p. 78).

## Arguments

- **The equivocation argument (how socialism captured the word freedom).**
  **Premises**: (p. 74) for the apostles of political freedom the word meant freedom from coercion by other men; (p. 75) the socialist 'new freedom' means freedom from necessity, from the compulsion of circumstances; (p. 75) that is another name for power or wealth; (p. 75) what was actually promised was the disappearance of disparities in people's range of choice; (unstated) limits imposed by circumstances, including one's lack of means in a society of property, are not themselves coercion by other men; (unstated) a concept that reduces to power or wealth is not a kind of freedom at all.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 75) the demand for the new freedom is the old demand for equal distribution of wealth under another name; the two groups used one word for two different things.

- **The compatibility question (raised, not answered).**
  **Premises**: (p. 75) the two freedoms are distinct; (p. 75) few asked whether they could be combined; (unstated) securing freedom from necessity for all by equalising distribution requires the coercive direction that removes freedom from coercion.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 75) what was promised as the Road to Freedom may be the High Road to Servitude. The unstated premise is the whole burden of chs. 5-9; here the conclusion is put conditionally ('if it should prove').

- **Argument from origins.**
  **Premises**: (p. 74) the founders of socialism were openly authoritarian and hostile to freedom of thought; (p. 74) the alliance with democracy dates only from the 1840s; (unstated) a doctrine's original character reveals its real tendency better than its later professions.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 74) it is 'extraordinary' that the same socialism won acceptance under the flag of liberty; the authoritarian element is native, not accidental. My observation: as an inference this is genetic; it shows what early socialists wanted, not what later democratic socialists must produce.

- **Argument from disillusioned witnesses.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 75-76) Eastman, Chamberlin, Voigt, Lippmann and Drucker, several of them former sympathisers, conclude that socialism or Marxism leads to dictatorship or is akin to fascism; (p. 76) more such statements could be cited from citizens of the totalitarian countries; (unstated) the convergence of independent observers who expected the opposite is good evidence of a real causal link.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 77) the connection between socialism and fascism has become 'increasingly obvious' to those who watched the transition at close quarters.

- **Argument from kinship of persons and enemies.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 76-77) leaders and rank and file moved easily between socialism or communism and fascism or naziism; (p. 77) both camps treat the liberal as the one irreconcilable enemy; (p. 77) Heimann, a socialist, grants that Hitler claimed democracy, socialism and Christianity but never liberalism; (unstated) movements that share recruits and a common enemy share a common essence.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 77) communism and fascism are closely related; their mutual hatred is that of heretics.

- **Chapter conclusion as a promissory note.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 77-78) socialists in the democracies still sincerely hold the liberal ideal; (p. 78) contradictions in terms such as 'individualist socialism' are still seriously discussed.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 78) a full examination is needed to show the development is not accidental; the thesis that democratic socialism is unachievable is stated here but explicitly not yet demonstrated.

## Evidence offered

- Epigraph from Hölderlin on the state made a hell by the attempt to make it heaven (pp. 73-74). Kind: motto; editor supplies source and German text (p. 78).
- Saint-Simon's prediction that those who disobey his planning boards would be 'treated as cattle' (p. 74). Kind: quotation from an opponent, uncited by Hayek. **Editor's correction**: the translated source reads that such a man will be 'regarded and treated by others as an animal', and the sentence was apparently deleted by disciples from some later versions (p. 78). Hayek's wording is a loose rendering.
- 'Terminate the revolution' and coercive 'spiritual power' (p. 74). Kind: quoted phrases, unattributed and uncited.
- Tocqueville's 1848 speech contrasting democracy and socialism (p. 74). Kind: quotation from an authority, cited by Hayek (p. 78). The editor prints the French original (p. 78). My observation from that original: Hayek's English gives 'individual freedom' for indépendance individuelle and drops 'un instrument' from the series agent, instrument, chiffre; the sense is preserved but the translation is not exact. Context worth keeping: the speech was on the right to work.
- 'Despotism of physical want', 'restraints of the economic system', 'not worth having' (pp. 74-75). Kind: phrases in quotation marks attributed to socialists generally; no source given.
- Paul Janet on the Saint-Simonian theory of liberty, and John Dewey's 'liberty is the effective power to do specific things' in Hayek's note 4 (pp. 78-79). Kind: quotation from an opponent; the one piece of evidence that a named socialist or left thinker actually defines freedom as power.
- Max Eastman on Stalinism as worse than fascism and as socialism's unforeseen political accompaniment (pp. 75-76). Kind: testimony of a disillusioned sympathiser. **Editor's correction**: Hayek listed all three passages as p. 82; they are on pp. 82, 82 and 154 (p. 79). Editor adds that Eastman later condensed this book for Reader's Digest (p. 79).
- W. H. Chamberlin on socialism as the road to dictatorship (p. 76). Kind: testimony; cited (p. 79). Note Chamberlin's own hedge, 'in the beginning at least' (p. 76).
- F. A. Voigt: Marxism 'is' Fascism and National Socialism (p. 76). Kind: testimony. **Editor's correction**: the editor could not locate the quoted passage at the page Hayek cites, and offers a similar passage from p. 35 of Voigt's book (p. 79). The quotation is therefore unverified.
- Walter Lippmann on the nemesis of the planned society (p. 76). Kind: testimony; cited (p. 79).
- Peter Drucker on Russia and Germany travelling the same road (p. 76). Kind: testimony. **Editor's correction**: Hayek gave p. 230; the passage is on pp. 245-246 (p. 79). Drucker's passage itself denies that communism and fascism are essentially the same and calls fascism the stage after communism has proved an illusion (p. 76), which is a different thesis from Voigt's identity claim; Hayek says Drucker puts it 'perhaps more justly' (p. 76).
- Leaders who began as socialists: Mussolini, Laval, Quisling (pp. 76-77); Michels cited for the Italian cases in Hayek's note 10 (p. 80). Kind: historical examples.
- Ease of conversion between communist and Nazi, 'generally known in Germany'; students returning unsure which they were (p. 77). Kind: anecdote and appeal to common knowledge.
- Eduard Heimann, a religious socialist, on liberalism as the doctrine most hated by Hitler (p. 77). Kind: admission by an opponent; cited (p. 80). Heimann calls the grain of truth in Hitler's socialist claim 'infinitesimal' (p. 77).
- Hitler's February 1941 statement that National Socialism and Marxism are basically the same in Hayek's note 12 (p. 80). Kind: quotation from the enemy; Hayek hedges with 'whatever may have been his reasons' (p. 80).
- Where language outruns evidence: 'There can be no doubt' (p. 75), 'Unquestionably' (p. 75), 'Everyone who has watched' (p. 76), 'it was socialism that had killed it' (p. 77) and 'not only unachievable' (p. 78) are all asserted on testimony and anecdote. The chapter's evidence is almost entirely quotation: it shows that some informed people believed in the connection, not that the connection holds. Hayek concedes as much at p. 78. Two of the five witness quotations carry wrong page references and one cannot be found.

Concessions and qualifications recorded:
- Circumstances limit the range of choice 'for some very much more than for others' (p. 75): the inequality the socialists point to is real.
- The socialist belief in freedom is 'genuine and sincere' (p. 75); most socialists 'believe profoundly in the liberal ideal of freedom' and would recoil from its destruction (pp. 77-78).
- Socialist promises of greater wealth were 'often' irresponsible, but Hayek grants that the core demand was equality of range of choice, not abundance (p. 75).
- Communists and Nazis or Fascists did clash with each other more than with other parties before 1933 and 1922 (p. 77).
- The conclusions of this chapter 'will only confirm the apprehensions which others have already expressed'; the reasons are deferred (p. 78).
- No state activity is discussed in this chapter.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Definition of freedom: ch. 1 declined to define it and offered 'tolerance' (p. 65); here it becomes freedom from coercion by other men (p. 74). This negative definition is the one the rest of the book relies on.
- The phrase 'economic freedom' changes owner between chapters: Hayek's own in ch. 1, meaning freedom of economic activity as a condition of political freedom (p. 65); the socialists' here, meaning freedom from want (p. 74). Synthesis should not let the two be run together; ch. 7 returns to the point.
- My observation: Hayek grants that circumstances limit choice unequally (p. 75) but treats that as outside freedom by definition. The argument that the 'new freedom' is merely power or wealth is definitional, not demonstrated. It also sits uneasily with ch. 1, where the liberal era is praised for giving the workingman 'personal independence' through material progress (p. 67), which treats wealth as bearing on freedom.
- The socialism-to-fascism thesis continues the Introduction (p. 58) (p. 61) and anticipates ch. 12. Drucker's stage theory, that fascism follows the failure of communism (p. 76), and Voigt's identity claim (p. 76) are not the same thesis as Hayek's own, naziism as outcome of socialist trends (p. 58).
- The claim that both extremes recruit from 'the same type of mind' (p. 77) anticipates ch. 10.
- 'Democratic socialism is unachievable' (p. 78) is the thesis ch. 5 (Planning and Democracy) must make good.
- The good faith of socialists (p. 75) (pp. 77-78) matches the 'people of good will' of the Introduction (p. 58) and is essential to the book's unintended-consequences structure.
- Ch. 3 opens by separating socialist ends from socialist means (p. 80); this chapter treats 'socialism' as a single thing throughout, so its historical claims apply strictly only to socialism as method.

## Open questions

- Is the negative definition of freedom (p. 74) argued for anywhere in the book, or only asserted against the 'confusion' with power? The Dewey footnote (p. 79) is the only engagement with a rival view.
- Can dependence on an employer or landlord under threat of destitution count as 'arbitrary power of other men' on Hayek's own definition (p. 74)? If so the socialist complaint is not wholly an equivocation. Check chs. 7-9.
- Does the later argument show that democratic socialism is unachievable (p. 78) or only that comprehensive central planning is incompatible with democracy? The two differ if socialist ends can be sought by other means, which ch. 3 allows (pp. 80-81).
- How representative is the witness list? All five are critics by the time they write. Does Hayek ever engage a socialist who considered the freedom problem and answered it?
- The historical claim that liberalism was dead in Germany by 1933 and 'socialism had killed it' (p. 77): what is the evidence in ch. 12, and what of nationalism, conservatism and the war?
- The editor's corrections (Saint-Simon wording, Eastman and Drucker pages, the untraceable Voigt passage) suggest Hayek quoted from memory or notes; synthesis should treat his quotations of opponents as paraphrase unless the editor confirms them.
