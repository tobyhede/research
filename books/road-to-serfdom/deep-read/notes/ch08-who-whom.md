# ch08: Who, Whom? (pp. 130–143)

## Question this chapter answers

If the state plans the economy for the sake of distributive justice, by what principle will it decide who gets what, and who will do the deciding? Hayek's answer: no agreed principle exists short of absolute equality, which nobody wants; so the distribution will be decided by whoever holds the coercive power, every economic question becomes a political one, and the struggle for that power among groups with rival ideas of their due station is what produced fascism and National Socialism out of socialism.

## Leading propositions

- That competition is blind, so that nobody can foretell who gains or loses, is a merit, parallel to the generality of legal rules (pp. 130-131).
- The real choice is not between desert and chance, but between distribution by the will of a few and distribution depending partly on ability and enterprise and partly on unforeseeable circumstances (p. 131).
- Chances under free enterprise are unequal because of property and inheritance; there is a strong case for reducing that inequality within limits (p. 131).
- The poor in a competitive society are freer than materially better-off people in a different kind of society; an unskilled worker in England has more freedom than a small entrepreneur in Germany or an engineer in Russia (p. 131).
- Transferring all means of production to the state makes the state's action decide all incomes (p. 132).
- The state's power in that case is newly created, not transferred; divided property means nobody has exclusive power over particular people (p. 132).
- Private property is the most important guaranty of freedom for owners and non-owners alike (p. 132).
- A world where the wealthy are powerful is better than one where only the already powerful can acquire wealth (p. 132).
- Planners would prefer to plan production only and leave distribution to impersonal forces under general rules of fairness, but planning cannot be stopped there (p. 133).
- Inequality is more readily borne, and wounds dignity less, when it results from impersonal forces than when it is due to design (p. 134).
- A government that plans for justice cannot refuse responsibility for anyone's fate; all effort is then directed at influencing the authority (p. 134).
- Under total planning every economic or social question becomes political, and the only power worth having is a share in the directing power (pp. 134-135).
- All government affects who gets what; the difference between free and totalitarian systems is whether measures aim at particular effects and how far government activity extends (p. 135).
- Only absolute equality would give the planner a definite rule; people do not want it; "greater equality" gives no guidance (p. 136).
- Our notions of just price and fair wage derive from the competitive regime and would vanish with it (pp. 136-137).
- In deciding what is produced the planner decides the relative importance of groups and persons, and must discriminate between individuals, whether by income or by category (pp. 137-138).
- Successful planning requires a common view on essential values, which is why material restriction touches spiritual freedom (p. 138).
- That common view cannot come from education or reason; it requires acceptance of a creed, and socialists first built the instruments of indoctrination later used by Nazis and Fascists (pp. 138-139).
- As incomes come to depend on the state, people can improve their position only through organised groups that control the state; the poorest need not win that contest (p. 139).
- Fascism and National Socialism were a revolt of a new underprivileged lower middle class against the labour aristocracy, a conflict "between rival socialist factions" agreed on state assignment of station and divided on who gets which place (pp. 140-141).
- The new movements succeeded because they had no illusions about democratic or rational solutions and offered a Weltanschauung justifying the privileges they promised (pp. 141-142).

## Arguments

**A. Impersonality as the mark of a free distribution (pp. 130-132)**

- **Premises**: (p. 131) No system gives everyone what they deserve by an absolute standard. (p. 131) The alternatives are distribution by a few persons' will or by ability plus unforeseeable circumstance. (p. 131) In the second, nobody can prevent a person from trying, and success does not depend on "the favors of the mighty". (pp. 131-132) Freedom is the absence of absolute impediments and of confinement by force.
- **Unstated premises**: that these two alternatives are exhaustive, with no mixed system in which general rules shift the distribution without anyone's "will" deciding individual shares; that outcomes not intended by any person raise no question of justice; that freedom is to be measured by absence of coercion and not by the range of options actually available.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 131) The poor in a competitive society are "much more free" than better-off people elsewhere; (p. 131) competition's blindness is a commendation.

**B. Divided property as the guarantee of freedom (p. 132)**

- **Premises**: While property is divided among many independent owners, none has exclusive power over any person's income and position; one is tied to an owner only by his offering better terms. If all means of production are in one hand, whoever controls them has complete power. That power does not exist under competition, so it is created, not transferred.
- **Unstated premises**: that owners do in fact act independently and compete for labour (no monopsony, no collusion, enough employers); that the state as owner acts as a single will; that the worker's alternative offers are adequate for the "tie" to be a free one.
- **Conclusion**: Private property protects those without property; the minority member with no property is freer where others of his community own and can employ him.

**C. Why planning cannot stop short of deciding individual incomes (pp. 132-134, 137-138)**

- **Premises**: (p. 132) State ownership of the means of production makes state action decide all incomes. (p. 133) Planners would like to leave individual distribution to impersonal forces within general rules of equity. (p. 133) Interdependence of economic phenomena forces controls to become comprehensive once the market is impeded "beyond a certain degree" (referred back to an earlier chapter). (p. 134) Once position is known to depend on authority, government cannot refuse responsibility for anybody's fate. (p. 137) Fixing quantities fixes the "fair" wage; (p. 138) income differences within trades are as large as between classes, so categories do not remove the need to discriminate between individuals.
- **Unstated premises**: that the "certain degree" has been or will be passed by the programmes he has in mind; that public attribution of responsibility to government is irreversible and total once it begins.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 137-138) The planner "will necessarily exercise direct control over the conditions of the different people".

**D. The psychological argument: designed inequality is harder to bear (pp. 133-134)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 133-134) Inequalities, disappointments and misfortune exist in any society. (p. 134) Being refused by a particular firm is no slight; being judged by authority as to "whether he is of use for anything" is. (p. 134) People submit to suffering that may hit anyone, not to suffering decided by authority.
- **Unstated premise**: that people in a market society actually perceive their position as nobody's doing; and that greater bearability is a reason in favour of a system and not only a fact about acquiescence.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 134) "Dissatisfaction of everybody with his lot will inevitably grow" under conscious direction; all effort turns to influencing authority; politics absorbs everything (pp. 134-135).

**E. No usable principle of distributive justice (pp. 135-138)**

- **Premises**: (p. 135) A government directing the economy must realise some ideal of distributive justice. (p. 136) Only absolute equality yields a determinate answer. (p. 136) People do not regard mechanical equality as desirable; no socialist movement aiming at it won substantial support. (p. 136) "Greater equality" is negative and settles no particular case. (pp. 136-137) "Just price" and "fair wage" mean the customary or the competitive level, and so presuppose competition. (p. 137) The "full produce of labor" claim is abandoned and was based on error. (p. 138) Mill: a handful of men weighing everyone at their pleasure would not be borne.
- **Unstated premises**: that a principle must be fully determinate for every case to be usable at all (partial orderings, floors, ranges and procedures are not considered); that moral standards cannot develop under new institutions, though he asserts they "necessarily disappear" (p. 137) without showing why new customary expectations would not form.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 136, 138) The planner must decide "in every particular instance between the merits of particular individuals or groups" with no help from any formula, so the decision is the will of those in power.

**F. From socialism to fascism: the group-conflict mechanism (pp. 138-142)**

- **Premises**: (p. 138) Planning needs a common creed; socialists built the organisational means for instilling one. (p. 139) As socialist methods extend, each person sees that income depends on the state and on belonging to a group able to control it. (p. 140) Raising one group's relative position cannot win the support of all; those whose position worsens form rival movements. (p. 140) Socialist theory ignored the new middle class of clerks, teachers, small officials. (p. 140) These were poorer than organised workers and envied them. (p. 141) Their training made them expect directing positions. (pp. 141-142) The new movements accepted state assignment of station, dropped democratic and egalitarian illusions, and promised privileges backed by a Weltanschauung.
- **Unstated premises**: that the economic position of the lower middle class was worsened *by socialist policy* and not by inflation, depression or war; that agreement on state control of the economy is the defining feature that makes the movements "socialist"; that the Austrian and German pattern generalises.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 140) The conflict between fascist and older socialist parties was "the kind of conflict which is bound to arise between rival socialist factions".

**Position versus argument.** He holds that private property should be kept. Reason stated: dispersal of control over means, hence no complete power over anyone (p. 132). Not offered as reasons here: natural right, desert, or efficiency. He holds that market distribution should not be replaced by designed distribution. Reason stated: it is impersonal and leaves no one's will supreme (p. 131), not that it is just; he denies it tracks merit (p. 131).

## Evidence offered

- **Mechanism and conceptual argument**: the main support for A-E (pp. 130-138).
- **Comparative assertion without data**: the English unskilled worker versus the German small entrepreneur and the Russian engineer (p. 131). Supported only by a list of freedoms (changing job, residence, views, leisure).
- **Rhetorical questions in place of evidence**: "Who can seriously doubt", "who will deny" on the minority member, the millionaire versus the fonctionnaire, and the wealthy-powerful world (p. 132).
- **Testimony against interest**: Max Eastman, "as prominent an old communist", on private property and freedom, and on Marx's own view that private capitalism was a precondition of democratic freedoms (pp. 132-133).
- **Data, second-hand**: Hayek's note 2: Eastman, from official Russian sources, on a highest-to-lowest salary ratio of about 50 to 1 in Russia, as in the United States; Trotsky via Burnham, that the upper 11 or 12 per cent of the Soviet population received about 50 per cent of national income against 35 per cent for the top 10 per cent in the United States (p. 142). Hayek hedges: "What little information we have". Used to show that abolishing property income need not reduce inequality.
- **Quotation, authority**: Mill on a handful of human beings weighing everybody (p. 138); Disraeli's "no avenue to wealth and honor" (p. 134); Acton epigraph (p. 130).
- **Quotation from opponents or observers**: the "who, whom?" phrase, attributed with "I believe it was Lenin himself" (p. 135), sourced only to Muggeridge and Feiler (Hayek's note 5, p. 143); Lasswell's "who gets what, when, and how" (p. 135); the Austrian socialist Georg Wieser on organisations "for every field of activities" (p. 139); Hendrik de Man on anti-capitalist resentment turning against the socialist movement (Hayek's note 10, p. 143).
- **Historical example / sociological claim**: socialist parties as originators of youth organisations, party sport, greetings, cells; Balilla, Hitlerjugend, Dopolavoro, Kraft durch Freude as "little more than imitations" (p. 139). Supported by the one Wieser quotation and Hayek's own knowledge of Austria; English "book clubs" offered as a parallel (Hayek's note 9, p. 143).
- **Historical claim on the social base of fascism**: early Nazi rank and file poorer in money income than the average trade unionist; lower-middle-class envy as the largest single economic factor (p. 140). Introduced with "There can be little doubt" and "Nor can there be much doubt"; no figures or sources.
- **Illustration**: income spread among doctors, architects, plumbers, grocers "as great as those between the propertied and the propertyless classes" (pp. 137-138). Asserted, no data.
- **Language outrunning evidence**: "in every real sense" (p. 131); "Dissatisfaction of everybody with his lot will inevitably grow" (p. 134); "must indeed inevitably become the central political problem" (p. 134); "would necessarily disappear soon after the disappearance of competition" (p. 137); "No socialist movement which aimed at complete equality has ever gained substantial support" (p. 136); "It was not the Fascists but the socialists" repeated three times (p. 139).

- Note 3: Hayek cited Eastman as "Reader's Digest, July, 1941, p. 39"; correct is June 1941, p. 47; Hayek also dropped Eastman's hyphens in "free-and-equalness" (p. 142).

## Concessions and qualifications

- Competition and justice "may have little else in common" beyond impartiality (p. 130).
- In competition "chance and good luck are often as important as skill and foresight" (p. 131); rewards do not follow merit (p. 131).
- "In a system of free enterprise chances are not equal" because of private property and inheritance (p. 131). Inheritance is necessary to free enterprise "perhaps not with the same necessity" as property (p. 131).
- **Accepted aim of policy**: "There is, indeed, a strong case for reducing this inequality of opportunity as far as congenital differences permit and as it is possible to do so without destroying the impersonal character of the process" (p. 131). No measures are named.
- The opportunities of the poor "are much more restricted than those open to the rich"; the probability of a poor man reaching great wealth is "much smaller" (p. 131).
- The price the poor worker pays for following his inclinations may be high "and to many appear too high" (pp. 131-132).
- "It is true that in periods of prolonged mass unemployment the effect on many may be very similar" to designed inequality; "there are other and better methods to prevent that scourge than central direction" (p. 134). **State action against mass unemployment is thus accepted in principle**, unspecified here.
- "It may be bad to be just a cog in an impersonal machine" (p. 134).
- The ideal of justice of most socialists would be satisfied by abolishing property income alone; he calls this "true" (p. 132).
- "It is impossible to direct industry without exercising some influence on distribution" and planners would prefer general rules of equity (p. 133): he grants the planners' good intentions and reluctance.
- Lasswell's "who gets what, when, and how" is "in a way" "not untrue": "all government affects the relative position of different people" and in so far as government does anything it affects distribution (p. 135). This concedes that the liberal state is not distributively neutral in effect.
- The word "greater equality" names a real sentiment; he calls the distinction "not a quibble about words" (p. 136).
- Whether absolute equality would be practicable (incentives) is set aside (p. 136).
- "There is a great deal of truth" in the description of fascism as middle-class socialism (p. 140); the lower middle class saw organised labour as exploiters "not without some justification" (p. 141).
- Labour socialism "had grown in a democratic and liberal world" and took over "many of the ideals of liberalism" (p. 141).
- The Soviet income evidence is admitted to be "little information" (p. 142).
- The Lenin attribution is hedged: "I believe it was Lenin himself" (p. 135).

## Quotes

- **Paraphrase:** Hayek contrasts outcomes assigned by a few decision-makers with outcomes shaped by individual initiative and unpredictable circumstances. Neither system guarantees that everyone receives a morally deserved share. (p. 131)
- "There is, indeed, a strong case for reducing this inequality of opportunity as far as congenital differences permit and as it is possible to do so without destroying the impersonal character of the process by which everybody has to take his chance and no person’s view about what is right and desirable overrules that of others." (p. 131)
- "What our generation has forgotten is that the system of private property is the most important guaranty of freedom, not only for those who own property, but scarcely less for those who do not." (p. 132)
- "And who will deny that a world in which the wealthy are powerful is still a better world than one in which only the already powerful can acquire wealth?" (p. 132)
- "Inequality is undoubtedly more readily borne, and affects the dignity of the person much less, if it is determined by impersonal forces than when it is due to design." (p. 134)
- "As the coercive power of the state will alone decide who is to have what, the only power worth having will be a share in the exercise of this directing power." (p. 134)
- "Second, it is the extent of the activities of the government which decides whether everything that any person gets any time depends on the government, or whether its influence is confined to whether some people will get some things in some way at some time. Here lies the whole difference between a free and a totalitarian system." (p. 135)
- "There was no difference between them about the question of its being the will of the state which should assign to each person his proper place in society." (p. 140)

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch06**: the opening analogy between blind competition and legal rules whose beneficiaries cannot be predicted (pp. 130-131) applies ch06's criterion of formal rules (ch06, p. 110) to market outcomes; "This point we have already discussed" (p. 135) refers to it. The concession that the Rule of Law produces undesigned inequality (ch06, p. 113) is here turned into a positive claim that undesigned inequality is easier to bear (p. 134).
- **Depends on ch07**: takes up ch07's admission that distribution is the only serious argument for planning (ch07, p. 128) and the monopolist argument (ch07, p. 124), restated as a single owner having "complete power over us" (p. 132).
- **Depends on ch04 or ch03**: "We have already seen" that interdependence makes it hard to stop planning where we wish (p. 133) is a back-reference; the claim is not re-argued here.
- **Depends on ch05**: "greater equality" is as empty as "common good" (p. 136; ch05, p. 97); fascists "had no illusions about the possibility of a democratic solution of problems which require more agreement among people than can reasonably be expected" (p. 141) is ch05's argument seen as historical fact.
- **Feeds ch09**: "other and better methods" against mass unemployment (p. 134) and the remark that the young "flocked" to salaried security (p. 141) lead into Security and Freedom.
- **Feeds ch10-ch12**: the need for a creed and the socialist origins of totalitarian party technique (pp. 138-139) are developed in ch10, ch11 and above all ch12 (socialist roots of Naziism). The class analysis of fascism here (pp. 139-142) should be compared with ch12's intellectual-history account; one is sociological, the other doctrinal.
- **Shift in "socialism" (my observation)**: the book's working definition, as I understand ch03 (not read in this pass; check against the ch03 note), ties socialism to ends (social justice, equality) pursued by planning. Anyone who wants "a deliberate sharing-out of wealth according to their ideas of justice" (p. 140) counts as socialist, including movements promising hierarchy and privilege (p. 142). The identification of fascism with socialism depends on defining socialism by method alone.
- **Shift in "power" (my observation)**: here power is strictly power over particular persons' income and position (p. 132), and he denies that private wealth confers much of it. ch07 called the economic motive a desire for power to achieve unspecified ends (ch07, p. 121). The claim that socialisation "creates" power instead of transferring it holds only on the first sense.
- **Tension (my observation)**: "freedom" for the poor is defined by absence of coercion and of absolute impediments (pp. 131-132), consistent with ch02. But the argument for money in ch07 (ch07, p. 122) praised it for widening the "range of choice", a range-of-options notion of freedom. He uses the wider notion when it favours the market and the narrower when the poor's restricted options are in question.
- **Tension (my observation)**: the "strong case" for reducing inequality of opportunity (p. 131) is a distributive aim. By ch06, p. 113, policy aiming at substantive equality destroys the Rule of Law. The qualifier "without destroying the impersonal character of the process" is how he reconciles them, but no example is given of a measure that passes.
- **Tension (my observation)**: he concedes that all government affects "who gets what" (p. 135) and that the legal framework of property and inheritance creates unequal chances (p. 131). The claim that market outcomes are "nobody's conscious choice" (p. 133) then holds only at the level of individual shares, not at the level of the rules, which are chosen.
- **Tension (my observation)**: the bearability argument (p. 134) is about how inequality is felt. It could count equally in favour of concealing responsibility. It also sits beside the claim that in mass unemployment the effect is "very similar" (p. 134), which admits the perception of impersonality can fail.

## Open questions

- Is the dichotomy "will of a few persons" versus "impersonal process" exhaustive (p. 131)? Tax-and-transfer systems change the distribution by general rule without anyone assigning individual shares. Does anything in chs. 6-9 rule them out, or does his own p. 131 concession admit them?
- Which measures does he have in mind for reducing inequality of opportunity (p. 131) and for preventing mass unemployment (p. 134)? Collect with ch09 and with the inheritance aside (p. 131), which leaves room for limits on inheritance.
- Does the claim that moral standards of fairness "would necessarily disappear" with competition (p. 137) rest on anything beyond the two definitions of "just price"? Wartime rationing and wage norms are obvious test cases.
- The class account of fascism (pp. 139-141): how well does it fit the evidence on Nazi support, and does it treat inflation and depression as products of "socialist policy"? Compare ch12 and the editor's introduction.
- "no avenue to wealth and honor would exist save through the government" (p. 134): does the divided-property argument presuppose competitive labour markets? What does Hayek say about private monopoly and company towns (ch04, ch13)?
- If private property protects non-owners only because owners are many and independent (p. 132), does concentration of ownership weaken the argument by his own standard? Test against his treatment of monopoly.
- The comparison of the English unskilled worker with the Russian engineer (p. 131) mixes the effects of planning with the effects of dictatorship. Can the book separate them, given ch05's claim that the first leads to the second?
- Quotation accuracy: Disraeli (p. 134) is inexact and several citations in this chapter were wrong in the original (pp. 142-143). Later use of these quotations should cite the editor's corrections.
