# ch12: The Socialist Roots of Naziism (pp. 178–190)

## Question this chapter answers

Where did the doctrines of National Socialism come from, and what brought a minority creed to majority support in Germany? Hayek's answer: they are the culmination of a long collectivist intellectual tradition, and they were carried to power not by a capitalist reaction against socialism but by support from the socialist camp, through a fusion of socialists of the Left and Right against liberalism (pp. 178-179). The chapter is intellectual history by exhibit: a sequence of writers, mostly ex-Marxists, quoted at length.

Epigraph: Moeller van den Bruck, "All antiliberal forces are combining against everything that is liberal." (p. 178); editor's source at p. 186, note 1.

## Leading propositions

- It is a mistake to treat National Socialism as an irrational movement without intellectual background; were it so it would be less dangerous (p. 178).
- Its doctrines culminate a long evolution of thought with influence far beyond Germany; the system has ruthless consistency once its premises are accepted (p. 178).
- German thinkers led but were not alone: Carlyle, H. S. Chamberlain, Comte and Sorel belong to the same development (p. 178).
- Qualification: it is easy to exaggerate the importance of these ideas in Germany before 1914; they were one strand among a people perhaps more varied in views than any other, held by a small minority and despised by most Germans (p. 178).
- What brought them to majority support was not merely defeat, suffering and nationalism, still less a capitalist reaction against socialism; the support came from the socialist camp, and through the absence of a strong bourgeoisie (p. 178).
- The doctrines guiding Germany's ruling elements opposed not the socialism in Marxism but its liberal elements, internationalism and democracy; as these proved obstacles to socialism, Left socialists approached the Right (pp. 178-179).
- The union of anticapitalist forces of Right and Left drove out everything liberal (p. 179).
- Socialism and nationalism were linked in Germany from the beginning: Fichte, Rodbertus and Lassalle are ancestors of National Socialism and acknowledged fathers of socialism (p. 179).
- Under Marxist leadership of the labor movement the authoritarian-nationalist element receded for a time (and, per Hayek's note, only partially); from 1914 a series of Marxist-trained teachers led workers and idealist youth, not conservatives, toward National Socialism (p. 179; p. 186, note 5).
- The sequence of teachers: Sombart (pp. 179-180), Plenge (pp. 180-182), Ostwald and the scientists and engineers (p. 182), Rathenau and Naumann (p. 182), Lensch (pp. 182-184).
- Plenge and Lensch supplied the leading ideas for the immediate masters of National Socialism, Spengler and Moeller van den Bruck (p. 184); Hayek's note adds Spann, Freyer, Schmitt and Jünger (p. 188, note 27).
- For Spengler and Moeller, liberalism is the archenemy; the World War was socialism's war against liberalism (pp. 184-185).
- Moeller's Third Reich was meant to give Germans a socialism adapted to their nature and undefiled by Western liberalism; "And so it did" (p. 185).
- These writers were not isolated: by 1922 an observer (Pribram) noted anticapitalism being treated as continuation of the war against the Entente (pp. 185-186).
- The fusion was completed first in the German Youth Movement and then expounded by the Tat circle (p. 186).
- The English and American present resembles this: the same drawing together of Left and Right socialists and contempt for the liberal; conservative socialism is the dominant trend "among us now" (p. 186).

## Concessions and qualifications

- The ideas were a minority strand before 1914 and are easily exaggerated (p. 178).
- Defeat, suffering and nationalism are admitted as partial causes ("not merely") (p. 178).
- The authors were powerful writers, not cranks (p. 178).
- Marxism contained liberal elements, internationalism and democracy (pp. 178-179); the nationalist element receded while Marxism directed the labor movement (p. 179).
- Sombart's outburst was at the time too much even for most Germans (p. 180).
- Plenge at first hoped to reconcile liberty and organization (p. 181).
- Rathenau would have shuddered had he realized the consequences of his economics (p. 182).
- Lensch's historical account of Bismarckian protection and cartelization is interesting and in many respects accurate (pp. 182-183); his remark on the mutual transformation of state and Social Democracy contains much truth (p. 184).
- Opinions may differ on how far Spengler can be regarded as a socialist (p. 184).
- Hayek's own note concedes that Kolnai's study covers these thinkers only after the ideas had been taken over by nationalists (p. 188, note 27), which is to say the standard account locates them on the nationalist Right.

## Arguments

- **A1. The main historical thesis.**
  **Premises**: (p. 179) the principal ancestors of National Socialism are also fathers of socialism + (pp. 179-184) from 1914 a line of prominent Marxist or ex-Marxist intellectuals (Sombart, Plenge, Lensch, Naumann) taught that Germany embodied socialism-as-organization against English liberal individualism + (p. 184) Spengler and Moeller took their leading ideas from Plenge and Lensch + (pp. 184-185) Spengler and Moeller identify Prussianism with socialism and liberalism as the enemy + (p. 186) the Youth Movement and Tat circle fused socialism and nationalism.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 178-179) the support that brought Nazi ideas to power came from the socialist camp; National Socialism is the fusion of Left and Right socialism.
  **Unstated premises**: that intellectual genealogy (who taught what to whom) explains mass political support; that writers who left or were expelled from Marxist socialism still count as "the socialist camp"; that shared anti-liberalism and a shared word (socialism) indicate a shared doctrine; that the ideas of intellectuals caused, rather than rationalized, the movement.

- **A2. Why Left moved Right.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 178-179) the liberal elements in Marxism (internationalism, democracy) are obstacles to the realization of socialism (established in ch05 and ch10, assumed here) + (p. 179) this became increasingly clear in Germany.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 179) socialists of the Left approached those of the Right.
  **Unstated premise**: that the socialists themselves perceived this and acted on it; the chapter shows individual intellectuals doing so, not the party or its voters.

- **A3. Against the capitalist-reaction thesis.**
  **Premises**: (p. 178) the ideas' carriers were socialists + (p. 178) Germany lacked a strong bourgeoisie + (pp. 183-184) the educated bourgeoisie were, on Lensch's own testimony, the liberals who opposed the tendency.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 178) Nazism was not a capitalist reaction against socialism.
  **Note**: this is the Marxist interpretation of fascism Hayek is denying; he gives no evidence about business support or its absence.

- **A4. The lesson for England and America.**
  **Premises**: (p. 186) the same convergence of Left and Right socialists and contempt for liberalism is visible in current English and American literature + A1.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 186) Britain is on the path Germany took; the ideological war against the West had almost succeeded before the real war began.
  **Unstated premise**: similar ideas in a different institutional setting will have similar political results.

## Evidence offered

For each item: what it is, and what a historian would want.

- **Genealogical assertion**: Fichte, Rodbertus, Lassalle as joint ancestors of socialism and National Socialism (p. 179). No texts quoted. The editor's note supplies identifications, and says Fichte's categories anticipated the Herrenvolk concept (p. 186, note 4). A historian would want: evidence that Nazis drew on these three, and that their socialism (state socialism, not Marxism) was representative.
- **Secondary authority**: Rohan Butler's Roots of National Socialism (1942) for the 150-year persistence of the strand (p. 178; p. 186, note 3) and for Naumann (p. 187, note 19). A wartime British study; Hayek leans on it rather than on primary sources for the pre-1914 period.
- **Hayek's footnote quotation**: Bebel telling Bismarck in 1892 that Social Democracy is a sort of preparatory school for militarism (p. 186, note 5). No source given. A historian would want context: this may be an ironic or tactical parliamentary remark.
- **Quotation and paraphrase of an opponent**: Sombart's Händler und Helden, 1915 (pp. 179-180). Biographical claims (Marxian until at least 1909; denied a chair; main channel of Marxian ideas into German thought) are asserted without citation (p. 179). The editor supplies the page for the one direct quotation (p. 187, note 7). Most of pp. 179-180 is Hayek's paraphrase in free indirect style, so Sombart's voice and Hayek's summary are hard to separate. A historian would want: how widely read, and whether a wartime polemic by an academic who had by then abandoned Marxist politics shows anything about socialists.
- **Quotation of an opponent**: Plenge, 1789 und 1914 (pp. 180-181). Hayek gives no page references in the text; all are supplied by the editor (p. 187, notes 11-15). Hayek's quotation at p. 181 stitches passages from different pages with ellipses. The claim that Plenge's Marx und Hegel began the Hegel renaissance among Marxian scholars is asserted (p. 180).
- **Quotation, source untraceable**: Plenge in Die Glocke, 1918, that socialism must be power policy, and on self-determination (pp. 181-182). **Editor's note**: neither the editors of the German Collected Works edition nor Caldwell could locate the exact source (p. 187, note 16). These are the passages Hayek says forecast all the ideas of Hitler's New Order, so the most pointed evidence in the Plenge section is unverified.
- **Hearsay**: Ostwald "is reported to have stated publicly" that Germany has discovered organization (p. 182). No source from Hayek; the editor's note gives only biography (p. 187, note 17).
- **Assertion**: Rathenau determined the economic views of the wartime generation; some of his closest collaborators later formed the backbone of the staff of what Hayek calls Göring's Five-Year Plan administration (p. 182). No names or sources. The editor notes Rathenau was a progressive internationalist and a Jew, assassinated in 1922 (p. 187, note 18), which complicates his placement in a history of Nazi ideas; my observation.
- **Assertion**: Naumann a former Marxist; Mitteleuropa probably the best-selling German war book (p. 182). Supported only by reference to Butler (p. 187, note 19). The editor describes Naumann as founder of the German Democratic Party (p. 187, note 19); whether he was ever a Marxist is not substantiated here.
- **Quotation of an opponent, the fullest documentary evidence**: Lensch, Three Years of World Revolution, English translation 1918 (pp. 182-184; Hayek's cite at p. 188, note 21; pages supplied by the editor, notes 22-26). **Editor's correction**: in the long passage at p. 183 Lensch actually wrote that the country "is to be regarded as revolutionary or not", where Hayek's text has liberal or not (p. 188, note 22). This is a substantive misquotation in a sentence about liberalism. The editor also notes Lensch moved right during the war and was expelled from the party in 1922 (pp. 187-188, note 20), which bears on whether he represents "the socialist camp".
- **Quotation of an opponent**: Spengler, Preussentum und Sozialismus, 1920 (pp. 184-185). Pages supplied by the editor (p. 189, notes 29-37). Hayek's own note links Spengler's triad to Carl Schmitt's three stages ending in the totalitarian state (p. 189, note 32). The influence claim, that Spengler "merely gave expression to ideas widely held by German socialists" (p. 184), rests on the preceding four authors.
- **Quotation of an opponent**: Moeller van den Bruck (p. 185). **Editor's correction**: Hayek listed the three quotations at pp. 87, 90 and 100 of Sozialismus und Aussenpolitik; they are at pp. 100-102 (p. 189, note 38). Moeller as "patron saint" of National Socialism is asserted (p. 185); the editor says Das dritte Reich profoundly influenced Hitler, and also that Moeller criticized socialism along with liberalism and democracy (p. 188, note 27).
- **Contemporary observer**: Karl Pribram, 1922, on anticapitalism as continuation of the war, with Scheler and Korsch as further examples (pp. 185-186; p. 189, note 40). The one piece of evidence that is neither an exhibit nor Hayek's own say-so; it supports diffusion among intellectuals.
- **Assertion**: the German Youth Movement was almost entirely socialist in inspiration (p. 186). No evidence.
- **Assertion**: the Tat circle and Fried's Ende des Kapitalismus (p. 186); not quoted. Editor identifies (pp. 189-190, note 41).
- **Assertion of parallel**: resemblance to current English and American literature (p. 186); no examples here (deferred to ch13).

**What is missing overall (my assessment)**: no evidence on who voted for or joined the Nazi party; nothing on the Social Democratic and Communist parties' actual conduct 1918-1933 (they were the Nazis' principal opponents and first victims); nothing on Hitler's own writings or the party programme; nothing on racial anti-Semitism as an independent tradition; nothing on conservative elites, the army, or business in 1930-1933; no test of the counterfactual that countries with strong socialist intellectual traditions but no military defeat went the same way. The chapter shows convincingly that a body of German anti-liberal, pro-organization, nationalist writing used the word socialism approvingly and that several of its authors began as Marxists. It does not show that the socialist camp supplied the support that brought Nazism to power, which is the claim at p. 178. Confident formulations that outrun this: "came precisely from the socialist camp" (p. 178); "it cannot be denied" (p. 178); "And so it did" (p. 185); the war of ideas having "almost succeeded" (p. 186).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on the Introduction and ch01-ch02**: the thesis that Nazism is an outgrowth of socialist trends, not a reaction to them, is announced early in the book; this chapter is its historical documentation.
- **Depends on ch05 and ch10** for A2: that democracy and internationalism obstruct socialism's realization (ch10 p. 158) is the theoretical premise; ch12 presents German socialists as having discovered it in practice.
- **Reuses ch09**: Beamtenstaat (ch09 p. 152; here p. 185) and Germany's organized society; the commercial/military typology (ch09 p. 148) reappears as Sombart's merchants and heroes with the valuation reversed (pp. 179-180). Hayek and his opponents agree on the typology and disagree on which side is admirable; my observation.
- **Reuses ch10**: Prussian virtues (ch10 pp. 162-163); glorification of power, the Webbs and Shaw resembling the German ancestors (ch10 p. 160); Plenge on self-determination of small peoples (p. 182) parallels Shaw on small states; anti-Semitism and anticapitalism from one root (ch10 p. 157) is not developed further here, and anti-Semitism is nearly absent from this chapter.
- **Reuses ch11**: redefinition of freedom by German thinkers (ch11 p. 171) is illustrated by Lensch's socialist ideal of freedom (p. 182) and his dismissal of English conceptions of liberalism (p. 183).
- **Feeds ch13** (The Totalitarians in Our Midst): the closing paragraph (p. 186) hands over directly; ch13 opens by saying England resembles the Germany of twenty or thirty years earlier and equates organization with planning (p. 190).
- **Tension with ch10** (mine): ch10 explains the rise of the worst by mass-selection mechanisms (lowest common denominator, enemy-hatred); ch12 explains Nazism by the ideas of professors and powerful writers. The book holds both, but does not say how the two levels connect.
- **Tension within the chapter** (mine): Hayek concedes Marxism restrained nationalism (p. 179) and that the ideas were a despised minority strand before 1914 (p. 178), then attributes the change to war hysteria and defeat (p. 179), yet denies that defeat and nationalism are the main cause (p. 178). The war is doing more work in his own narrative than his thesis allows.
- **Shift in "socialism"** (mine): in ch03 socialism is defined by its means (abolition of private enterprise, central planning) as a species of collectivism. Here it is defined by the Germans quoted: organization, the state above the individual, anti-liberalism. Spengler's "authoritarian socialism" since the eighteenth century (p. 184) is not socialism in ch03's sense of collective ownership pursued for equality. The chapter's title claim depends on accepting the opponents' broad usage. Hayek half-acknowledges this for Spengler (p. 184).
- **Shift in "liberalism"**: here liberalism is identified through German polemics with England, 1789, commerce and parliamentarism. Compare ch01's positive account. Note also Lensch's remark, which Hayek calls true, that German liberalism belonged to the bourgeoisie of the 1850s-1870s (p. 183).
- **Editor versus author**: the editor's notes repeatedly supply missing page references, flag an unlocatable source, a substantive misquotation and a page-citation error, and add biographical facts (Rathenau, Lensch's expulsion, Moeller's criticism of socialism) that cut against Hayek's grouping (pp. 187-189).

## Open questions

- Is the chapter's thesis about ideas (Nazi doctrine has socialist intellectual ancestry) or about support (socialists brought it to power)? Hayek asserts both at p. 178; the evidence bears only on the first. Synthesis should keep these apart when evaluating the book.
- How representative are Sombart, Plenge and Lensch of German socialism? All three had broken with or been marginal to the Social Democratic party; what did the party's mainstream say in 1914-1918 and after?
- Does the argument prove too much? If any doctrine exalting organization and the state is socialism, then the claim that Nazism has socialist roots approaches a tautology.
- How does Hayek's account compare with other explanations (Sonderweg, crisis of 1929-1933, conservative elites' miscalculation, racial ideology)? He names and dismisses only the Marxist capitalist-reaction thesis.
- The role assigned to the First World War and its planned economy (pp. 179, 181): is wartime planning, rather than socialist doctrine, the real common cause? That would connect to ch09 p. 145 and ch11 p. 173 on wartime as partial totalitarianism, and to the book's concern about carrying war planning into peace.
- What weight should the Lensch misquotation carry (p. 188, note 22)? With "revolutionary" restored, the sentence says constitutions do not determine whether a country is historically revolutionary, which is a weaker support for Hayek's point about redefining liberalism.
- Is the claimed parallel with Britain and America (p. 186) borne out by ch13's evidence, and by what happened after 1945?
- Fichte, Rodbertus and Lassalle are named but not examined. A cross-book synthesis on the history of socialism should check whether calling them ancestors of National Socialism is more than Butler's wartime framing.
