# fm03: Introduction (Hayek) (pp. 56–63)

Scope note: the chapter file opens with the tail of the 1976 preface (p. 56) and ends with the opening of ch. 1 (p. 63). Hayek's Introduction proper runs from the heading on p. 56 to the top of p. 62; its footnotes run pp. 62-63. Unbracketed footnotes are Hayek's; bracketed ones are the editor's (Caldwell).

## Question this chapter answers

Why should England and the United States, at war with Nazi Germany, study the growth of the ideas that produced National Socialism, and what entitles the author to warn that the democracies are in some danger of repeating Germany's course?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| socialism (wide sense) | p. 59 | Not a party programme but the shared conviction of nearly all opinion-formers in the democracies that society must go on moving in a socialist direction; party disputes are only over how much and in whose interest. Not yet defined analytically that comes in ch. 3 (p. 80). |
| liberalism 'in the old sense' / nineteenth-century liberalism | p. 57, p. 61 | The creed the reformers now hold in contempt and which socialism 'drove out' in Germany. Used as the contrast term to socialism; no positive content given yet. |
| totalitarianism | p. 60, p. 62 | The Nazi, Fascist and Soviet regime type, treated as one family; said to be produced by the 'socialist element', not the specifically German one. |
| National Socialism / naziism | p. 58 | A 'necessary outcome' of earlier socialist trends, not a capitalist reaction against them; its conflict with the German Left is a quarrel between rival socialist factions. |
| inevitability / 'inevitable trends' | p. 56, p. 57 | Rejected at two levels: history obeys no known laws, and the move toward socialism is driven by opinion, not by 'objective facts'. Fatalism about trends is itself listed as a German symptom. |
| planning | p. 59 | Mentioned once, in scare quotes, as something whose 'alleged inevitability' will be dealt with later (ch. 4). Not defined here. |
| Prussianism | p. 62 | The Prussian tradition of a state consciously organised from the top; kin to socialism, but not the cause of totalitarianism. |
| freedom | p. 59 | Stated only as what the Allies fight for: freedom to shape one's life according to one's own ideas; said to be 'a great deal, but not enough' as a positive creed. |

## Leading propositions

- No historical development is inevitable and man knows no laws that history must obey; for that very reason one can learn from the past how to avoid repeating it (p. 56).
- The book is the product of twice watching a similar evolution of ideas, first in the German-speaking world and then, twenty to twenty-five years later, in England and America (pp. 56-57).
- England and the United States are in some danger of repeating Germany's fate; the danger is not immediate and present conditions are still remote from Germany's, but the road gets harder to leave the further one goes (p. 57).
- The resemblance is not to Hitler's Germany but between the present current of ideas in the democracies and German thought during and after the First World War (p. 57).
- The shared symptoms: the wish to keep wartime organisation for peacetime purposes, contempt for nineteenth-century liberalism, spurious realism and cynicism, fatalistic acceptance of inevitable trends (p. 57).
- In Germany it was largely people of good will, admired in the democracies, who prepared the way for the forces that now stand for everything they detest (p. 58).
- Fascism and naziism were not a reaction against the socialist trends of the preceding period but a necessary outcome of them (p. 58).
- Many who sincerely hate naziism work for ideals whose realisation would lead straight to the same tyranny (p. 58).
- The argument does not rest mainly on country parallels, which are deceptive, and it does not claim the development is inevitable (p. 58).
- The socialism in question is not a party matter: the people whose views count in the democracies are all, in some measure, socialists (p. 59).
- The move toward socialism happens because nearly everybody wants it; no objective facts make it inevitable (p. 59).
- A second reason for the inquiry is the war itself: the democracies lack a clear statement of their positive ideals and so cannot answer enemy propaganda or plan the peace (pp. 59-60).
- Both Left and Right were deceived in thinking the Nazi party served the capitalists and opposed all socialism (p. 60).
- The theory that Germans as such are inherently vicious is untenable and discreditable; the real problem is how a particular set of ideas won over the last seventy years and why its victory brought the most vicious elements to the top (pp. 60-61).
- Blaming German wickedness is dangerous because it becomes the excuse for imposing the very institutions that produced that wickedness (p. 61).
- Socialist exiles and correspondents cannot see German events in perspective; the fact that socialists were the only numerically significant opposition to the Nazis shows only that practically all Germans had become socialists (p. 61).
- The Nazi Right versus Left conflict is the kind that always arises between rival socialist factions (p. 61).
- German refugees with semi-Fascist views are socialists several stages further along than English and American socialists, not specimens of Germanness (p. 61).
- Prussianism helped German socialists, but it was the prevalence of socialist views, not Prussianism, that Germany shared with Italy and Russia, and National Socialism arose from the masses, not from the Prussian-tradition classes (p. 62).

## Arguments

- **Argument from the twice-watched trend (the book's warrant).**
  **Premises**: (p. 57) the influences on thought in civilised nations are largely similar but operate at different times and speeds; (p. 57) the author heard in England and America opinions and proposals he had met twenty or twenty-five years earlier in the German-speaking world; (p. 58) he spent half his adult life in Austria and half in the US and England; (unstated) similar opinions, once dominant, tend to produce similar institutions and political outcomes regardless of differing national traditions; (unstated) the German outcome was caused by those opinions and not mainly by defeat, inflation, depression or the Versailles settlement.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 57) the recurrence suggests, 'if not the necessity, at least the probability', that developments will take a similar course; Germany's fate is the one the democracies risk repeating.

- **Argument that naziism is an outcome of socialism, not a reaction to it.**
  **Premises**: (p. 58) the internal regimes of communist Russia and National Socialist Germany share many repellent features; (p. 61) socialists were the only numerically significant opposition to the Nazis, which shows liberalism in the old sense had already been driven out; (p. 61) the Nazi quarrel with the Left is a quarrel of rival socialist factions; (p. 62) what Germany, Italy and Russia had in common was socialist opinion, not Prussianism; (unstated) a feature shared by all cases of an outcome and absent elsewhere is its cause.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 58) the rise of fascism and naziism was 'a necessary outcome' of the preceding socialist tendencies. The demonstration is deferred: 'As we hope to show' (p. 61).

- **Argument against the national-character explanation.**
  **Premises**: (p. 60) Mill's *On Liberty* drew on Goethe and Humboldt, while Carlyle and H. S. Chamberlain, forebears of National Socialism, were British; (p. 60) the national-character view borrows the worst of German racial theory; (p. 61) it works as escapism, sparing us the re-examination of beliefs taken over from the Germans.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 60-61) the thing to explain is the victory of a set of ideas under particular circumstances, not German viciousness.

- **Argument from non-inevitability to the point of the book.**
  **Premises**: (p. 59) the movement toward socialism is driven by what nearly everybody wants, not by objective facts; (p. 59) those whose convictions drive it might recoil if they saw where it leads; (p. 58) if the development were inevitable there would be no point in writing.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 58) the development can be prevented if people realise in time where their efforts may lead. Ideas are the operative cause, so persuasion is the remedy.

- **Wartime-relevance argument.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 59-60) the democracies showed insecurity and uncertainty of aim toward the dictators, explicable only by confusion about their own ideals; (p. 60) features of Hitler's system have been recommended for imitation by people unaware they are integral to it.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 59-60) understanding the forces behind National Socialism is needed to understand the enemy, counter propaganda and build the post-war world.

## Evidence offered

- Autobiographical testimony: half an adult life in Austria, half in the US and England (p. 58). Kind: personal witness. It grounds the claim of similarity but cannot by itself establish that similar ideas have similar effects.
- List of shared symptoms of opinion (p. 57). Kind: bare assertion, unreferenced. The figure 'at least nine out of every ten' of the lessons reformers draw from the war (p. 57) is rhetorical, not a count.
- Germany's socialist policy, then Sweden, held up as models by progressives (p. 58). Kind: historical example, unreferenced.
- Similarity of the internal regimes of communist Russia and Nazi Germany, said to be 'widely recognized' (p. 58). Kind: appeal to common knowledge.
- 'We are all socialists now' (p. 59). Kind: slogan as evidence of consensus. Editor's note 3 attributes it to Sir William Harcourt (p. 62).
- Mill's debt to Goethe and Humboldt; Carlyle and Chamberlain as British forebears of National Socialism (p. 60). Kind: historical counterexample. Hayek's own note 4 backs the Mill claim with Lord Morley's remark that the main argument of *On Liberty* 'came from Germany' (p. 62).
- Semi-Fascist views heard from socialist German refugees (p. 61). Kind: anecdote.
- Novalis on Prussia administered like a factory, and the claim that early French socialists recognised the kinship in Hayek's note 5 (p. 62). Kind: quotation.
- Where language outruns evidence: 'necessary outcome' (p. 58) and 'lead straight to the abhorred tyranny' (p. 58) are asserted before any mechanism is given, and sit beside the denial of inevitability on the same page. The inference from 'socialists were the only opposition' to 'practically all Germans had become socialists' (p. 61) is a non sequitur as stated (my observation): it ignores Catholic, conservative and liberal voters and reads opposition strength as proof of a general conversion.

Concessions and qualifications recorded:
- The danger is 'not immediate' and conditions in England and the US are 'still so remote' from Germany's (p. 57).
- No resemblance yet to Hitler's Germany (p. 57).
- Country parallels are 'deceptive' and are not the main basis of the argument (p. 58).
- The development is not inevitable (p. 58); probability, not necessity (p. 57).
- The Germans who prepared the way were 'largely people of good will' (p. 58); socialist refugees act 'with the best will in the world' (p. 61).
- Prussian tradition did give German socialists 'much support' (pp. 61-62).
- Fighting for freedom to shape our own lives is 'a great deal', though not enough (p. 59).

Editor versus author in the notes:
- Editor's note 2 ties Hayek's 'definite trend' to his LSE inaugural lecture, 'The Trend of Economic Thinking' (p. 62).
- Editor records that Hayek's note 4 was originally placed after the name Humboldt and has been moved (p. 62).
- Editor supplies the German original of the Novalis line, which names Friedrich Wilhelm the First; Hayek's English rendering says only 'Frederick William' (pp. 62-63). The editor adds that Novalis looked forward to a spirituality that would make government unnecessary (p. 63), context Hayek does not give.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Internal tension (my observation): naziism is a 'necessary outcome' of socialist trends and socialist ideals would 'lead straight' to tyranny (p. 58), yet nothing is inevitable (p. 56) (p. 58). The consistent reading is conditional: if the ideals are pursued to realisation, the result follows; whether they are pursued is open. Synthesis should check whether later chapters keep to the conditional form.
- The promise to deal with the 'alleged inevitability of planning' (p. 59) is redeemed in ch. 4 (pp. 88-97).
- 'Why in the end this victory has brought the most vicious elements among them to the top' (pp. 60-61) announces ch. 10 (Why the Worst Get on Top).
- The 'rival socialist factions' reading of Nazi versus Left (p. 61) and the Prussianism-socialism kinship (p. 62) announce ch. 12 (The Socialist Roots of Naziism); 'features of Hitler's system recommended for imitation' (p. 60) announces ch. 13 (The Totalitarians in Our Midst).
- The claim that wartime organisation is to be kept for peacetime 'creation' (p. 57) links to ch. 1's point that the war has only accelerated an older trend (p. 63).
- 'Socialism' here is a loose sociological label for the climate of opinion (p. 59); ch. 3 narrows it to a method, central planning (pp. 80-82). The Introduction's sweeping 'we are all socialists' only fits the argument of the later chapters if the opinion-formers want planning in that narrow sense.
- The denial that the argument rests 'mainly' on parallels (p. 58) should be held against chs. 12-13, which are built largely on German-English parallels.
- Method link: ideas, not interests or material conditions, drive history (p. 57) (p. 59). The same assumption underlies ch. 1 and is argued against technological determinism in ch. 4.

## Open questions

- What exactly is the modal strength of the thesis: necessity, probability, or tendency? The Introduction uses all three (pp. 57-58). Which does the body of the book actually argue for?
- Is the German case sound as the paradigm? Hayek names no rival explanation (war defeat, hyperinflation, depression, constitutional weakness) in the Introduction; does any later chapter address them?
- Does the 'socialism' the opinion-formers are said to share (p. 59) coincide with the 'planning against competition' that the mechanism chapters attack, or does the argument slide from the wide sense to the narrow one?
- Sweden is mentioned as the current progressive model (p. 58). Does Hayek ever say why Sweden, with a long socialist record, did not follow the German path?
- How far does the autobiographical warrant (p. 58) carry? It supports the similarity of opinion, not the causal claim.
- Who are the 'people of good will' in Germany (p. 58)? Names are deferred; check ch. 12.
