# ch13: The Totalitarians in Our Midst (pp. 190–207)

Chapter text runs pp. 190–202; the footnotes run pp. 202–207. Notes in square brackets in the source are the editor's (Caldwell's); the rest are Hayek's.

## Question this chapter answers

How far has England (and, secondarily, America) already travelled the intellectual and institutional path Germany took a generation earlier, who is carrying it there, and where does the real impetus come from? Hayek's answer: the ideas of pre-Nazi Germany are now common in English writing (illustrated by E. H. Carr and C. H. Waddington), but the driving force is the joint support of organized capital and organized labor for monopoly, made possible by the Left's propaganda against competition (pp. 190, 197-201).

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Totalitarians (in our midst) | p. 192 | Not Nazis or sympathizers with Nazi rule, but sincere, disinterested idealists whose views are "the views from which totalitarianism springs"; includes people who are Hitler's enemies only because of his anti-Semitism (p. 192). The word is applied to holders of ideas, not to advocates of dictatorship. |
| "Organization" / planning | p. 190 | Treated as the same thing: "the enthusiasm for 'organization' of everything (we now call it 'planning')" (p. 190). Planning here keeps its book-wide sense of central direction replacing competition, and is asserted to be a German import. |
| Realism (Carr's) | p. 193 | As Hayek reads Carr: making morality a function of politics and accepting no standard of value but fact; Hayek glosses it as "nothing but expediency matters" (p. 193). The gloss is Hayek's, not Carr's wording. |
| Abstract general principles / morals | p. 193 | The "old morals" Carr is said to discard; for Hayek, without them "merit becomes solely a matter of arbitrary opinion" and treaties lose meaning (p. 193). Morals here means general rules binding irrespective of expediency, the moral analogue of the Rule of Law of ch. 6. |
| Scientific organization of society | p. 195 | The ideal of society organized "through and through" from the top by experts according to a blueprint; associated with the "intolerance of reason" of the specialist (p. 195). |
| Monopoly | p. 197 | Chiefly the deliberate "monopolistic organization of industry" by capital and labor in concert, backed by the state (pp. 197-198). Distinguished from (a) "inevitable" (natural) monopoly such as railways, gas, electricity (p. 199), and (b) state monopoly, which is "always a state-protected monopoly" (p. 199). Private monopoly is "scarcely ever complete" and rarely lasting (p. 199). |
| Corporative society | p. 198 | The monopolists' actual aim: organized industries as semi-independent, self-governing "estates"; not totalitarianism, but in Hayek's view it cannot remain in private hands (p. 198). |
| Competition / competitive society | p. 198 | The order in which the entrepreneur's favored position is justified because "of the many who take the risks, only a few achieve the success" (p. 198); the only order in which "at least some degree of independence and freedom has been secured to every worker" (p. 201). |
| Exploitation | p. 199 | Used of monopoly gains whoever receives them: the higher wages a monopolist can pay are "just as much the result of exploitation as his own profit" and impoverish consumers and other wage-earners (p. 199). |
| Impersonal discipline of the market | p. 201 | One pole of a strict two-way choice; the other is an order "directed by the will of a few individuals" (p. 201). "Impersonal" contrasts with personal will, not with human origin. |
| Reactionary | p. 201 | Inverted from ordinary usage: the Labour party's planning programme is "a reactionary movement"; Labour risks becoming "a second reactionary party" beside the traditionalist Right (pp. 201-202). Liberalism is the progressive position. |
| Vested interests | p. 197 | Organized capital and organized labor (p. 197); also the new interests created by "every raid on the gains of monopoly" (p. 199). |

## Leading propositions

- The magnitude of Nazi outrages has strengthened, not weakened, the belief that it cannot happen here; but fifteen years earlier the same outcome would have seemed fantastic in Germany too (p. 190).
- The relevant comparison is not present Germany but the Germany of twenty or thirty years ago, which conditions in the democracies increasingly resemble (p. 190).
- Symptoms: convergence of Right and Left on economic views and their common opposition to liberalism; veneration for the state; admiration of power and bigness; enthusiasm for "organization" (p. 190).
- England has become half-ashamed of the political views once thought characteristically English; Morley, Sidgwick, Acton, Dicey are "obsolete Victorians", Bismarck is treated sympathetically and Gladstone sneered at (p. 191).
- Almost every single feature of totalitarianism has been recommended by somebody in England or America, including by people who oppose Hitler only on account of his anti-Semitism (p. 192).
- The similarity is more one of temper than of specific argument: readiness to break all cultural ties with the past and stake everything on one experiment (p. 192).
- The writers preparing the way are sincere idealists of intellectual distinction; Hayek deliberately picks authors whose sincerity is above suspicion (p. 192).
- E. H. Carr's two books show the German influence most markedly: historical-school "realism", rejection of abstract principles, denial that pacta sunt servanda is a moral principle, equation of Nazi and Anglo-American identification of national interest with right, collapse of the society/state distinction, propaganda as corollary of mass production, inevitability of centralized planning in larger units, autarky, approval of Hitler's reorganization of Europe, the moral function of war (pp. 192-195).
- Carr's inevitability thesis rests on "familiar economic fallacies": technological necessity of monopoly and "potential plenty" (p. 194).
- The agitation of scientists for a "scientific" organization of society is a German development now reappearing in English-speaking countries (p. 195).
- German scholars and scientists, especially natural scientists and engineers, submitted to the Nazis more readily than almost any other class (p. 195).
- A general shift of education from the "humanities" to the "realities", as in Germany 1840–1940, has political effects (p. 195).
- Waddington, unlike most such writers, sees and says that the tendencies he supports lead to a totalitarian system, and prefers it (p. 196); his social and economic claims are unscientific and rest on political tracts (p. 197).
- Middle-class socialist movements (Acland's Common Wealth, Priestley's 1941 Committee) are symptomatic but not yet important political forces (p. 197).
- The impetus toward totalitarianism comes mainly from organized capital and organized labor, whose policies point the same way: monopolistic organization of industry (pp. 197-198).
- This movement is not inevitable, but if continued it "will lead us to totalitarianism" (p. 198).
- Capitalist monopolists aim only at a corporative society of self-governing industrial "estates", but they are shortsighted: no society will long leave such decisions and such aggregations of power in private hands (p. 198).
- Blame should not fall exclusively or mainly on capitalists; their propensity to monopoly is old and would not by itself be formidable. The fatal development was their enlisting other groups and, through them, the state (p. 198).
- The change in public opinion that made this possible, working through legislation and the courts, is "more than anything the result of the propaganda against competition by the Left" (pp. 198-199).
- Measures aimed at monopolists often strengthen monopoly: every raid on monopoly gains creates new vested interests in it; a monopoly whose gains are widely shared is politically more dangerous and more powerful (p. 199).
- Higher monopoly wages are exploitation of consumers and of other wage-earners (p. 199).
- Even where monopoly is inevitable, state ownership is doubtful as a remedy; separate private monopolies leave the consumer stronger than "coordinated" central control (p. 199).
- State monopoly is protected against both potential competition and effective criticism, and tends to make a temporary monopoly permanent (p. 199).
- A state entangled in running monopolies has crushing power over the individual yet is weak in formulating policy, becoming identified with "those who run things" (pp. 199-200).
- Where monopoly really is inevitable, strong state control of private monopoly with stringent price control (the older American plan) is probably better than state management, even at some cost in efficiency (p. 200).
- The fatal turning point was the labor movement's adoption of anti-competition doctrines and its entanglement in the strife for privilege; recent monopoly growth is largely deliberate collaboration of organized capital and organized labor at the expense of the poorest and the unemployed (p. 200).
- There are only two possibilities: the impersonal discipline of the market or direction by the will of a few (p. 201).
- The Labour party programme for a "planned society" is "indistinguishable" in outline, detail and wording from German socialist dreams of twenty-five years earlier (p. 201).
- That the main progressive party has joined what is in fact a reactionary movement is "the decisive change" of the time and "the source of the mortal danger to everything a liberal must value" (p. 201).

## Arguments

**A. The "it can happen here" argument by lagged analogy**

- **Premises**: (p. 190) Nazi Germany would have seemed impossible to Germans and foreign observers fifteen years before. (p. 190) The proper comparison is with the Germany of 20–30 years ago, whose "typically German" features (state veneration, admiration of power and bigness, organization of everything, Right–Left agreement against liberalism) are now familiar in England. (p. 191) English opinion has repudiated its own liberal tradition. (pp. 191-197) Current English writing (Keynes's 1915 summary of Jaffé as a mirror; Carr; Waddington; Labour documents) reproduces the German ideas.
  **Unstated premises**: (i) those ideas were a principal cause of German totalitarianism rather than a by-product of defeat, inflation, depression or institutional weakness (argued in ch. 12, assumed here); (ii) the same ideas will have similar effects in a country with very different institutions and traditions; (iii) a handful of selected authors are representative of "hundreds of others" (asserted p. 192).
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 190, 192, 198) the democracies are moving in the same direction and will arrive at totalitarianism if they continue.

**B. Carr as German-style totalitarian thinker**

- **Premises**: (p. 193) Carr adopts historical-school realism, rejects abstract general principles and the moral status of treaties. (p. 193) Without general principles, merit is arbitrary opinion and treaties are meaningless. (p. 193) Carr denies the society/state distinction, which is Schmitt's definition of totalitarianism. (pp. 193-194) Carr treats propaganda as a corollary of mass production. (pp. 194-195) Carr holds the trend to centralized planning inevitable, endorses autarky and a reorganization of Europe "such as Hitler has undertaken", and praises the moral function of war.
  **Unstated premise**: holding the same doctrine as a Nazi theorist on one point is evidence of a totalitarian tendency in the holder (guilt by doctrinal resemblance).
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 193-195) Carr's future society is "quite on the totalitarian model"; German views of the last war are now Carr's.

**C. Scientism and politics**

- **Premises**: (p. 195) In Germany scientific and technical specialists had unique influence on political opinion, and it was "not often on the side of liberty". (p. 195) They submitted readily to the Nazis. (p. 196) The belief that science is competent in morality, and that history obeys scientific laws, is favored by partisans of arbitrary authority because it eliminates liberty and individual action (Benda). (pp. 196-197) Waddington claims science can pass ethical judgment, denies scientific value to the freedom to be odd, and expects a centralized totalitarian economy.
  **Unstated premise**: the disposition of the specialist (impatience with the unplanned) is a cause, not merely a correlate, of political authoritarianism.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 195-197) the scientists' campaign for planning is a totalitarian tendency, and its economic content is unscientific.

**D. Monopoly as the road to totalitarianism**

- **Premises**: (pp. 197-198) Organized capital and organized labor jointly support monopolistic organization of industry. (p. 198) Decisions by managers of organized industries are not ones "any society will long leave to private individuals"; a state that permits such aggregations of power cannot leave them in private control. (p. 198) Hence the corporative order will be taken over by the state, as happened to the German industrialists.
  **Unstated premises**: monopolistic organization of many industries requires central coordination between them (argued in ch. 4 and ch. 8); state takeover of organized industry amounts to central planning and so to totalitarianism (ch. 5–7).
  **Conclusion**: (p. 198) continuing on this path "will lead us to totalitarianism", though the movement is not inevitable.

**E. Why the Left, not the capitalist, is chiefly responsible**

- **Premises**: (p. 198) The capitalists' taste for monopoly is old and by itself not formidable. (pp. 198-199) What made it formidable is state support obtained through a change in public opinion. (p. 199) That change is mainly due to the Left's propaganda against competition. (p. 200) Labor shares monopoly profits at the expense of the community.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 200-201) Left support makes the tendency "so irresistible"; labor leaders who have "done once and for all with the mad competitive system" proclaim the doom of individual freedom.

**F. Against nationalizing monopolies**

- **Premises**: (p. 199) Private monopoly is seldom complete or lasting and faces potential competition. (p. 199) State monopoly is protected from competition and criticism and is made permanent. (p. 199) The controller becomes interested in defending its appointees; remedying abuse means admitting responsibility. (p. 199) With many monopolies, separate private hands leave the consumer stronger than one coordinating authority.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 200) where monopoly is inevitable, regulated private monopoly with strict price control is better than state management; the resulting inefficiency is "a small price".

**G. The dichotomy**

- **Premises**: (p. 201) Either the impersonal discipline of the market or direction by the will of a few. (p. 201) Destroying the first helps create the second.
  **Unstated premise**: there is no stable intermediate arrangement (this is the book's "no middle way" thesis from ch. 3, restated without argument).
  **Conclusion**: (p. 201) Labour's planned society endangers the personal freedom of workers.

## Evidence offered

- **Quotation from an opponent/authority**: Harold Nicolson on Conservative backbenchers being "socialists at heart" (p. 190; source p. 202). One remark in the Spectator carries a claim about a whole party.
- **Historical generalization, unsupported**: Fabians' sympathy with Conservatives (p. 190); the editor supplies the supporting instances (Boer War, education, imperial preference) (p. 202).
- **Quotation as mirror**: Keynes's 1915 review of Jaffé on the "militarisation of our industrial life" (p. 191; source p. 202). Hayek then asks rhetorically whether any passage is not mirrored in current English literature; no English text is set beside it. Hayek concedes one disanalogy: no English author has "dared openly to disparage individual happiness" (p. 191).
- **Bare assertion**: "there is scarcely a leaf out of Hitler's book" not recommended by someone (p. 192); "hundreds of others" hold the views of the chosen authors (p. 192). The only example in the footnote is an unnamed speaker at an unnamed "recent conference" (p. 203).
- **Assertion with an inference about refugees**: former socialists who became Nazis should be compared with those "whose conversion would not in any case have been prevented by their ancestry"; few non-Jewish Left refugees (pp. 202-203). No figures.
- **Quotations from Carr** (pp. 193-195), sourced by the editor (pp. 203-204). See the reliability list below.
- **Historical example**: German "political professors", citing Schnabel (p. 195; p. 204); du Bois-Reymond's 1870 "intellectual bodyguard of the House of Hohenzollern" speech (p. 204); Brady on the ease with which Nazis coordinated natural scientists (pp. 204-205). Brady is the one piece of independent testimony for the claim that natural scientists were the most compliant; the phrase "It is well known" (p. 195) outruns a single witness, and Brady himself notes that dismissed natural scientists were "primarily Jewish", which complicates "submitted more readily".
- **Quotation from an ally**: Benda on the superstition of science competent in morality (p. 196).
- **Quotations from Waddington** (pp. 196-197), page references supplied by the editor (pp. 205-206).
- **Mechanism argument**: the dynamics of monopoly, vested interests, state capture and the incentives of a state that owns monopolies (pp. 198-200). This is the analytically strongest part of the chapter and carries no empirical support beyond passing references to German industrialists (p. 198) and American public utilities (p. 200).
- **Citation**: W. Arthur Lewis on the Crofter case for the role of law and courts in monopoly (p. 206).
- **Quotation from opponents**: Laski's "mad competitive system" and the Labour conference resolution on retaining wartime controls; "The Old World and the New Society" on a planned society being freer (pp. 201, 207). The editor prints the resolution in full (p. 207), which lets the reader see that the resolution itself asserts that "political democracy and personal liberty can be combined" with planning; Hayek does not engage that claim here.
- **Bare assertion**: Labour's scheme is "indistinguishable" from German socialist dreams "in detail and even wording", and its catchwords are "bodily taken over from the German ideology" (p. 201). No German text is quoted for comparison.

**Reliability of Hayek's quotations and citations, per the editor's notes**

- Epigraph: Hayek's own source notes gave the Times date as February 24, 1940; it is February 24, 1937 (p. 202).
- Carr, "who makes morality a function of politics": the editor could not find the phrase; the nearest is "who regard ethics as a function of politics" on another page (p. 203). So a phrase Hayek puts in quotation marks is a paraphrase.
- Carr on society and state: Hayek omits the clause "within the national community"; the editor adds that Carr may have been reporting what the national community believed, not his own view (p. 203). This bears directly on the Schmitt comparison at p. 193, the chapter's sharpest charge.
- Carr on autarchy: Carr wrote "promotion", Hayek printed "production" (pp. 203-204). Minor.
- Carr's section heading is "The Moral Function of War" (singular) per the editor; Hayek gives "Functions" (pp. 195, 204). The three excerpts come from two different pages (p. 204), so the sentence at p. 195 is a composite.
- The Hegel-and-Marx quotation at p. 193 contains Hayek's bracketed insertions "[which]" and "[whose]", and Hayek says Carr "frankly confessed himself an adherent"; the quoted words describe the school, and the adherence is Hayek's characterization (p. 193).
- The "wrong side" charge is expressly Hayek's inference: "although he does not explicitly say so" (p. 193).
- Benda is quoted from the English translation (p. 205). Hayek's note spells the physiologist "du Bois-Raymond" and then "du Bois-Reymond" (p. 204).
- Waddington's "best authorities": the editor identifies them as Time magazine surveys, a Gollancz book and Boyd Orr's diet survey (pp. 205-206), which a reader may or may not regard as "political tracts of questionable scientific standing" (p. 197).

- "there is scarcely a leaf out of Hitler’s book which somebody or other in England or America has not recommended us to take and use for our own purposes" (p. 192)
- "the impetus of the movement toward totalitarianism comes mainly from the two great vested interests: organized capital and organized labor" (p. 197)
- "While there is no reason to believe that this movement is inevitable, there can be little doubt that if we continue on the path we have been treading, it will lead us to totalitarianism." (p. 198)
- "But a state monopoly is always a state-protected monopoly—protected against both potential competition and effective criticism." (p. 199)
- "Personally, I should much prefer to have to put up with some such inefficiency than have organized monopoly control my ways of life." (p. 200)
- "There is no other possibility than either the order governed by the impersonal discipline of the market or that directed by the will of a few individuals; and those who are out to destroy the first are wittingly or unwittingly helping to create the second." (p. 201)
- Editor, on Hayek's truncated Carr quotation: "It may be that Carr was referring to what the national community believed, rather than to what he himself believed." (p. 203)

## Concessions and qualifications

- Few if any in England would "swallow totalitarianism whole" (p. 192).
- No English author has openly disparaged individual happiness (p. 191).
- The authors attacked are sincere, disinterested and often distinguished (p. 192).
- Common Wealth and the 1941 Committee are not yet important political forces (p. 197).
- The movement to monopoly is not inevitable (p. 198).
- Capitalists do not aim at totalitarianism (p. 198), and Hayek disclaims "any tenderness toward the capitalists" (p. 198).
- Some monopolies may be "inevitable" (railways, road and air transport, gas, electricity are given hypothetically) (p. 199).
- If only a single industry were in question, state ownership of an inevitable monopoly "might well be so" the best control (p. 199).
- **State authority accepted**: "a strong state control over private monopolies", including "stringent price control which leaves no room for extraordinary profits" (p. 200). Conditions: only where monopoly is really inevitable; consistently pursued; accepted even at the cost of less satisfactory service (p. 200).
- Under planning "some workmen will perhaps be better fed" (p. 201).
- Twenty-five years ago there was "perhaps still some excuse" for believing a planned society could be freer (p. 201).
- Threats from the traditionalist Right are "a phenomenon of all ages which need not alarm us" (p. 201).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Depends on ch. 12 for the claim that the German ideas quoted there produced Nazism; ch. 13 only shows resemblance between English and German ideas, so its force is no greater than ch. 12's causal claim (pp. 190, 194).
- Depends on ch. 4 for the rejection of the technological inevitability of monopoly and of "potential plenty"; here both are dismissed as "familiar economic fallacies" without re-argument (pp. 194, 197).
- The monopoly analysis extends ch. 4 and ch. 9 (security for some groups at others' expense): labor's share in monopoly gains is the ch. 9 "security" problem in institutional form (pp. 199-200).
- The dichotomy at p. 201 restates ch. 3's claim that competition and central direction cannot be mixed. It sits uneasily with this chapter's own acceptance of price-controlled private monopoly (p. 200), which is neither market discipline nor direction of the whole economy. My observation: Hayek's practical position is a three-way scheme (competition; regulated natural monopoly; planning), but his rhetoric is two-way.
- Price control of monopolies (p. 200) also needs reconciling with ch. 6's Rule of Law objection to discretionary price-fixing and with ch. 3's remark that price controls are incompatible with competition; here the control applies only where competition is absent.
- "No reason to believe that this movement is inevitable" (p. 198) is consistent with the book's anti-historicism (ch. 4), yet the same sentence says continuing "will lead us to totalitarianism" and p. 198 says the state "cannot afford" to leave monopoly private. My observation: Hayek rejects inevitability of the first step while asserting something close to it for the later steps. Synthesis should decide whether this is the "slippery slope" his critics allege or a conditional prediction.
- The charge that Carr holds a doctrine of historical inevitability (p. 194) mirrors Hayek's own conditional inevitability; the difference is that Hayek's is conditional on policy choice.
- The complaint that Carr reinterprets liberty "in predominantly economic terms" (p. 194) links to ch. 2 (the "new freedom") and forward to ch. 14 on economic versus moral ends.
- Carr on propaganda (pp. 193-194) links to ch. 11; the scientists link to ch. 11's Crowther reference (p. 196; p. 205) and to Hayek's wider critique of scientism.
- Carr's "larger units under centralized planning" and Grossraumwirtschaft (pp. 194-195) set up ch. 15's attack on international planning.
- The footnote on men who "have tasted the powers of coercive control" in wartime (p. 206) links to ch. 14's discussion of the transition from war to peace and to ch. 10 on who rises under planning.
- The claim that a state running monopolies becomes "weak" in policy (p. 199) is a distinct argument from the usual "too strong" charge and anticipates later capture arguments; worth cross-referencing in thematic synthesis on state capacity.
- Tension with Hayek's stated method: he says he will show "how far this development has actually advanced" (p. 192) but offers two authors, one party resolution and a mechanism argument.

## Open questions

- How damaging are the editor's corrections to the Carr indictment? The society/state quotation (p. 203) is the basis of the Schmitt comparison; if Carr was reporting a belief, the "accidental or deliberate" insinuation (p. 193) fails. Synthesis should treat Hayek's quotations of opponents in this chapter as paraphrase-grade unless checked.
- Is "resemblance of ideas" evidence of a causal tendency, or is this the genetic fallacy? Test against ch. 12's method.
- Did the post-1945 Labour government's nationalizations and retained controls produce the predicted results? Hayek's prediction is conditional and undated ("if we continue") (p. 198); synthesis should state what would count as falsification, and compare with the 1956 and 1976 prefaces.
- What distinguishes an "inevitable" monopoly from a merely organized one, and who decides? Hayek gives hypothetical examples only (p. 199).
- Is stringent price control of a private monopoly (p. 200) administrable under the Rule of Law as defined in ch. 6, given that setting a price that "leaves no room for extraordinary profits" requires discretionary judgment?
- The claim that monopoly wages impoverish "still more all other wage-earners" (p. 199) is asserted without mechanism here; where, if anywhere, is it argued?
- Is the equation of English "organization"/planning with German ideas fair to non-German sources of English collectivism (Fabianism, Benthamite administration, wartime experience)? Hayek's own footnote dates the "German" atmosphere in England to men from the First World War controls (p. 206), which suggests a domestic, experiential source.
- How does Hayek's low alarm about the traditionalist Right (p. 201) square with the fact that the totalitarian regime under discussion came to power with conservative assistance?
