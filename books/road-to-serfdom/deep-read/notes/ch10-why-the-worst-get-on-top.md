# ch10: Why the Worst Get on Top (pp. 153–168)

## Question this chapter answers

Are the repellent features of existing totalitarian regimes a historical accident, due to the particular blackguards who set them up, so that the same system run by decent people would be benign? Hayek's answer: no. The worst features are products of the system itself, which both selects for unscrupulous people and generates a morality in which nothing is forbidden (pp. 154-155).

Epigraph: Acton, power tends to corrupt (p. 154; editor's source at p. 165, note 1).

## Leading propositions

- The belief that totalitarian horrors are accidental to the system consoles those who think it inevitable and weakens resistance; it is false (p. 154).
- The worst features are not accidental by-products but phenomena totalitarianism is certain sooner or later to produce (p. 154).
- As the democratic planner must choose between dictatorial powers and abandoning his plans, the dictator must choose between disregarding ordinary morals and failure; hence the unscrupulous succeed in a society tending toward totalitarianism (p. 154).
- The ethics a system produces need not be the ethics that motivated its creation (p. 155).
- Before democracy is suppressed, impatience with slow democratic procedure creates demand for a strong man or party organized on military lines; socialist parties in Central Europe had already familiarized the masses with such organization (p. 155).
- Socialism can be put into practice only by methods most socialists disapprove; the old socialist parties were inhibited by democratic ideals, and in Germany and Italy fascism's success was preceded by socialists' refusal to take over government (p. 156).
- A large, strong, homogeneous group is likely to be formed by the worst, not the best, elements, for three reasons: uniformity is found at lower moral and intellectual standards; the docile and gullible can be recruited; and agreement is easiest on a negative program against an enemy (pp. 156-157).
- Hayek's qualification: this does not mean the majority have low moral standards, only that the largest group with very similar values is one with low standards (p. 156).
- German anti-Semitism and anticapitalism spring from the same root (p. 157).
- Collectivism can exist only as some particularism (nationalism, racialism, classism); collectivism on a world scale is unthinkable except in the service of a small ruling élite (p. 158).
- Socialism is internationalist in theory and violently nationalist in practice; liberal socialism is purely theoretical; the practice of socialism is everywhere totalitarian (p. 158).
- The concepts of humanity and internationalism are products of the individualist view of man and have no place in collectivist thought (p. 159).
- Planners tend to be nationalists and imperialists; the Webbs and Shaw are the English example (pp. 159-160).
- Collectivists must create power over men of unprecedented magnitude; concentrating power does not transfer it but heightens it; the competitive system is the only one designed to minimize power of man over man (pp. 160-161).
- Substituting political for economic power substitutes inescapable power for limited power (p. 161).
- Collectivist ethics has no general rules and makes the end justify the means; individualist ethics, like formal law, consists of general absolute rules (pp. 161-162).
- The virtues cultivated under collectivism are those of the efficient instrument; the individualist virtues decline (pp. 162-163).
- The masses in totalitarian states are not devoid of moral fervor; their moral emotion is comparable to that of great religious movements (p. 163).
- Those who run the system, by contrast, must be personally committed to the leader and completely unprincipled; readiness to do bad things becomes a path to promotion (pp. 164-165).

## Concessions and qualifications

- An English or American fascism would differ greatly from the Italian or German; a non-violent transition might give a better type of leader; Hayek would rather live under one run by Englishmen or Americans (p. 154). But in the end it would not prove so very different (p. 154).
- There is nothing bad or dishonorable in approving a dictatorship of the good, though it might be unwise; not all good people are democrats (p. 154).
- The desire for collectivism springs from high moral motives (p. 155).
- The old socialist parties were genuinely democratic and reluctant to use force (p. 156).
- The lowest-common-denominator point is "probably true, in general" and is not a claim about the majority's morals (p. 156).
- Bertrand Russell is named as a contemporary socialist who has inherited the liberal tradition's view of power as the arch-evil (p. 160); so not all socialists are "strict collectivists".
- Economic power in private hands can be an instrument of coercion (p. 161).
- Rules of individualist ethics are imprecise in many respects (p. 161); we may sometimes be forced to choose between evils (p. 162).
- The Germans possess real virtues: industry, discipline, thoroughness, conscientiousness, sense of duty, self-sacrifice, physical courage (p. 162). The commercial towns of south and west Germany and the Hanse towns long shared Western moral concepts (p. 163).
- Mass supporters of National Socialism and communism act from often unselfish devotion to an ideal (pp. 163-164).
- War in the democracies produced only a very moderate approach to totalitarianism (p. 164).
- The subject is not exhausted here; selection by conformity of opinion leads to ch11 (p. 165).

## Arguments

- **A1. The selection argument (who forms the ruling group).**
  **Premises**: (p. 155) the demand is for a party able to do whatever it wants, which requires unreserved support of a disciplined body, not a mere majority + (p. 156) in a planned society the operative question is which is the largest single group agreeing sufficiently for unified direction + (p. 156) education and intelligence differentiate views, so large-scale uniformity exists only at lower standards + (p. 157) numbers must be swelled with the docile and gullible + (p. 157) cohesion is most easily got from hatred of an enemy and envy.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 156) such a group is likely to be formed by the worst rather than the best elements.
  **Unstated premises**: that a comprehensive plan really does require one homogeneous supporting group rather than a negotiated coalition (carried over from ch05); that agreement on a detailed hierarchy of values is what the group must share; that intellectual differentiation correlates with moral quality (he slides from "lower moral and intellectual standards" to "worst").

- **A2. The particularism argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 158) common aims presuppose more similarity than exists among men merely as human beings + (pp. 158-159) if the community has ends prior to the individual, only those serving those ends are members, and dignity derives from membership + (p. 158) no socialist seriously proposes equal division of capital among the world's peoples + (p. 159) group identification often springs from inferiority feelings and is satisfied by superiority over outsiders + (p. 159) outside contacts obstruct planning.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 158) collectivism in practice must be nationalist, racialist or classist; internationalist socialism is theory only.
  **Unstated premise**: that redistribution within a nation rests on exactly the same principle (exploitation) as would mandate world redistribution, so that stopping at the border is inconsistency rather than a difference of political obligation.

- **A3. The power argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 160) to achieve their ends collectivists must create power over men of unprecedented magnitude + (p. 160) power dispersed among many independent holders, once united in one body, is not the same sum but something far greater + (p. 161) private boards do not act in concert unless competition has ended + (p. 161) private economic power never extends over a person's whole life.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 161) decentralization reduces the absolute amount of power; the planning board's power would be without precedent and inescapable; the liberal socialist hope of extinguishing power by socializing it is a tragic illusion (p. 160).
  **Unstated premise**: that the relevant measure of power is the scope of what one will can consciously determine, not the degree to which individuals are constrained by impersonal market outcomes (his note 12 addresses this by definition, p. 168).

- **A4. The ethics argument.**
  **Premises**: (p. 161) collectivism needs a common system of ends and maximum power for the group + (p. 162) if the good of the whole is the only criterion, no act is excluded in advance + (pp. 161-162) individualist ethics consists of general rules forbidding types of act regardless of purpose.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 162) in collectivist ethics the end justifies the means necessarily becomes the supreme rule; (pp. 163-164) intolerance, suppression and disregard of individual life follow from the premise that the individual is a means.
  **Unstated premise**: that a society pursuing collective ends cannot also bind itself by side-constraints (rights); i.e. that "a few specific ends" must be all-overriding.

- **A5. The promotion argument (who runs the machine).**
  **Premises**: (p. 164) the leader alone sets ends, so instruments must have no moral convictions of their own + (p. 164) the posts offer little to attract people of traditional morals + (p. 165) there will be jobs bad in themselves that must be done efficiently, and the positions requiring cruelty and deception are numerous and are the route to the top.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 165) readiness to do bad things becomes a path to promotion and power.

- **Overall**: A1 + A5 (selection) and A2-A4 (the moral atmosphere) together yield the opening thesis that the worst features are systemic, not accidental (p. 154).

## Evidence offered

- **Mechanism arguments** throughout (A1-A5); these are the chapter's substance. The verbs are hedged at the start ("strong reasons for believing", "likely", "probably true") (pp. 154, 156) but harden to "certain sooner or later" (p. 154), "necessarily" and "inevitable" (pp. 162, 164). Confident language outruns evidence most at p. 158 (the practice of socialism is everywhere totalitarian), where the sample is Russia and Germany.
- **Historical example, asserted**: Central European socialist parties built semi-military organizations absorbing members' private lives (p. 155). No party or organization named here.
- **Historical example, asserted**: in Germany and Italy socialist parties refused to take over government before fascism succeeded (p. 156). No dates or episodes.
- **Historical-sociological explanation, asserted**: Jews in Germany and Austria identified with capitalism because excluded from esteemed occupations and admitted to despised commercial ones; the kulak as Russian parallel (p. 157). No source. A historian would want evidence on the actual content of Nazi anti-Semitism (racial, religious, anti-Bolshevik strands) which this single-root account omits; my observation.
- **Reductio / thought experiment**: Indians' claim to a proportional share of British capital (p. 158).
- **Quotation from a third party**: Niebuhr on men delegating their vices to larger groups, quoted at second hand from E. H. Carr (p. 159; p. 166, note 5; the editor traces the original 1927 article).
- **Quotation from a planning sympathizer**: the editor of the Planned Society symposium (MacKenzie) that most planners are militant nationalists (p. 159; p. 166, note 6).
- **Quotation, testimony about opponents**: Halévy on the Webbs and Shaw during the Boer War, and Shaw on big states (pp. 159-160; pp. 166-167, note 7). This is the chapter's main documentary evidence for British socialists' imperialism; it is one witness's recollection of forty years earlier plus one Shaw sentence.
- **Citation**: Marx and Engels on Czechs and Poles (p. 160; p. 167, note 8). **Editor's correction**: Revolution and Counter-revolution was written by Engels, though published under Marx's name, which is why Hayek cites Marx; the editor also supplies the text of Engels's letter of May 23, 1851 (p. 167, note 8).
- **Quotation from opponent, refuted**: Lippincott's claim that a planning board's power would be no greater than that collectively exercised by private boards of directors (pp. 160-161; pp. 167-168, note 11).
- **Authority**: Russell on the desire for power behind planning (p. 160; p. 167, note 10); Acton and Burckhardt (p. 160).
- **Hayek's footnote**: Nietzsche's Zarathustra on the one goal for humanity as being in the spirit of collectivism (p. 166, note 4; editor gives location).
- **National-character generalization**: virtues and deficiencies of the "typical Prussian" versus the English (pp. 162-163), introduced with "few people will deny" and "often thought to lack". Stereotype used as illustration, hedged but unsupported.
- **Contemporary examples**: shooting of hostages, killing of the old or sick, mass deportations, a proposal for conscription of women for breeding (p. 164). No sources.
- **Institutional examples**: Gestapo, concentration camps, Ministry of Propaganda, SA, SS and their Italian and Russian counterparts (p. 165).
- **Quotation from an ally**: Frank Knight's whipping-master analogy (p. 165; p. 168, note 14).
- **Editor's notes**: identify the Nazis named at p. 154 (pp. 165-166, note 2) and Burckhardt and Russell (p. 167, note 9).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch05** (Planning and Democracy): the claim that a plan requires agreement on a complete hierarchy of ends, which majorities cannot supply, is assumed at pp. 154 and 156 and is what makes a homogeneous group necessary.
- **Depends on ch06** (Rule of Law): the general-rules versus particular-ends contrast is explicitly transposed from law to ethics (p. 161). The formal structure of the book's liberalism (general, end-independent rules) is thus applied in three registers: law, economics, morals.
- **Depends on ch07-ch08**: separation of economic and political aims as a guaranty of freedom (p. 161, "we have seen before").
- **Reuses ch09**: commercial versus military type of society (p. 163; ch09 p. 148); Germany's organized, functionary society (ch09 p. 152) now supplies the "Prussian" virtues (pp. 162-163).
- **Feeds ch11**: selection by readiness to conform to changing doctrine and the fate of truthfulness (p. 165).
- **Feeds ch12**: the Webbs/Shaw passage is said to resemble the German ancestors of National Socialism (p. 160); anticapitalist roots of anti-Semitism (p. 157); Prussianism.
- **Feeds ch13 and ch15**: British planners' nationalism (pp. 159-160); the have-not nations and the problem of international order (p. 158).
- **Tension with ch09** (mine): ch09 allows a national guaranteed minimum; ch10 says no socialist will extend redistribution beyond the nation and treats that as inconsistency (p. 158). Hayek's own footnote in ch09 (p. 153, note 3) admits his minimum raises the same international problem.
- **Tension within the chapter** (mine): the mass of supporters are morally fervent idealists (p. 163), yet the supporting group is formed by "the worst elements" via lowest-common-denominator selection (p. 156). The two are reconciled only if "worst" means least independent rather than least moral; Hayek's text wavers.
- **Slide of terms** (mine): the chapter title and opening concern totalitarianism (p. 154); the argument at pp. 158-162 concerns collectivism and socialism generally. The inference from the claim that totalitarian regimes select the worst to the claim that socialist planning does so depends on the book's earlier claim that planning leads to totalitarianism; within this chapter it is carried by using the words interchangeably (p. 155).
- **Power**: here power is a non-conserved quantity (pp. 160-161); compare ch02 and ch07 where freedom-as-power is rejected as a confusion.

## Open questions

- Is the selection argument (A1) an empirical prediction or a conceptual claim? What would count against it: democratic socialist governments that planned extensively without the predicted personnel? Synthesis should test against Hayek's own later prefaces.
- Does A1 apply to any mass political movement (including liberal or nationalist-democratic ones), in which case it does not discriminate collectivism specifically?
- Is the account of collectivist ethics (pp. 161-162) a fair description of any actual socialist moral theory, or a construction from raison d'état? Which socialists held that the good of the whole is the only criterion?
- The treatment of anti-Semitism as derivative of anticapitalism (p. 157): how much does the book's thesis that Nazism is a species of socialism depend on it, and does ch12 give further support?
- Does the claim that humanity and internationalism are individualist concepts with no place in collectivism (p. 159) survive the history of socialist internationalism, or does Hayek's theory/practice distinction (p. 158) make the claim unfalsifiable?
- The power argument (A3) is among the most durable parts of the book. Does it address large private concentrations of economic power (monopoly, single-employer towns), which ch13 takes up?
- National-character claims about Germans (pp. 162-163) sit uneasily with Hayek's insistence elsewhere that the cause is ideas and institutions, not German nature. Which is doing the work?
