## Classification

- **Kind**: practical in aim (a warning addressed to a political public about what to avoid), carried by a theoretical argument about what central planning requires.
- **Genre**: political philosophy written as a wartime polemic, with two chapters of intellectual history (ch12, ch13) and an editor's scholarly apparatus. Read with the philosophy and practical-book strategies: dig for unstated premises, track shifting terms, separate positions from arguments, and flag confident language resting on thin evidence.

## The whole-book question

Can a free society direct its economic life by a central plan and keep democracy, the Rule of Law and personal freedom, and if it cannot, what should it do instead?

## Unity statement

Hayek argues that comprehensive economic planning requires a single ranking of everyone's ends that no free people shares, so the ranking must be imposed, and with it come discretionary power, the erosion of the Rule of Law, control of opinion and the rise of the unscrupulous. He presents this as a tendency that can be stopped, not a prediction, and offers Germany as the case where it was not stopped. The alternative he sketches is competition inside a deliberately designed legal framework, with security provided outside the market and a limited federation abroad.
