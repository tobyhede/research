# ch02: Supervenience and Explanation (pp. 37–91)

## Question this chapter answers

What framework is needed to ask whether consciousness is physical and whether it can be explained in physical terms? Chalmers builds one out of supervenience. His answer has four parts: (1) the relation that matters is logical supervenience, as distinct from mere natural supervenience (pp. 39-42); (2) a phenomenon is reductively explainable just when it supervenes logically on the physical (pp. 51-52); (3) logical necessity, conceptual truth, a priori knowability and conceivability line up once each concept is given two intensions, primary and secondary (pp. 58-73); and (4) almost every high-level fact supervenes logically on the physical, so that if consciousness fails to, it is a near-unique exception (pp. 73-91). The chapter argues for the framework and for the "almost everything" claim. It does not argue that consciousness fails to supervene; that is left to chapter 3 and used here only as illustration (p. 42, p. 54).

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Supervenience | p. 37 | A relation between two sets of properties in which one set of facts fully determines another. Template: no two possible situations alike in A-properties differ in B-properties (p. 38). Varieties come from what counts as a situation and which possibility is meant. |
| A-properties / B-properties | p. 37 | Low-level (base) and high-level (supervening) properties. A-facts and B-facts are instantiations of them (p. 38); the identity of the individual instantiating the property is not part of the fact (pp. 336-337, note 2). |
| Physical properties / physical facts | p. 37 | Restricted to the fundamental properties of a completed physics; high-level properties such as giraffehood are excluded. By stipulation the physical facts include the basic physical laws (p. 38). Sometimes called microphysical or low-level physical. |
| Local / global supervenience | p. 38 | Local: an individual's A-properties determine its B-properties. Global: the A-facts about a whole world determine its B-facts. Local implies global, not conversely (p. 38). |
| Logical (conceptual) supervenience | p. 39 | No two logically possible situations alike in A-properties differ in B-properties. Revised (p. 44, p. 45): for any logically possible world A-indiscernible from ours, the B-facts true of our world are true of it. Equivalently the A-facts entail the B-facts (p. 40). Unqualified, it means global logical supervenience (p. 43) and, by default, supervenience according to primary intension (p. 72). |
| Logical possibility | p. 39 | Possibility in the broadest sense, roughly conceivability, unconstrained by natural law; what God could have created. Broadly logical, not deducibility in a formal system (p. 39, p. 55). The logically possible world is treated as a primitive (p. 69). |
| Entailment | p. 40 | One fact entails another if it is logically impossible for the first to hold without the second (p. 40, p. 73). Not formal derivability. |
| Natural (nomic, empirical) supervenience | pp. 40-41 | Any two naturally possible situations alike in A-properties are alike in B-properties; a lawful, counterfactual-supporting correlation (p. 41). |
| Natural possibility | p. 41 | What could occur in nature without violating natural laws; a proper subset of logical possibility. Chalmers takes the intuitive notion as prior to the definition by laws (pp. 337-338, note 7). |
| Mere natural supervenience | p. 42 | Natural supervenience without logical supervenience. Example: gas pressure on temperature and volume. |
| Metaphysical supervenience | p. 42 | Supervenience under necessity *tout court*. Provisionally allowed; later identified with logical supervenience according to secondary intension (p. 72). |
| Weak supervenience | p. 42 | Within-world dependence only; dismissed as too weak (p. 42; p. 338, note 10). |
| Positive fact / positive property | p. 44 | A positive fact in W holds in every world containing W as a proper part; a positive property, if instantiated in W, is instantiated by the corresponding individual in every larger world. Negative facts involve negative existence claims (pp. 44-45). |
| Materialism (physicalism) | p. 45 | True if all the positive facts about the world are globally logically supervenient on the physical facts (pp. 45-46). Equivalent: any physically indiscernible world contains a copy of ours as a part (p. 46). |
| The second-order fact that the listed facts are all | p. 46 | A fact saying the basic particular facts are all there are; added to the base it yields the negative facts (p. 46, p. 88). |
| Reductive explanation | p. 46 | First by example: explanation wholly in terms of simpler entities (p. 46). Then explicated: a phenomenon is reductively explainable when the property of instantiating it supervenes globally and logically on lower-level properties (pp. 51-52). A mystery-removing explanation, not necessarily an illuminating one (p. 52). Distinct from reduction of a phenomenon and reduction of a theory (p. 47). |
| Functional analysis | p. 48 | Rough analysis of a concept in terms of performance of functions, taken causally and not teleologically. Causal-role concept: characterised by typical causes and effects (p. 49). |
| Explanatory gap | p. 51 | Levine's phrase for the gap between any functional or physical account and consciousness. |
| Autonomy and simplicity | p. 52 | Two contingent features of our physics: every physical event has a physical explanation, and the laws are reasonably simple (pp. 52-53). They are what let reductive explanations also be illuminating. |
| Explication / explanation | p. 55 | Two projects in a reductive explanation: the conceptual one of analysing what needs explaining, and the empirical one of showing how low-level facts satisfy the analysis. |
| Conceptual truth | p. 56 | A statement true in virtue of the meanings of its terms, where meaning is equated by stipulation with intension (p. 69). |
| Supervenience conditional | p. 56 | A conditional from a full low-level specification of a situation to its high-level facts. The only conceptual truths the arguments are said to need. |
| Intension | p. 57 | A function specifying how a concept applies to different situations (pp. 57-58); from worlds to extensions (p. 59). Replaces definition as the relevant sense of meaning. |
| Primary intension | p. 60 | Function from (centred) worlds to extensions reflecting how actual-world reference is fixed: what the concept picks out in a world considered as actual (p. 63). Independent of empirical factors, so a priori (p. 60, p. 61). |
| Secondary intension | p. 60 | What the concept picks out in a world considered as counterfactual, given how the actual world turned out. For rigid terms, the primary intension evaluated at the actual world and rigidified (p. 62). Not determined a priori. |
| Watery stuff | p. 60 | Term of art for whatever the primary intension of the water concept is (p. 61); roughly the dominant clear, drinkable liquid in the oceans and lakes. |
| *dthat* | p. 62 | Kaplan's rigidifying operator: turns an intension into a rigid designator by evaluation at the actual world. |
| Considered as actual / as counterfactual | p. 63 | Two ways of looking at one and the same space of worlds; matched to Kaplan's context of utterance and circumstance of evaluation. |
| Centred world | p. 63 | A world plus a marked individual and time; needed as the domain of primary intensions because of indexical elements (pp. 63-64). |
| Two-dimensional function F | p. 64 | Doubly indexed function from a centred world (as actual) and a world (as counterfactual) to a referent; determined a priori. Primary intension is its diagonal. |
| Primary / secondary proposition | pp. 66-67 | The two sets of truth conditions of a statement. Primary proposition: Stalnaker's diagonal proposition, a function from centred worlds. Secondary proposition: Kaplan's content (p. 67). |
| 1-necessity / 2-necessity | p. 68 | Truth in all worlds evaluated by primary or by secondary intensions. 1-necessity is Evans's deep necessity and coincides with apriority (p. 67, p. 72); 2-necessity is Kripkean, superficial necessity. |
| Conceivable (of a statement) | p. 69 | True in some conceivable world. Not the sense in which something is conceivable if not known to be impossible (pp. 69-70). 1- and 2-conceivability parallel 1- and 2-possibility (p. 70). |
| Modulo conscious experience (and indexicality) | p. 75 | Supervenience on the physical facts together with the phenomenal facts (and a centre marking an agent's location). |
| Structural / functional property | p. 81 | Structural: a condition on an object's intrinsic physical structure. Functional: a condition on its external causal role. |

## Leading propositions

- Supervenience formalises the idea that one set of facts fully determines another; the varieties differ by scope (individual or world) and by modality (logical or natural) (pp. 37-38).
- Insofar as consciousness supervenes on the physical at all, it supervenes locally, so the local/global distinction matters little for it (p. 39).
- Where B-properties supervene logically on A-properties, the A-facts entail the B-facts, and the B-facts are nothing over and above the A-facts (p. 40, p. 45).
- Mere natural supervenience is a contingent lawful connection between distinct features of the world; the B-facts are something over and above the A-facts (p. 42, p. 45).
- Logical supervenience must be stated as a thesis about our world and restricted to positive facts, to survive the possibility of physically identical worlds with extra nonphysical stuff (pp. 43-45).
- Materialism is the thesis that all positive facts about our world supervene globally and logically on the physical facts (pp. 45-46).
- Reductive explanation works by a rough analysis of the phenomenon, usually functional, plus an account of how lower-level mechanisms satisfy the analysis (p. 48).
- For psychological states no further question remains once the functions are explained; for phenomenal states one does, because they are not defined by causal role (pp. 50-51).
- A phenomenon is reductively explainable exactly when it supervenes logically on the physical; necessity is the part the later argument needs (pp. 51-52).
- The standard objections to conceptual truth (no definitions, revisability, a posteriori necessity) do not touch supervenience conditionals (pp. 56-58).
- Each concept has a primary intension, known a priori, and a secondary intension, which depends on how the actual world turned out; Kripkean a posteriori necessity is the case where the secondary but not the primary proposition is necessary (pp. 59-60, p. 67).
- The primary intension is what fixes what needs explaining, so logical supervenience according to primary intension is what decides reductive explainability (p. 60, p. 72).
- The difference between logical and metaphysical possibility is a difference in how statements are evaluated at worlds, not a difference between two spaces of worlds (pp. 70-71).
- Conceivability of a world implies its logical possibility (p. 71). 1-necessary truths are exactly the a priori truths (p. 72).
- Logical supervenience can be established or refuted by three routes: conceivability, epistemology, analysis (p. 73).
- All high-level facts about natural phenomena supervene logically on the microphysical facts plus laws, insofar as they are facts at all, modulo conscious experience and indexicality (pp. 74-75, p. 89).
- The exceptions are consciousness, perhaps a primitive indexical fact, negative facts, and laws and causation (pp. 87-89).
- Every supervenience relation of a high-level property on the physical is either logical (primary or secondary) or contingently natural; where neither holds there are no objective facts of that kind (p. 90).

## Arguments

**A. Logical supervenience means no further facts; natural supervenience means further facts (pp. 40-45)**

- **Premises**: (p. 40) Where logical supervenience holds, a being with the A-facts and the B-concepts could read off the B-facts; it is logically impossible for the A-facts to hold without the B-facts. (p. 42) Where supervenience is merely natural, God would have had more work to do after fixing the A-facts: he would have had to fix a law. (p. 45) A contingent lawful connection connects distinct features of the world.
- **Unstated premises**: that the God image tracks ontology, i.e. that whatever is not entailed by a base is an addition of being to that base, and whatever is entailed is not. That entailment by the base is sufficient for being nothing over and above it (a fact could be necessitated by a base yet be a distinct existent, if there were brute necessary connections; Chalmers defers that possibility to chapter 4, p. 42, p. 71).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 45) Logically supervenient facts may be different facts but are not further facts; merely naturally supervenient facts imply something new in the world.

**B. Repairing the definition against the angel world (pp. 43-45)**

- **Premises**: (p. 43) A world physically identical to ours with added angels and ectoplasm is logically possible and biologically different from ours. (p. 43) Weakening the modality (Haugeland, Petrie) destroys the logical/natural distinction; denying the possibility (Teller) is arbitrary. (p. 44) Biological facts are intuitively determined by physical facts in our world whatever happens in bizarre worlds. (p. 44) Negative facts depend on the world's limits and cannot be fixed by localised facts.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 44-45) Define logical supervenience as: every world A-indiscernible from ours makes true the B-facts true of ours; restrict to positive facts. Note 13 gives the precise form by an injection that must extend the A-mapping, with a countable-minds counterexample showing why the constraint is needed (pp. 338-339). Note 16 says the thesis should arguably be lawful, quantifying over naturally possible worlds, which also stops uninstantiated properties supervening vacuously (pp. 339-340).

**C. The definition of materialism (pp. 45-46)**

- **Premises**: (p. 45) Materialism is the doctrine that there is nothing over and above the physical. (p. 45) By argument A, nothing over and above the physical means logically supervenient on the physical. (p. 46) Negative existentials do not supervene, but their failure is compatible with materialism.
- **Unstated premises**: that logical possibility is the right modality for an ontological thesis. Chalmers flags this himself and lets objectors substitute metaphysical possibility, promising to show in chapter 4 that it comes to the same (p. 46).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 46) Materialism is true if all positive facts about our world are entailed by the physical facts; equivalently, every physically indiscernible world contains a copy of ours. The equivalence needs a further principle about proper parts (p. 340, note 18). Note 17 adds that a world free of ectoplasm in which ectoplasm is naturally possible should arguably count as non-materialist (p. 340).

**D. Reductive explanation requires analysis (pp. 47-51)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 47-48) An objection that a cellular account explains production of a similar entity but not reproduction would be met with impatience, because that is all the word means. (p. 48) Without an analysis there is no explanatory bridge from the lower-level facts to the phenomenon. (p. 48) The interesting concepts are functional; explaining how a function is performed explains the phenomenon. (p. 48) If the results of the functions are physically characterisable and all physical events have physical causes, a physical explanation of the performance exists.
- **Unstated premises**: that the analysis is knowable a priori, or at least in advance of the explanation (made explicit only for heat on p. 49 and generalised in section 4). That explanation of a phenomenon is fixed by the concept under which we ask for it, not by what the phenomenon turns out to be.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 48, p. 50) Phenomena whose concepts are functional are reductively explainable in principle; this covers the psychological concepts of chapter 1.

**E. The further-question argument, stated here as preview (pp. 50-51)**

- **Premises**: (p. 51) For learning, asking why the functioning is accompanied by learning gets a semantic answer. (p. 51) There is no corresponding analysis of consciousness; phenomenal states are not defined by causal role. (p. 51) Whatever functional account is given, it seems logically possible that it be instantiated without consciousness.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 51) If that is logically possible, any functional or physical account is fundamentally incomplete; there is an explanatory gap. Chalmers marks this as conditional and to be developed later (p. 51). Any mental concept with a phenomenal element inherits a partial gap (p. 51).

**F. Reductive explainability coincides with logical supervenience (pp. 51-54)**

- **Premises (necessity)**: (p. 52) If the property of exemplifying a phenomenon does not supervene logically on the lower-level properties, then for any lower-level account a further question is left: why is the process accompanied by the phenomenon? (p. 52) Reductive explanation needs an analysis whose realisation the low-level facts imply.
- **Premises (sufficiency)**: (p. 52) If P supervenes logically, an account of the low-level facts makes P's exemplification a logical consequence. (p. 52) Two worries: the low-level facts may be an arbitrary hotchpotch, and instances of P may have unlike bases. (p. 52) Reply: such accounts are still explanations that remove mystery, though not always illuminating. (pp. 52-53) In our world autonomy and simplicity of physics usually close the remaining gap; where they do not (perhaps sociology, economics), a reductive explanation exists in principle (p. 53).
- **Unstated premises**: that a further unanswered why-question is the mark of failed explanation, and that only an a priori entailment can close such a question (an a posteriori identity would not). That removing mystery is the right standard for explanation. Chalmers concedes the explication has "perhaps an element of stipulation" (p. 52).
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 51-52) Reductively explainable means logically supervenient on the physical. What matters most: if logical supervenience fails, every kind of reductive explanation fails (p. 54). He says necessity "is all that is required for my arguments about consciousness in the next chapter" (p. 52).
- **Corollaries**: reductive explanation is of particulars, not types (p. 53); it need not descend to microphysics, and the ladder of sciences "is little more than a pipe dream at the moment" (p. 54); a phenomenon can be explainable relative to a non-supervenient base without being explainable simpliciter (pp. 54-55); global, not local, supervenience is the requirement (p. 55).

**G. Supervenience conditionals survive the three objections to conceptual truth (pp. 56-58)**

- **Premises**: (p. 56) Objections: most concepts lack definitions (Wittgenstein); apparent conceptual truths are revisable (Quine); application conditions across worlds are not a priori (Kripke). (pp. 56-57) Entailment needs no definition: vague concepts still have clear cases, and for a clear case the conditional from the base description to the high-level fact is a conceptual truth; roundness of a curve given by an equation is the model. (p. 58) The antecedent of a supervenience conditional includes all relevant empirical factors, so evidence can refute the antecedent but not the conditional. (p. 58) If meaning determines an intension and worlds are finitely describable, there is a vast class of conceptually true conditionals.
- **Unstated premises**: that there is a fact of the matter, grasped by the concept's possessor, about how each concept applies to each fully specified world (p. 58 states it as a condition: as long as there is such a fact, there is an intension). That Quinean revisability has no purchase on what we would say about a completely specified scenario; note 28 handles the residue by ruling that purported conceptual truths that turn out false were never conceptual truths (pp. 342-343).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 56) The supervenience conditionals are untouched; they "are the only conceptual truths that my arguments need" (p. 56).

**H. The two-dimensional account of a posteriori necessity (pp. 58-68)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 58-59) Kripke: the statement that water is H2O is necessary but not knowable a priori. (p. 59) Reference in the actual world and reference in counterfactual worlds are determined by different mechanisms, so no single intension does all the work of meaning. (p. 60) Primary intension: if the actual world had XYZ in the oceans, the water concept would pick out XYZ; it maps each world, taken as actual, to what the concept would then refer to. (p. 60, p. 62) Secondary intension: given that the watery stuff is actually H2O, the concept picks out H2O in every counterfactual world; it is the primary intension evaluated at the actual world and rigidified. (p. 61) Working out the primary intension may take much reflection about cases but is a priori, since we can reason about what a term would refer to whichever way the world turns out. (pp. 62-63) We could not know what our concepts refer to given how the world is unless we could know what they would refer to if it were various ways. (pp. 63-64) Primary intensions take centred worlds as arguments because of an indexical element. (p. 64) Both intensions derive from one a priori two-place function F; they agree at the actual world.
- **Unstated premises**: that the conditional ability to assign reference under hypotheses about the actual world is a feature of the concept and not itself revisable by experience. That primary intensions are stable enough across speakers; Chalmers sets this aside by idealising to one speaker or an equally informed community (p. 61). That reference-fixing belongs to meaning; note 25 concedes Kaplan puts it in metasemantics and says the label does not matter (p. 342).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 67) A statement has a primary and a secondary proposition. A posteriori necessity arises exactly when the secondary proposition is necessary and the primary is not; the contingent a priori (water is watery stuff) is the reverse case. (pp. 67-68) Puzzles from Kripke come from forcing a two-dimensional picture into one notion of meaning or necessity.
- **Replies built in**: the framework is not a description theory, since the function and not any summarising description is what counts, and it is compatible with a causal theory, which is itself reached by evaluating primary intensions at scenarios (pp. 61-62). Note 45 uses the same move for names: Kripke's own Gödel cases proceed by evaluating a name's primary intension (p. 345).

**I. Conceivability, possibility and apriority (pp. 68-72)**

- **Premises**: (p. 68) A statement is logically necessary iff true in all logically possible worlds; there are two readings, by primary or secondary intension. (p. 69) Logically possible worlds are taken as a primitive tool; every conceivable world is logically possible. (p. 69) With meaning stipulated to be intension, conceptual truth and necessary truth coincide on each reading. (pp. 69-70) A statement is conceivable if true in some conceivable world, so a conceivability judgment needs both a conceived world and a correct description of it. (p. 70) The alleged counterexamples divide: in the Kripkean case a conceived world is misdescribed (described by primary intension when the secondary is relevant); in the Goldbach cases either the world is misdescribed or no world is conceived at all (p. 71). (p. 71) Hence no case shows a conceivable world to be impossible.
- **Unstated premises**: that the listed cases exhaust the reasons for doubting that conceivable worlds are possible (he says any other reason would have to be quite independent of the standard ones, p. 71, and postpones a stronger metaphysical modality to chapter 4). That we can tell real conceivability of a world from apparent conceivability; note 32 admits the defeasibility removed from the definition "reappears in the form of a larger gap between what one finds conceivable at first glance and what is really conceivable" (p. 344). That worlds are individuated prior to language, as distributions of basic qualities (p. 343, note 30).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 70) 1-conceivability implies 1-possibility and 2-conceivability implies 2-possibility. (pp. 70-71) The logical/metaphysical distinction is at the level of statements; the space of worlds is the same. (p. 71) Matters of 1-possibility are accessible from the armchair in principle. (p. 72) The 1-necessary truths are the a priori truths.
- **Application given in the notes**: Descartes's disembodiment argument moves from 1-conceivability to a conclusion needing 2-possibility (p. 344, note 33). Metaphysical necessity is conceptual necessity with an a posteriori semantic twist (p. 344, note 34).

**J. Primary intensions govern explanation; equivalent formulations (pp. 72-73)**

- **Premises**: (p. 72) At the start of inquiry only the primary intension is available, and it sets the test of a satisfactory explanation; the secondary intension emerges only after the explanation is done. (p. 73) Holding one kind of intension fixed, logical supervenience can be stated as: entailment of B-facts by A-facts; no conceivable world with the same A-properties and different B-properties; truth of the supervenience conditional in virtue of intensions. (p. 73) On the primary reading the implication is a priori, so a knower of the A-facts with the B-concepts could work out the B-facts.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 72) "It is logical supervenience according to a primary intension that determines whether reductive explanation is possible." (p. 72) (p. 73) Three routes to establish or refute logical supervenience: conceivability, epistemology, analysis. He will run each main argument by all three so that nothing rests on equivocation (p. 73).

**K. Almost everything supervenes logically: conceivability (pp. 75-76)**

- **Premises**: (p. 75) Fix every microphysical fact, including those about a wombat, its children, their environments and histories; ask what a world with those facts but without the wombat having two children could differ in. (p. 76) Fixing microphysics fixes macroscopic structure and dynamics, leaving no room for biological, economic, meteorological or other facts to vary. (p. 76) The inconceivability is in principle, not a limit of our capacity.
- **Unstated premise**: that inconceivability in principle implies impossibility (the converse direction of argument I, which Chalmers called trickier and plausible only for an idealised conceiver, p. 71).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 76) These high-level properties supervene logically on the physical.

**L. Almost everything supervenes logically: epistemology (pp. 76-79)**

- **Premises**: (p. 76) If a physically identical but biologically different world were possible, all external evidence would be the same in both, so we could not know the biological facts from external evidence. (p. 76) There is no special sceptical problem about biology, architecture, economics or meteorology. (p. 77) Where there is a sceptical problem there is a failure of logical supervenience, and conversely: other minds (consciousness), causation and laws (Hume), and, earlier in the order, the external world and theoretical entities relative to experience and observation (pp. 77-78). (p. 78) All external evidence supervenes on microphysical facts, so external evidence gives no reason to believe in anything that does not. (pp. 78-79) Direct version: a superbeing with the microphysical facts and the concepts could simulate the world and read off that a man is carrying an umbrella.
- **Unstated premises**: that knowledge of high-level facts from physical evidence is not itself an inference to the best explanation, although Chalmers allows exactly that for causation, laws, the external world and electrons (pp. 77-78). That abduction from microphysical facts stops at fundamental laws (p. 78, asserted). That the absence of a recognised sceptical problem is good evidence of entailment and not just of settled practice.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 78) Modulo conscious experience, all phenomena are logically supervenient on the physical. Our own consciousness escapes the sceptical consequence because it is at the centre of our epistemic universe and known without external evidence (p. 77).

**M. Almost everything supervenes logically: analysability (pp. 79-83)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 79-80) If microphysical facts entail a high-level fact, they must fix the features in virtue of which the high-level intension applies. (p. 80) Indeterminacy in an intension mirrors indeterminacy in the facts; short analyses always fail but rough ones suffice to show the kind of property involved. (p. 81) High-level intensions are characterisable structurally, functionally, or both. Structural properties are entailed by microphysics; functional properties are too, given a wide base and laws to fix counterfactuals (pp. 81-82). (p. 82) Interdefined functional roles can be cashed out together provided they bottom out in structural or phenomenal properties (Ramsey-sentence method, p. 344, note 38). (p. 81) Both intensions of the water concept supervene; rigidification makes no ontological difference.
- **Unstated premise**: that the rough analyses given (table, life, prosperity) are representative of all high-level concepts, and that the residue left out of each is of the same functional or structural kind.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 83) Most high-level concepts are analysable to the extent that their intensions specify functional or structural properties; in virtue of this, high-level facts are derivable in principle from microphysical facts. Chalmers also argues in the other direction: if the physical facts fix the high-level facts, we should expect such analyses to exist (p. 83).

**N. The problem cases introduce no new failure comparable to consciousness (pp. 83-89)**

- Consciousness-dependent properties (redness, heat, light, sound): the primary intension involves a relation to experience and fails to supervene on the physical alone; the secondary intension supervenes. Reductive explanation fails in a weak sense; explanation modulo experience is accepted in practice (pp. 83-84). Love, belief on some accounts, standing next to a conscious person: fail even a posteriori, but supervene modulo experience (p. 84).
- Intentionality: any failure derives from consciousness; no conceivable world physically and phenomenally identical to ours differs in intentional content (pp. 84-85). If the Kripke-Wittgenstein argument against entailment were right, the conclusion would be that there are strictly no intentional facts, not that they are further facts (p. 345, note 43).
- Moral and aesthetic properties: no naturally identical, morally distinct world is conceivable, and moral facts are not forced on us, so the options are antirealism or an a priori link from natural to moral facts. A posteriori moral realism (Boyd, Brink) is hard to maintain because a posteriori equivalences rest on a priori reference fixation (pp. 85-86).
- Names: each use has a primary intension even if none is shared; in any case being a named individual is a matter for explication, not explanation (pp. 86-87).
- Indexicals: for arbitrary speakers the indexical fact can be relativised away; for myself, that a given person is me may be a primitive fact, a failure of reductive explanation distinct from consciousness but thin (p. 87).
- Negative facts: not entailed by any particular facts; handled by one second-order fact; not a serious failure (p. 88). Note 47 checks that all three arguments (K, L, M) fail for them (pp. 345-346).
- Laws and causation: do not supervene on particular physical facts, shown by a world with our history and one extra law about gold that is never triggered (p. 88). Bypassed by putting laws in the base, which "steps over the metaphysical puzzle rather than answering it" (pp. 88-89). Note 48 checks that K, L and M all fail for laws (p. 346).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 89) The world's facts are exhausted by particular physical facts, facts about conscious experience, laws of nature, a second-order totality fact, and perhaps an indexical fact.

**O. Explaining supervenience relations themselves (pp. 90-91)**

- **Premises**: (p. 90) A logical supervenience relation by primary intension is explained by analysis of the concept; the conditional is an a priori conceptual truth. (p. 91) One by secondary intension is explained by a priori analysis plus contingent facts about the actual world. (p. 91) A mere natural supervenience relation is a contingent law, explainable at best by more fundamental laws.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 91) Mere natural supervenience is ontologically expensive; it is the exception.

**Position versus argument.** The position: reductive explainability is logical supervenience by primary intension, and nearly everything has it (p. 72, p. 89). The arguments: D and F for the first half, K, L and M for the second. H and I are defensive: they are there so that Kripkean a posteriori necessity cannot be used to block the move from conceivability to possibility. Note 22 says outright that the same points could have been made by speaking of watery stuff throughout and skipping the secondary intension, and that Chalmers "was tempted to do so" (p. 341).

## Evidence offered

- **Thought experiments and images**: God creating the world and what comes for free (p. 39, p. 42, p. 45, p. 89), borrowed from Kripke (p. 42), with the caveat that God could not create a world not created by God (p. 337, note 5). Laplace's demon reading off biology (p. 40). The Mona Lisa and its replica (p. 38). The angel and ectoplasm world, with the wombat that is childless here and not there (pp. 43-44). Twin Earth and XYZ (pp. 59-62). Goldbach's conjecture (p. 70). The wombat with two children (p. 75). The man with the umbrella (p. 79). The 200 tons of gold in a vacuum (p. 88). The creation myth and the epistemological myth (p. 89).
- **Scientific illustrations**: the ideal gas law as natural without logical supervenience, with the admission that he pretends all gases are ideal (p. 40); a cubic mile of gold against a cubic mile of uranium-235 (p. 41); reproduction, adaptation, life, heat, phases of the moon, earthquakes, learning as reductively explained phenomena (pp. 46-47); connectionist models as how-possibly explanations (p. 50); mass under relativity as a case for primary intensions (p. 61; pp. 341-342, note 24).
- **Rough conceptual analyses**: reproduction (p. 48), learning (p. 48), heat (p. 49), life (p. 56, p. 82), table (p. 80, p. 81), economic prosperity in the 1950s, a puzzle case from Ned Block (pp. 82-83; p. 344, note 39). Chalmers calls the prosperity analyses "ridiculously oversimplified" (p. 83).
- **Figures**: 2.1, crisp against vague dependence of a property on two parameters (p. 57); 2.2, a round curve given by an equation and a non-round one (pp. 57-58).
- **Authors drawn on**: Kripke, Putnam, Kaplan, Stalnaker, Lewis, Evans, Davies and Humberstone for the two-dimensional framework (p. 59); Frege for sense as intension (p. 59); Quine and Lewis for centred worlds (p. 63); Levine for the explanatory gap (p. 51); Jackson for the definition of physicalism (p. 46); Lewis, Dennett, Dretske, Fodor for functional accounts of intentionality, none "entirely compelling" (p. 85); Hume on causation and induction (pp. 77-78); Nagel and Searle on explaining heat without heat sensations (p. 84). Allies on the ubiquity of logical supervenience: Horgan, Jackson, Kirk, Lewis (p. 344, note 35), and Armstrong, Nagel (p. 346, note 50). The source prints Horgan as Morgan in note 35.
- **Authors opposed**: Haugeland and Petrie (modality too strong), Teller (extra-stuff worlds impossible) (p. 43); Quine and Wittgenstein on conceptual truth (p. 56); Moore on moral intuition, Boyd and Brink on a posteriori moral realism (p. 86); Humeans about laws, whom he says "have the worse of the arguments here" (p. 88), with the literature in note 49 (p. 346); Seager, Hellman and Thompson, Bacon, Heil on supervenience not being logical (p. 90). The British emergentists are cited as describing a possible but non-actual kind of world (p. 53).
- **History of the notion**: Moore introduced the idea, Hare the name, Davidson the application to mind and body; Kim, Horgan and others the theory (p. 336, note 1).
- **Assertion without argument (my reading)**: that consciousness supervenes locally if at all (p. 39); that it supervenes naturally (p. 42, very likely); that causation is more than regularity, "as I will assume it must be" (p. 77); that explanation stops at fundamental laws (p. 78); that the two epistemological problems exhaust those arising from failure of supervenience on the physical (p. 78).

## Concessions and qualifications

- The non-supervenience of consciousness is not argued here: "I use it for now only as an illustration" (p. 42), and it seems logically possible "at least to many" (p. 42).
- The identification of reductive explanation with logical supervenience has an element of stipulation, and sufficiency is "somewhat less clear" (p. 52).
- Reductive explanation is not the only or always the best kind of explanation; historical and high-level explanations have their place (p. 47).
- Explaining heat leaves its effect on experience unexplained; this is somewhat paradoxical but harmless in practice (pp. 49-50).
- He does not claim that high-level facts follow from microphysical laws, or laws plus boundary conditions; on that "the evidence is not yet in" (p. 74). The claim is only about the totality of microphysical facts.
- He is not offering a full semantic theory, and meaning is used as an honorific (p. 61, p. 65); the equation of meaning with intension is stipulative (p. 69).
- The implication from possibility to conceivability holds only for an idealised conceiver (p. 71).
- Some 1-necessary truths (certain mathematical or very complex statements) may be unknowable by us and are a priori only in principle (p. 72).
- Facts about abstract entities are set aside (p. 74).
- Establishing the main thesis conclusively would need a more detailed examination of all kinds of phenomena (p. 89).
- Not everyone accepts that water is necessarily H2O; that it is easy to be swayed both ways suggests little turns on the secondary intension (p. 341, note 22).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch01**: the claim that psychological concepts are functionally analysable and phenomenal ones are not is imported from the psychological/phenomenal distinction (p. 50, p. 51). The claim that no world physically and phenomenally identical to ours differs in intentional content is also referred back to chapter 1 (p. 84).
- **Feeds ch03**: the argument that consciousness is not logically supervenient is promised for the next chapter (p. 54, p. 73) and will reuse the three routes of p. 73. Argument F supplies the bridge from that result to the failure of reductive explanation; only its necessity half is needed (p. 52).
- **Feeds ch04**: the definition of materialism is to be justified further there (p. 46); the claim that metaphysically possible worlds are just the logically possible worlds is asserted here and argued there (p. 42, p. 71); so is the claim that there is no variety of supervenience between the logical and the natural (p. 90).
- **Feeds later chapters on psychophysical laws**: mere natural supervenience as a contingent law that may be fundamental (p. 91), and the creation myth's laws relating physics to conscious experience (p. 89), set up the positive theory. The remark that consciousness and causation, the two non-supervenient kinds, may be metaphysically related (p. 89) is a speculation to track.
- **Term shift: logical supervenience (my observation)**: defined first over all pairs of logically possible situations (p. 39), then as a thesis about our world and positive facts (pp. 44-45), then arguably over all naturally possible worlds (pp. 339-340, note 16), then split into primary and secondary versions with primary as the default (p. 72). The main text keeps the middle version; the later chapters should be read with the default of p. 72 in mind.
- **Term shift: physical facts (my observation)**: stipulated to include laws (p. 38), but in the problem cases the physical facts are the particular facts of a world's history and laws do not supervene on them (p. 88). The headline thesis is supervenience on physical facts including laws (p. 89). So the base against which materialism is defined already contains something Chalmers calls irreducible (p. 88).
- **Term shift: conceivable (my observation)**: glossed early as roughly what logical possibility corresponds to (p. 39), later stipulated so that a statement is conceivable only if true in a conceivable world correctly described (pp. 69-70). On the later sense the conceivability-possibility link is close to definitional for statements, and the work is done by the claim about worlds (p. 71) and by our ability to tell real from apparent conceivability (p. 344, note 32).
- **Term shift: reductive explanation (my observation)**: introduced as explanation wholly in terms of simpler entities that makes mystery go away (p. 46), then explicated as logical supervenience even where the result is unilluminating and graspable only by a superbeing (p. 53). The weak sense is the one in force when he says almost everything is reductively explainable (p. 90).
- **Internal tension (my observation)**: the epistemological argument treats inference to the best explanation as enough for knowledge of causation, laws, the external world and theoretical entities while holding that these do not supervene on the evidence (pp. 77-78). It then infers logical supervenience of biology from the absence of a sceptical problem (p. 76). A defender of non-supervenient biological facts could say they too are known abductively. Chalmers's reply is that abduction from microphysics ends at laws (p. 78), which is asserted.
- **Internal tension (my observation)**: indexicality is treated as harmless (p. 75), then as a possible primitive fact and a failure of reductive explanation analogous to consciousness (p. 87), then doubted because God could not fix it (pp. 89-90). Its status is left open.
- **Internal tension (my observation)**: heat is a paradigm of successful reductive explanation (p. 47, p. 49) and also a case where the primary intension fails to supervene on the physical and explanation fails in a weak sense (p. 84). The two are reconciled only by the modulo-experience qualifier.

## Open questions

- Is the link from conceivable worlds to possible worlds (p. 71) an argument or a stipulation about what logical possibility means (p. 69)? If the latter, the substantive question becomes whether logical possibility so defined is the modality relevant to materialism, which is chapter 4's burden (p. 46).
- Can a materialist accept everything in sections 1-3 and deny that explanation requires a priori entailment, holding that an a posteriori identity closes the gap? Argument F rules this out through the further-question premise (p. 52); check how chapter 3 and chapter 4 defend it.
- Are primary intensions really a priori and determinate enough? The text idealises away deference and variation between speakers (p. 61) and allows indeterminacy and divided reference (p. 341, note 23). How much indeterminacy can the supervenience conditionals bear before the claim on p. 56 weakens?
- The analysability argument rests on a few admittedly crude analyses (p. 83) and on the expectation that analyses must exist if the conceivability argument is sound (p. 83). Is M independent support, or a restatement of K?
- Laws and causation are irreducible further facts (pp. 88-89) yet sit inside the physical base. What does that do to the claim that only consciousness threatens materialism?
- Does the treatment of moral facts (pp. 85-86) generalise? The rule on p. 90 says that where neither logical nor natural supervenience holds there are no objective facts. Why is the same eliminative option closed for consciousness other than by the claim that its existence is forced on us (p. 85)?
- Note 16's lawful version of logical supervenience and note 17's stronger materialism (pp. 339-340) are said to make no difference. Do the zombie and related arguments of chapter 3 go through unchanged under the stronger definitions?
- The speculation linking consciousness and causation (p. 89): is it taken up in the later chapters?
