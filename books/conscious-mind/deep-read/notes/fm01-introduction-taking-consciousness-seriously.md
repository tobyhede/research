# fm01: Introduction: Taking Consciousness Seriously (pp. 3–10)

The chapter file opens with the tail of the acknowledgments and the table of contents (p. 3); the Introduction proper starts at the foot of p. 3 and ends on p. 10, where the Part I epigraph and the opening of Chapter 1 begin. There are no endnotes to the Introduction.

## Question this chapter answers

What is the problem of consciousness, what constraints does Chalmers set himself in addressing it, and what does the book claim? His answer: the problem is why physical processing is accompanied by experience at all; he will assume that experience exists and is not to be redefined as a function, stay compatible with science, and treat consciousness as lawful; and the book's claim is conditional: anyone who accepts the first constraint should end at his position, a nonreductive, dualist but naturalistic theory.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Easy problems | p. 4 | Questions about how the brain processes stimulation, integrates information and produces reports on internal states. Important, but answering them does not solve the hard problem. Always in scare quotes here. |
| Hard problem | p. 4 | Why the processing is accompanied by an experienced inner life. |
| Taking consciousness seriously | p. 5 | Assuming that consciousness exists and refusing to redefine the problem as that of explaining how cognitive or behavioural functions are performed. The first and most important constraint. |
| Taking science seriously | p. 6 | Not disputing current scientific theories where they have authority (causal closure of the physical, physical explanation of behaviour), while treating claims that consciousness can be physically explained as hopes, not results. |
| Natural phenomenon | p. 6 | Something falling under natural laws. The laws for consciousness need not be physical laws and may differ in kind. |
| Great Divide | p. 5 | The split between those who hold that answering the easy problems explains everything and those who hold there is a further problem. |
| Reductive explanation | p. 6 | Not defined here; named as what he will argue is impossible for consciousness. Defined in ch02. |
| Dualism | p. 6 | Not defined here; he says he argues for a form of it. Developed in ch04. |
| Supervenience | p. 9 | The one technical concept he calls crucial; preferred to identity as the frame for the mind-body question. What matters is the strength of the connection: logical necessity, natural necessity, or something else. Defined in ch02. |
| Organizational invariant | p. 8 | Preview only: every system with the right functional organization has the same sort of experience whatever it is made of. Argued in ch07. |
| Strong artificial intelligence | p. 8 | Preview only: implementing an appropriate program gives rise to a conscious mind. Argued in ch09. |

## Leading propositions

- Consciousness may be the largest outstanding obstacle to a scientific understanding of the universe; unlike the gaps in physics and biology, we lack even a sense of what a solution would look like (pp. 3-4).
- Most recent work addresses the easy problems and leaves the hard problem untouched (p. 4).
- A theory of consciousness is possible, and the book looks for one; it does not claim to solve the problem (p. 4).
- First constraint: consciousness exists, and redefining the problem as one about functions is unacceptable (p. 5).
- The existence of a further problem cannot be proved, because the existence of consciousness cannot be proved; it is known more directly than anything else (p. 5).
- The argument rests on intuition at some point, as all arguments do (p. 5).
- There is a Great Divide; past a point it cannot be argued across. The book is addressed to those on the side that feels the problem (p. 5).
- The book's real argument is conditional: if one takes consciousness seriously, one should end at his position (p. 6).
- Second constraint: the physical world is causally closed and behaviour is physically explicable; neither is disputed (p. 6).
- Third constraint: consciousness is a natural phenomenon governed by natural laws, so there should be a correct scientific theory of it, whether or not we can reach it (p. 6).
- The problem is scientific in subject matter but needs philosophical methods first, because outside the first-person case data are hard to come by (p. 6).
- Reductive explanation of consciousness is impossible and a form of dualism is true; this broadens the scientific picture and overturns nothing in it (pp. 6-7).
- The conclusions were reached against his own materialist temperament (p. 7).
- Supervenience, and the modal strength of the supervenience relation, is the right frame; identity talk obscures the issues (p. 9).

## Arguments

The Introduction mostly states positions and constraints. Two short arguments are given.

**A. That there is something further to explain (pp. 4-5)**

- **Premises**: (p. 5) We are surer of the existence of conscious experience than of anything else. (p. 5) After the processes of discrimination and action have been explained, something is still going on: the experience. (p. 4) The easy problems concern processing, integration and report.
- **Unstated premises**: that introspective certainty about the existence of experience carries over to certainty that experience is not itself one of the functions already explained; that the sense of a remainder is evidence about the world and not only about our concepts.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 4-5) Answering the easy problems does not answer the hard problem.
- He concedes this is not a proof and involves an appeal to intuition (p. 5).

**B. That consciousness is lawful and so admits of a theory (p. 6)**

- **Premises**: (p. 6) Consciousness is a salient part of nature, arising throughout the human species and probably others. (p. 6) Natural phenomena are subject to fundamental natural laws.
- **Unstated premise**: that the laws covering consciousness are simple and general enough to be stated in a theory; lawfulness alone does not give that.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 6) There should be a correct scientific theory of consciousness, whether or not we can arrive at it.

**Position versus argument.** What he holds: reductive explanation fails and a form of dualism is true (p. 6). What he offers for it here: nothing beyond argument A and the autobiographical report that he gave up materialism reluctantly (p. 7). The arguments are deferred to ch03 and ch04.

## Evidence offered

- **First-person report**: absorption in an orange sensation, and failed attempts to convince himself that experience is an illusion (p. 5).
- **Informal surveys**: two or three to one find it obvious that something further needs explaining, with the ratio said to be constant across academics and students in several fields (p. 5). No source, sample or wording is given.
- **Autobiography**: temperamentally inclined to materialist reductive explanation, no religious inclination, hoped for years for a materialist theory (p. 7). Offered to show the conclusions are forced and not wished for. It is testimony about motive, not support for the conclusions (my comment).
- **Analogy**: consciousness is a natural phenomenon like motion, life and cognition and calls for explanation as they do (p. 6).
- **Chapter plan** (pp. 7-8): ch01 separates concepts; ch02 builds the supervenience framework; ch03 argues reductive explanation fails; ch04 argues materialism is false; ch05 is defensive, on judgments about consciousness; ch06 coherence between consciousness and cognition; ch07 organizational invariance; ch08 information, the most speculative; ch09 strong AI; ch10 a no-collapse interpretation of quantum mechanics.
- **Reading guide** (pp. 8-9): the most technical sections are ch02 section 4, ch04 sections 2 and 3, and the final section of ch05. Short tour: ch01, early ch02, all of ch03, first and last sections of ch04, start of ch06, then ch07.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Sets the book's logical form.** The thesis is conditional on the first constraint (p. 6). A reader who rejects the constraint is told the book is not really for them (p. 5). Chapters that claim more than the conditional (ch03 and ch04, by the Introduction's own description on pp. 7-8) should be checked for whether they argue for the antecedent or assume it.
- **Causal closure plus dualism (my observation).** Accepting that the physical is causally closed and that behaviour is physically explained (p. 6), while holding that consciousness is not physical (p. 6), leaves no evident causal work for consciousness. The Introduction does not mention this. The chapter plan assigns the resulting problem, about judgments about consciousness, to ch05 (p. 8), and the contents list has a ch04 section asking whether the view is epiphenomenalism (p. 3).
- **First constraint versus definition (my observation).** The constraint rules out redefining the problem in functional terms (p. 5). ch01 then argues that a functional analysis of phenomenal concepts is false (ch01, p. 21). Whether ch01 supplies an argument or restates the constraint is the point to test.
- **Supervenience over identity** (p. 9): explains why ch02 comes before any argument about consciousness, and why the later arguments are modal. He grants that sceptics about modal notions will be sceptical of the whole discussion (pp. 9-10).
- **Parts III and IV depend on Part II.** The positive theory is said to be made possible by the broadening argued in ch03 and ch04 (p. 7). ch09 and ch10 apply what came before (p. 8).
- **Naturalism.** The third constraint (p. 6) is what the label naturalistic dualism, the title of ch04 (p. 3), later draws on.

## Open questions

- Is the conditional form of the thesis (p. 6) kept throughout, or do later chapters assert the consequent outright?
- The admission that the Divide cannot be argued across past a point (p. 5) sits beside a book of arguments. Which later arguments are meant to move someone on the other side, and which only to organise the view for those already convinced?
- What would count as evidence for a psychophysical law if data outside the first-person case are hard to come by (p. 6)? Check ch06 and ch08.
- The survey claim (p. 5) is unsourced. Nothing depends on it logically, but it is used to suggest that the intuition is the majority view.
- He says the laws of consciousness may be different in kind from physical laws (p. 6). In what sense is a theory built on such laws still scientific by his own second constraint?
