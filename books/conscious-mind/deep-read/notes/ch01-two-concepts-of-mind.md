# ch01: Two Concepts of Mind (pp. 10–37)

Endnotes for this chapter are at pp. 334-336 and are cited by their own pages.

## Question this chapter answers

What is the subject matter of a theory of consciousness, and how does it relate to the rest of the mind? Chalmers's answer: there are two concepts of mind, the phenomenal (how a state feels) and the psychological (what a state does). Together they exhaust the mental. Most mental terms, including the word consciousness, carry both senses. The psychological side poses only technical problems; the phenomenal side is the hard part of the mind-body problem, and explanations offered for consciousness usually explain a psychological property (awareness) and leave experience untouched.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consciousness (central sense) | pp. 11, 13 | The subjective quality of experience; what it is like to be a cognitive agent. Not definable in more primitive terms, only characterised by examples at the same level (p. 11). After p. 37 the bare word means phenomenal consciousness only. |
| Conscious (of a being, of a state) | p. 11 | A being is conscious if there is something it is like to be it; a mental state is conscious if there is something it is like to be in it, that is, if it has a qualitative feel. Phrase credited to Nagel; note 1 traces it to Farrell 1950 (p. 334). |
| Qualia / phenomenal qualities | p. 11 | Qualitative feels. Note 2: "those properties of mental states that type those states by what it is like to have them" (p. 334), with no commitment on incorrigibility or on whether they are intentional. |
| Experience, phenomenology, subjective experience, what it is like | p. 13 | Treated as picking out approximately the same class of phenomena as consciousness in the central sense; differences are matters of connotation. |
| Red sensation, green experience | p. 12 | Note 3: shorthand for experience of the type usually had, in the actual world, when looking at red or green objects; no claim that experiences have the colours objects have (p. 334). |
| Phenomenal concept of mind | p. 17 | Mind as conscious experience; a mental state as a consciously experienced state. Mind characterised by the way it feels (p. 18). |
| Psychological concept of mind | p. 18 | Mind as the causal or explanatory basis of behaviour; a state is mental if it plays the right causal role in producing behaviour or an appropriate role in explaining it. A stipulated usage, narrower than the everyday one (p. 18). |
| Functionalism (analytic) | p. 21 | The Lewis-Armstrong view that a mental state is defined wholly by its causal role: what tends to produce it, the behaviour it tends to produce, its interaction with other states. Note 8 excludes Putnam-style functionalism because it was offered as an empirical hypothesis, not conceptual analysis (p. 335). Said to correspond precisely to his definition of psychological properties (p. 22). |
| Logical behaviorism | p. 20 | Ryle's view, as commonly read, that all mental concepts are analysable in terms of behaviour or dispositions to behave. Note 7 calls this reading a useful fiction (pp. 334-335). |
| Phenomenal pain / psychological pain | p. 23 | The two components of the everyday concept of pain: the unpleasant quality, and the state produced by damage that leads to aversion. Model for splitting every dual mental term. |
| Perception / sensation | p. 24 | Proposed regimentation: perception as the psychological term, sensation as its phenomenal counterpart. Earlier, sensation is called a phenomenal concept in its central sense (p. 18); at p. 24 a central sense of it may be a combined notion. |
| Intentionality / propositional attitudes | p. 25 | The aboutness of states such as belief; having semantic content. |
| Deflationary / inflationary belief | p. 26 | Deflationary: a purely psychological concept not involving experience. Inflationary: experience (perhaps a specific experience) is required for truly believing. He declines to say which is the true concept. |
| Pseudobelief | p. 26 | Belief with any phenomenal component subtracted; said to do most of the explanatory work of belief. Parallel coinage: pseudo-attention (p. 33). |
| Relational component | p. 27 | An element of some mental states that depends on the environment (for example, the relation to water for beliefs about water). Either folded into the psychological or added as a third, minor component. |
| Mind-mind problem | p. 31 | Jackendoff's name, adopted for the residue once the physical-to-psychological link is granted: why psychological properties are accompanied by phenomenal ones. Note 14: Jackendoff's own pair is the phenomenological and the computational mind (p. 336). |
| Phenomenal consciousness | p. 31 | To be conscious in this sense is just to instantiate some phenomenal quality. |
| Psychological consciousness | p. 31 | The group of psychological properties for which the word consciousness is also used: awakeness, introspection, reportability, self-consciousness, attention, voluntary control, knowledge (pp. 32-33). |
| Awareness | p. 33 | A state in which we have access to some information and can use it in the control of behaviour. Functional. The most general kind of psychological consciousness. Reserved for this sense from p. 34. Matched to Block's access consciousness (p. 34). |

## Leading propositions

- Conscious experience cannot be defined in more primitive terms; it can only be characterised (p. 11).
- The problem of explaining phenomenal qualities is the problem of explaining consciousness, and is the hard part of the mind-body problem (p. 11).
- From the objective viewpoint consciousness is unexpected: the physical and information-processing facts alone would give no compelling reason to postulate it (pp. 11-12).
- There are two targets of explanation: that experience exists at all, and why particular experiences have the character they have (p. 12).
- A theory should say which physical processes give rise to consciousness, what experience each gives rise to, and make the arising intelligible (pp. 12-13).
- There are two distinct concepts of mind, phenomenal and psychological; neither is the correct analysis of mind; both pick out real phenomena (pp. 17-18).
- Historically the two were conflated in both directions: Descartes assimilated the psychological to the phenomenal; Ryle, Lewis and Armstrong assimilated the phenomenal to the psychological (pp. 19-21).
- The functional analysis of phenomenal concepts is false; the functional analysis of nonphenomenal mental concepts is broadly right (pp. 21-22).
- The psychological and the phenomenal together exhaust the mental, with at most a relational addition (pp. 23, 27).
- Everyday mental terms (pain, perception, emotion, belief) have both components, and which is essential to the word is a verbal question on which nothing important rests (pp. 23-24).
- Intentional states have no feature that outstrips the psychological, the phenomenal and the relational (pp. 27-28).
- In humans, phenomenal properties are always accompanied by corresponding psychological properties; this is an empirical fact and not a conceptual truth (pp. 24, 28).
- Our vocabulary for phenomenal qualities is borrowed from external causes and causal roles, but that fixes reference only; it does not make the concepts functional (pp. 28-29).
- The division splits the mind-body problem into an easy part, now dissolved, and a hard part, the link from the psychological to the phenomenal (pp. 30-31).
- Consciousness is itself ambiguous between a phenomenal and several psychological senses; the psychological senses are gathered under awareness (pp. 31-34).
- Consciousness is always accompanied by awareness, but not conversely (p. 34).
- Existing theories of consciousness (Rosenthal, Dennett, Armstrong, Van Gulick, Jaynes, Hofstadter) explain aspects of awareness, not phenomenal consciousness (pp. 35-36).

## Arguments

**A. Consciousness is surprising (pp. 11-12)**

- **Premises**: (p. 11) An objective story of fields, particles and complex systems makes information processing, behaviour, learning, memory and language unmysterious in principle. (p. 12) From those facts alone there would be no compelling reason to postulate experience. (p. 12) We nonetheless know directly that there is experience.
- **Unstated premises**: that what a completed objective story would lead us to predict is a fair guide to what that story entails; that first-person knowledge of experience is knowledge of something not already included in the processing.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 12) Experience is a further feature needing its own explanation: why it exists and why it has its specific character.
- Status: a preview. The entailment question is not argued until ch03.

**B. Against the functional analysis of phenomenal concepts (pp. 21-22)**

- **Premises**: (p. 21) Wondering whether someone has a colour experience is not wondering whether they receive stimulation and process it in a certain way; these are distinct questions. (p. 21) It is a conceptually coherent possibility that the causal role is played with no associated experience. (pp. 21-22) If the analysis were right, it would be unclear why anyone was ever troubled by the problem: how a state plays a causal role is not mysterious, why it feels like something is. Note 9 credits a similar point to Nagel 1970 against Armstrong (p. 335).
- **Unstated premises**: that the felt distinctness of two questions shows two concepts and not one concept under two modes of presentation; that a correct analysis of a concept must preserve the puzzlement the concept provokes; that the judgment of conceptual coherence is reliable here.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 21) The functional analysis is a false analysis of what it means to be phenomenal, an error as great as Descartes's in the other direction.
- He marks it as provisional: fuller treatment is promised later (p. 22).

**C. The functional analysis fits nonphenomenal concepts (p. 22)**

- **Premises**: (p. 22) For learning, asking whether a system can learn and asking whether it can adapt its behaviour to stimulation seem to be more or less the same question. (p. 22) No parallel worry about a left-over arises for learning, memory, perhaps belief.
- **Unstated premise**: the absence of a felt residue is a sufficient test for a concept being functional (the same test as in B, run the other way).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 22) Most nonphenomenal mental properties can be functionally analysed; nonphenomenal mental states are largely characterised by their role in the cognitive economy.

**D. The exhaustion argument (pp. 23, 27)**

- **Premises**: (p. 23) The manifest properties of mind that need explaining are the varieties of experience and the causation of behaviour; there is no third kind of manifest explanandum. (p. 27) What needs explaining is what we have first-person access to (experience) or third-person access to (behaviour, relations to the environment). (p. 27) Reasons for believing in any aspect of belief, including content, derive from one of these two classes. (p. 27) Fix a person's psychological, phenomenal and relational properties and nothing mental can be varied independently; we cannot imagine such a twin believing something different.
- **Unstated premises**: that a property of mind for which there is no independent source of evidence is not a further property; that inconceivability of independent variation shows there is no further aspect, as opposed to showing that content is fixed by the base in some way still needing an account; that the first-person/third-person division of evidence lines up with the phenomenal/psychological division of properties.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 23, 27-28) Every mental property is phenomenal, psychological, or a combination, with perhaps a relational element; intentionality is not a separate mystery.
- Endnote support: note 12 meets Kripke-style arguments that content is not fixed by psychological and phenomenal properties by saying their conclusion is indeterminacy of content, not a third irreducible element (p. 335).

**E. Co-occurrence does not license identification (pp. 28-29)**

- **Premises**: (p. 28) We have no independent language for phenomenal qualities; they are specified by external causes or causal roles. (p. 29) Even so, talk of a green sensation is talk of the kind of phenomenal state caused by grass and trees; the notion of experience remains unanalysed and primitive. (p. 29) We can coherently imagine phenomenal P without psychological P and the reverse. (p. 29) A Rolls-Royce icon is picked out as the icon found on Rolls-Royce cars, but is not a car.
- **Unstated premise**: that reference-fixing by a causal description and meaning are separable for phenomenal terms, as for the icon.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 29) Phenomenal P is not the same notion as psychological P; conflating them is fatal for explanation (p. 29).
- Endnote: note 13 calls this a topic-neutral analysis like Place's and Smart's, but one that does not yield their identity conclusion, because the notion of experience stays in the analysis; Smart's wording avoids it only by an equivocal phrase (pp. 335-336).

**F. The factoring of the mind-body problem (pp. 30-31)**

- **Premises**: (p. 30) Psychological properties are functional, so asking how a physical system could have one is asking how a state could play a causal role, a question for science with a defined research programme. (p. 31) Progress in the sciences has centred on the explanation of behaviour and leaves experience untouched. (p. 31) Phenomenal and psychological properties are closely correlated, which suggests a deep link.
- **Unstated premises**: that the physical-to-phenomenal link runs through the psychological and not directly through, say, biochemistry (he grants the alternative is conceivable but unlikely, p. 31); that the remaining philosophical problems about content and mental causation (p. 30) are not themselves deep.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 31) The psychological mind-body problem is dissolved; what remains is the mind-mind problem.

**G. Consciousness is always accompanied by awareness (p. 34)**

- **Premises**: (p. 34) Any conscious experience is reportable: if I am having it I can talk about it, or at least could focus on it and do so. (p. 34) Reportability implies awareness in the functional sense. (p. 34) Animals and prelinguistic humans cannot report but plausibly have a degree of awareness.
- **Unstated premises**: that reportability of every experience is established by the first-person human case; that the step from language users to creatures without language does not need separate support.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 34) Consciousness is always accompanied by awareness; awareness need not be accompanied by consciousness.

**H. The bait-and-switch diagnosis (pp. 31-32, 35-36)**

- **Premises**: (p. 31) The word consciousness has phenomenal and psychological senses. (p. 33) The psychological senses are functional, as shown by how one would explain them. (pp. 35-36) Rosenthal's higher-order thoughts, Dennett's model, Armstrong's self-scanner, Van Gulick's metapsychological information and Jaynes's theory each explain a kind of awareness.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 32, 35) Such accounts start with the weight of the phenomenal problem and end by explaining a psychological property; the phenomenal aspects generally go untouched.
- He records that Dennett would describe his own project differently (p. 35), and note 16 grants that Rosenthal's stated target is ambiguous (p. 336).

**Position versus argument.** The chapter's stated job is conceptual: to separate two notions and not beg the question of whether they turn out to be the same thing (p. 18). But argument B already asserts that the functional analysis of the phenomenal is false, on the strength of a conceivability judgment (p. 21). So the chapter both declares neutrality and takes the side the later chapters need.

## Evidence offered

- **Catalog of experiences** (pp. 13-17): visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory, taste, hot and cold, pain, other bodily sensations, mental imagery, conscious thought, emotions, sense of self. Explicitly pretheoretical; its job is to fix the subject matter. He includes a qualitative feel for occurrent thought, such as thinking of a lion (p. 16), which commits him early to phenomenology beyond the sensory.
- **Autobiographical case**: acquiring fuller depth vision with glasses as a child; the causal story about binocular vision does not reveal how the change felt (p. 14).
- **Dictionary entry**: Sutherland 1989 on the impossibility of defining consciousness (p. 10).
- **History of the conflation** (pp. 18-21): Descartes's reply to the Fourth Objections; Wundt and James as introspectionists; Freud's unconscious beliefs as evidence that a causal construal of belief was already in use; behaviourism; computational cognitive science; Ryle 1949; Chisholm 1957 and Geach 1957 against single-disposition analyses; Lewis 1966 and Armstrong 1968. Note 6 adds that Descartes often counted sensations as bodily, so the fit with his reading is loose (p. 334).
- **Test for a psychological concept**: could something be an instance of M with no particular phenomenal quality? (p. 24). Applied to learning, categorization, memory (pp. 24-25).
- **Belief cases**: Dylan touring Australia (p. 25); the Bradman belief, whose content seems fixed by the connection to Bradman and its cognitive role, not by its faint feel (p. 26); water beliefs for the relational element (p. 27).
- **Cited opponents on belief**: Searle 1990a, that content depends on consciousness (pp. 25-26). Note 10 replies that aspectual shape might be had without consciousness, for instance by a computer storing information under a name but not a number (p. 335). Note 11 attributes the weaker capacity view tentatively to Lockwood and Nagel (p. 335).
- **Linguistic evidence**: Ryle's remark that there are no neat sensation words; Wittgenstein on outward criteria (pp. 28-29); the catalog itself describes experiences only by their causes (p. 28).
- **Analogies**: the Rolls-Royce icon (p. 29); the absence of a pressing life-body problem in philosophy of biology (p. 30).
- **Allies for the awareness distinction**: Block 1995 on access consciousness and Newell 1992 (p. 34); note 15 adds Nelkin, Bisiach, Natsoulas and Dennett 1969 (p. 336).
- **Qualification by endnote**: psychophysics is an exception to the claim that experimental psychology does not bear on experience (p. 336).
- **Confident language outrunning argument (my comment)**: the functional analysis of the phenomenal is called simply false (p. 21) and the conflation fatal (p. 29) in a chapter that defers the supporting argument (p. 22).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Feeds ch03.** Argument B's premise that role without experience is conceptually coherent (p. 21), and the imagined twin who is psychologically identical but experiences something different (p. 27), are the zombie and inverted-spectrum intuitions in miniature. Here they are asserted; the chapter says the matter will be considered in much more detail later (p. 22).
- **Feeds ch02.** The contrast between a conceptual truth and a fact about the world (p. 24), and between what is logically possible and what is empirical fact (p. 28), is the logical/natural distinction that ch02 formalises as two strengths of supervenience (announced at fm01, p. 9).
- **Feeds ch06.** Awareness is introduced as the psychological correlate of consciousness, with a promise to constrain it until it is coextensive with phenomenal consciousness (p. 34). The claim that the link between the two is extremely strong is deferred to later chapters (p. 31).
- **Feeds ch05.** Reportability and judgments about experience are placed on the psychological side (pp. 32, 34), so they are among the things physically explained. Combined with causal closure (fm01, p. 6) this is the seed of the problem ch05 addresses.
- **Feeds ch09.** Treating intentionality as psychological plus perhaps phenomenal (pp. 26-28) is what later lets him recast Searle's argument as one about consciousness (ch09, p. 301). Note 10's reply to Searle (p. 335) anticipates it.
- **Term shift: sensation.** A phenomenal concept in its central sense (p. 18); a concept with both components that leans phenomenal (p. 24); possibly a combined notion requiring both (p. 24). The regimented use, perception's phenomenal counterpart, is a proposal (p. 24).
- **Term shift: psychological and functional.** Psychological is defined by role in causing or explaining behaviour (p. 18), then equated with functionalist analysis (p. 22). Whether the defining link is causation, explanation or both is left open (p. 22).
- **Term shift: easy and hard.** In fm01 the easy problems are particular questions about processing and report (fm01, p. 4). Here the easy part is the whole psychological mind-body problem (p. 30). Same contrast, wider scope.
- **Internal tension (my observation).** Phenomenal qualities are said to have a rich intrinsic character (p. 28), yet every public phenomenal category is fixed by causes or roles (pp. 28-29). The chapter does not say how, on that basis, two people could know they share a phenomenal concept. The book's later treatment of phenomenal belief content (ch05) is where to check.
- **Internal tension (my observation).** Co-occurrence is called a fact about the human mind (p. 28), while the accompaniment of consciousness by awareness is stated without restriction (p. 34). The support for the unrestricted form is the human reportability case.
- **Internal tension (my observation).** The chapter claims the psychological mind-body problem is dissolved (p. 31) while listing unsolved problems about content, mental causation and whether psychological properties are instantiated at all (p. 30), and granting that no complete functional analysis of belief exists (p. 25). The dissolution is a claim about the kind of problem that remains, not about results in hand.
- **Life-body analogy (my observation).** The analogy to biology (p. 30) is used for the psychological side only. A reductionist can run the same analogy for the phenomenal side. The chapter does not say why the analogy stops where it does; ch03 has to.

## Open questions

- Does any later chapter give an argument for the conceivability premise of B (p. 21) that does not rest on the same intuition? If not, the first constraint of the Introduction and the premise are one commitment.
- The exhaustion thesis (pp. 23, 27) is defended from the sources of evidence. Is it needed anywhere later as a premise, for example to show that explaining behaviour plus experience explains everything mental?
- The relational component (p. 27) is admitted and then set aside as raising no deep mystery. Does externalism about content cause trouble later, where the content of phenomenal beliefs is at issue (ch05)?
- Is the inflationary concept of belief (p. 26) ever needed? If a being without experience only pseudobelieves, what does a zombie's claim to be conscious express?
- Can awareness be constrained so as to be coextensive with consciousness (p. 34) without building the phenomenal into the constraint? Check ch06.
- The reportability argument (p. 34) concerns experiences one can focus on. Are there experiences outside attention, such as the fringes of the visual field (p. 32), that are conscious but not available for report in the required sense?
- The two targets of explanation (p. 12), existence and specific character, are distinguished at the start. Do the later positive chapters address both, or mainly the first?
