# ch07: Absent Qualia, Fading Qualia, Dancing Qualia (pp. 232–259)

## Question this chapter answers

In virtue of which physical properties does consciousness arise: biochemical makeup, or abstract causal organisation? Chalmers's answer: fine-grained functional organisation. Any system with the same fine-grained organisation as a conscious system has qualitatively identical experience, as a matter of natural law though not of logic. He argues for this by two reductio thought experiments: if a functional isomorph could lack experience (absent qualia) or have different experience (inverted qualia), then gradual or switched replacement would yield a rational subject whose experience fades or flips without the subject being able to notice, and that dissociation between consciousness and cognition is too implausible to accept.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Functional organization | p. 232 | The abstract pattern of causal interaction among a system's parts and its inputs and outputs: a number of components, a number of states for each, and dependency relations among states, inputs and outputs. The nature of the components is left unspecified. |
| Realization | p. 232 | A physical system realises an organisation when it can be divided into components and states whose causal dependencies precisely reflect the specified ones. The connections must be reliable and counterfactual-supporting, not mere regularities (pp. 236-237). |
| Fine-grained functional organization | p. 233 | Organisation at a level fine enough to determine behavioural capacities; the neural level is used for illustration, though a higher level might suffice or a lower one be needed. |
| Functional isomorphs | p. 233 | Two systems sharing fine-grained organisation and in corresponding states at the relevant times. |
| Principle of organizational invariance | p. 233 | Any system with the same fine-grained functional organisation as a conscious system has qualitatively identical experiences. Consciousness is an organisational invariant. |
| Nonreductive functionalism | p. 234 | Consciousness arises from functional organisation but is not a functional state; organisation suffices for experience with natural necessity, without constituting it (p. 258). |
| Absent qualia | p. 234 | The hypothesis that a functional isomorph of a conscious system could have no experience. |
| Inverted qualia | p. 234 | The hypothesis that a functional isomorph could have experience of a different kind (blue where I have red). |
| Possibility (in this chapter) | p. 235 | Natural or empirical possibility: compatibility with the laws of the actual world. Logical possibility of absent and inverted qualia is granted throughout. |
| Fading qualia | p. 240 | Along a series of gradual replacements from me to an unconscious isomorph, experience gradually fades while organisation is constant. |
| Suddenly disappearing qualia | p. 240 | Experience vanishes at a single step in the series. |
| Blue experience | p. 249 | Stipulated usage: the kind of experience that Chalmers himself calls blue and usually has on seeing the sky; not whatever a subject calls blue, nor whatever blue objects cause. |
| Dancing qualia | p. 252 | Experience switching back and forth between two significantly different qualities as a switch alternates two isomorphic circuits, with no change in organisation and so none in what the subject notices. |
| Noticing principle | p. 254 | When one's experiences change significantly, one can notice the change (given rationality and attention, p. 253). |

## Leading propositions

- Consciousness arises from fine-grained functional organisation; substrate is irrelevant (pp. 232-233).
- The invariance principle can be held without reductive functionalism (p. 234).
- The question is natural possibility; arguments for the logical possibility of absent and inverted qualia, including Chalmers's own in ch03, are untouched (p. 235).
- The thought experiments are plausibility arguments, not proofs (p. 235).
- Intuitions against consciousness in a population-brain are weak evidence, since a brain is intuitively no more suited to produce experience (p. 236).
- Chance realisation of the organisation is astronomically unlikely, because the required conditionals are many and must be counterfactual-supporting (pp. 236-237).
- Blindsight is no counterexample: blindsight subjects differ functionally (pp. 237-238).
- If absent qualia are possible, then on a gradual replacement series either experience fades or it suddenly disappears (p. 240).
- Sudden disappearance would need brute discontinuities in the laws of nature (p. 240).
- Fading means an intermediate subject, Joe, who is rational and conscious yet systematically wrong about his experience; this is implausible (pp. 240-241).
- Searle's alternative, that the subject knows experience is shrinking but cannot act on it, is ruled out because nothing in the system can realise the new beliefs (pp. 242-243).
- So absent qualia are almost certainly impossible (p. 244); the argument generalises to any isomorph, including a population (pp. 243-244).
- The fading argument does not rule out inversion, since intermediate cases of mild inversion preserve structure and involve no error (p. 250).
- If inverted qualia are possible, a switch between isomorphic neural and silicon circuits would make experience flip without the subject noticing (pp. 251-253).
- That is more implausible than fading; if it were possible it might be happening to us constantly as low-level physical properties change (pp. 253-254).
- The dancing argument also works against absent qualia and is the stronger of the two (pp. 254-255).
- Loopholes remain for speed, history, very mild inversions and unattended qualia; each leads to an arbitrary position (pp. 255-257).
- The result is nonreductive functionalism: natural, not logical, determination of experience by organisation (p. 258).
- The invariance principle is a law but not a fundamental one; it constrains the fundamental theory and supports the ambitions of artificial intelligence (p. 259).

## Arguments

**A. Deflating the intuition argument for absent qualia (pp. 235-237)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 235-236) Absent-qualia arguments exhibit a bizarre realisation and appeal to the intuition that it is not conscious. (p. 236) It is equally counterintuitive that a brain produces experience, yet it does. (p. 236) That we do not see experience in the system, that there seems no room for it, and that its functioning is explicable without it, are all true of brains. (pp. 236-237) Arbitrary systems will not realise the organisation by chance.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 237) The intuition is weak evidence; the issue is open. Chalmers accepts the burden of proof without conceding it is his.

**B. Fading qualia: reductio of absent qualia (pp. 238-244)**

- **Premises**: (p. 238) Suppose a silicon isomorph, Robot, has no experience. (pp. 238-239) A series of cases can be built, replacing neurons one at a time with chips of identical input-output function, preserving organisation throughout. (p. 240) Along the series experience either fades or disappears suddenly. (p. 240) Sudden disappearance requires a brute discontinuity unlike any known law, at an arbitrary point. (pp. 240-241) Under fading, an intermediate system Joe has degraded experience but, being isomorphic to me, makes all the judgments I make. (p. 241) So Joe is systematically wrong about his experience, with no functional pathology. (pp. 241-242) In every familiar case, rational conscious beings can judge their experience accurately, and ordinary fading (drowsiness, simpler animals) comes with functional change.
- **Unstated premises**: that judgments about experience are fixed by functional organisation (he says on a functional construal of belief, p. 241); that the reliability of introspection observed where organisation and substrate have never come apart carries over to cases where they do; that the fundamental psychophysical laws are continuous in the way physical laws are; that there is no third pattern, such as partial loss tracking the replaced region, that escapes both horns (he treats loss of distinctions as a form of fading, p. 241).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 243) Qualia do not fade; Joe's experience is as rich as mine; Robot is conscious. (p. 244) Absent qualia are almost certainly impossible.

**C. Against Searle's shrinking-experience scenario (pp. 242-243)**

- **Premises**: (p. 242) Searle: experience shrinks, the subject knows and is amazed, but behaviour is unaffected. (p. 243) New beliefs, desires and amazement must be reflected in some process unless one is a very strong dualist. (p. 243) The system is identical to the original at a fine grain; neither the remaining neurons nor the silicon can support the extra states.
- **Unstated premise**: that the contents of belief supervene on functional organisation and not on substrate.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 243) Fading requires either a capricious relation between belief content and physical state or massive error by a rational being; both are less plausible than no fading.

**D. Replies to objections to the fading argument (pp. 244-247)**

- Replacement impossible in practice: technological impossibility is irrelevant; if silicon isomorphs are naturally impossible the principle, being conditional, is unaffected (pp. 244-245).
- Some systems are massively mistaken (blindness denial): those systems are functionally impaired; the claim is restricted to rational systems with unimpaired mechanisms (p. 245).
- Sorites: the argument accepts a continuum and rejects the intermediate cases on independent grounds; it denies only sudden large transitions (pp. 245-246).
- Behavioural invariance would follow too: no, because behavioural equivalents such as a look-up table need not share judgments, and no small-step series connects me to them (p. 246).
- Residual option: bite the bullet on fading or on sudden disappearance. He says this response is not obviously wrong (pp. 246-247).
- Extension: gradual change of organisation suggests systems with similar, not only identical, organisation are conscious (p. 247).

**E. Existing inversion arguments do not touch the principle (pp. 247-250)**

- **Premises**: (p. 249) Rewiring from retina to central areas, in an adult or from birth, changes fine-grained organisation. (pp. 249-250) Block's Inverted Earth with inverting lenses keeps internal organisation and experience the same; it separates experience only from environmental and wide functional properties.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 249-250) Neither gives a case of internal isomorphs with different experience.

**F. Dancing qualia: reductio of inverted qualia (pp. 250-254)**

- **Premises**: (p. 250) Suppose my silicon isomorph has blue experience where I have red. (p. 251) On the replacement series there are two cases, A and B, differing in at most one-tenth of the brain and differing significantly in experience, because ten unnoticeable steps cannot span red to blue. (pp. 251-252) Take those to be me and Bill; install Bill's silicon circuit in my head as a backup with a switch. (p. 252) After the switch I am in effect Bill, so I experience what Bill does. (pp. 252-253) Organisation is unchanged, so no new belief, reaction or shift of attention can occur. (p. 253) A significant change in attended experience that a rational subject cannot notice is a radical dissociation of consciousness from cognition. (pp. 253-254) If it were possible, ordinary changes in the low-level physical realisation of our neurons could be making our qualia dance all the time, and only the noticing principle defends against that.
- **Unstated premises**: that the dangling, disconnected circuit makes no difference to experience (asserted, p. 252); that the same subject persists through the switch (he restricts the replaced fraction for this reason, p. 256); that noticing is a functional matter; that what the subject would compare, a present experience with a memory of the earlier one, is not itself altered by the switch.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 254) Replacement preserving organisation preserves qualia; experience is wholly determined by functional organisation. The same construction between two significantly different points on the way to absent qualia refutes absent qualia (p. 254).

**G. Loopholes and the double-switching objection (pp. 255-258)**

- Speed and history cannot be varied by a switch; qualia depending on them would be arbitrary, or would need a nonlocal effect of distal history (p. 255).
- Very mild inversions made of a few unnoticeable steps survive; the figure cannot be raised much above a fifth or a quarter without personal-identity problems; but if colour experience depends on a small area the argument reaches even the mildest noticeable inversion (pp. 255-256).
- Unattended qualia might be invertible (Rensink, O'Regan and Clark 1995 on unnoticed changes); the resulting position, invariance only under attention, is unattractive (pp. 256-257).
- Double switching (rewire inputs and compensate downstream): the compensation would have to simulate the central area, so the simulation does the causal work; and only behaviour, not organisation, is preserved, so the subject would notice (pp. 257-258).

**H. What the arguments do and do not establish (pp. 258-259)**

- **Premises**: (p. 258) Fading and dancing qualia are coherent, so not logically impossible. (p. 258) Even if they were logically impossible, that would yield only a necessary conditional (if one system with organisation F has an experience, all do); the antecedent is empirical, and making it necessary would require logical supervenience on the physical, which ch03-ch04 deny.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 258-259) Organisation suffices for experience with natural necessity. For every conscious physical system there is some organisation F it realises such that any realiser of F has identical experience; F must be fine enough to fix judgments, which is secured by fixing the mechanisms of behaviour.

**Position versus argument.** The position is invariance of experience over functional isomorphs. The argument is comparative plausibility: the alternatives require massive undetectable error or brute discontinuity. Chalmers says so repeatedly (pp. 235, 240, 254), though section 6 opens with the verb established for the conditionals and ends with a law (pp. 258-259).

## Evidence offered

- **Thought experiments**: Block's population of China (pp. 234, 236); gradual neuron-to-silicon replacement with Robot and Joe at a basketball game (pp. 238-241); extension from silicon to a population by expansion, slowing and substitution of people (pp. 243-244); the backup circuit and switch with Bill (pp. 251-252); Locke's violet and marigold (pp. 247-248); retinal rewiring (p. 249); Block's Inverted Earth (pp. 249-250); double switching, credited in note 18 to Terry Horgan (pp. 257, 368).
- **Numerical estimate**: about a billion parts with some ten states each; the chance of an arbitrary system satisfying the required conditionals is given as an iterated exponential (pp. 236-237), derived in note 5 (p. 366).
- **Appeal to physics**: in all fundamental laws known to date, dependence of one magnitude on a continuous magnitude is continuous; nonlinear dynamics does not give brute discontinuity (p. 240). Schrödinger's cat as the model for how a thought experiment constrains theories without deciding between them (p. 235).
- **Empirical observation**: blindsight subjects differ functionally (pp. 237-238); blindness denial as impaired cognition (p. 245); everyday fading in drowsiness and across species accompanies functional change (p. 242); unnoticeable differences between shades exist but are very small (p. 251); change-blindness results of Rensink, O'Regan and Clark (p. 256); prosthetic limbs as precedent for prosthetic neurons (p. 244).
- **Authors engaged**: Block 1978, 1981, 1990; Searle 1992, quoted at length (p. 242); Pylyshyn 1980, Savitt 1982 and Cuda 1985 as predecessors of the replacement argument (p. 238); Locke (pp. 247-248); Schlick 1932 for the verificationist dismissal, which he rejects (p. 248); Hardin and Harrison on asymmetry of colour space, set aside by granting symmetry for argument (p. 248); Gert, Lycan and Wittgenstein on behaviour-preserving inversion (p. 249).
- **From the endnotes**:
  - Note 2: the Churchlands' objection that a population simulation needs impossibly many people fails because inputs and states are combinatorially structured; no more people than brain cells are needed (p. 366).
  - Note 3: Bogen and Lycan deny qualia to accidental realisations on teleological grounds; Chalmers rejects this because it makes qualia depend on history, and concedes that a chance realisation would have qualia (p. 366).
  - Note 7: some of Joe's error might be removed by ch05's constitution of belief by experience (his concept of red experience might now refer to pink), but not his errors about distinctions (pp. 366-367).
  - Note 8: Cuda holds the description of such mistaken systems is senseless; Chalmers replies it is coherent, and that he knows it is false of himself by direct experience (p. 367).
  - Note 9: there are sorites arguments nearby (Tienson 1987), but his is not one (p. 367).
  - Note 11: Nida-Rümelin 1996 on pseudonormal vision: swapped cone response patterns could give actual subjects behaviourally similar to normals with inverted colour experience (p. 367).
  - Note 12: Putnam's and Shoemaker's rewiring arguments tell at most against a coarse-grained invariance principle. In the adaptation-and-amnesia variant the subjects end behaviourally identical but probably not organisationally identical; if organisation did fully revert, reversion of experience would not be implausible (p. 367).
  - Note 14: moving Block's lenses inward to the optic nerve or cortex still gives no isomorphs with different experience (p. 368).
  - Note 15: sources and parallels: Dennett's story, Shoemaker 1982, Seager 1991, and Zuboff 1994, who uses essentially the same argument for reductive functionalism by holding dancing qualia impossible a priori (p. 368).
  - Notes 16-17: White 1986 on tiny DNA differences; Shoemaker's criterion for what realises a quale, which Chalmers thinks will pick out a fine-grained functional property (p. 368).
- **Confident language against tentative language**: only plausibility arguments (p. 235); cannot be disproved (p. 240); almost certainly impossible (p. 244); we know what the result will be (p. 254); a proof is disclaimed in the same paragraph (p. 254).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch06**: both reductios turn on the coherence between consciousness and cognition, in particular the reliability principle (ch06, p. 205). The X-factor argument is named as the earlier version of the same conclusion (p. 234). The blindsight reply repeats ch06 (pp. 237-238).
- **Depends on ch03-ch04**: logical possibility of absent and inverted qualia is granted and was used against reductive functionalism (p. 235); section 6's limit on the result depends on the failure of logical supervenience (p. 258). The passing argument that inverted qualia among isomorphs already refutes materialism (p. 248) restates an earlier chapter, which I take to be ch04 (not read in this pass).
- **Tension with ch05 (my observation; ch05 not read in this pass, check against its note)**: ch05 argued, as I understand it, that phenomenal judgments are explicable without consciousness and that a zombie makes the same judgments. That is why Joe's judgments stay fixed. But it also means the cognitive mechanism does not secure the truth of the judgments; a logically possible zombie is a rational system wholly wrong about its experience. The claim that no rational, unimpaired system can be so mistaken (p. 245) is therefore not something Joe's intact mechanisms can support; it rests on the lawful regularity asserted in ch06, whose evidence comes from cases where organisation and substrate never come apart. Chalmers notes the zombie end of the series and sets it aside because nobody is there to be wrong (p. 241).
- **Tension with ch05 on belief content (my observation)**: the reply to Searle requires belief differences to show up in functioning unless one is a very strong dualist (p. 243). Note 7 refers to ch05 section 7 for the constitution of belief by experience, and concedes this could alter what Joe's concepts refer to (pp. 366-367). So Chalmers himself allows some belief content to vary with experience while organisation is fixed; the dispute with Searle is over how much.
- **Continuity premise and the status of the law (my observation)**: sudden disappearance is rejected because fundamental laws are continuous (p. 240), but the invariance principle is said not to be fundamental (p. 259), and high-level laws were allowed exceptions around the edges in ch06 (p. 228). The rejection needs a claim about whatever fundamental psychophysical laws underlie the principle, which are not yet stated.
- **Memory in the dancing case (my observation)**: noticing a flip needs a comparison between present experience and the remembered earlier one. Chalmers argues from the absence of any functional difference (p. 253). He does not consider whether an opponent could say the switch changes how the earlier experience is now recalled, so that there is nothing discrepant to notice.
- **Shift in the scope of functionalism**: the term covers a reductive thesis (rejected in ch03), a weaker natural-law thesis (p. 235), and nonreductive functionalism (pp. 234, 258). The argument is offered to reductive functionalists as support against biochemical views (p. 234).
- **Feeds ch08**: the invariance principle joins the two coherence principles as the three nonbasic laws that constrain the fundamental theory (p. 259).
- **Feeds ch09**: the conclusion is said to support artificial intelligence, discussed in ch09 (p. 259); the formal account of realisation is deferred to ch09 (p. 232).
- **Link to ch06 note 10**: the endnote uses inversion among isomorphs against representationalism (p. 364); here inversion among isomorphs is naturally impossible (p. 254).

## Open questions

- What fixes the right level of organisation F? It is said to be fine enough to fix judgments and behavioural mechanisms (p. 259), illustrated at the neural level while allowing that a lower level might be needed (p. 233). If a lower level is needed, how much substrate independence remains?
- Every system realises many organisations (p. 259), and realisation requires only a suitable division into parts and states (p. 232). Does the invariance principle then attribute experience too widely? Chalmers refers the formal treatment to ch09.
- Is the noticing principle (p. 254) an empirical regularity or part of what attending to an experience is? Chalmers treats the conceptual reading as disputable (p. 258); if it is empirical, its evidence is first-person and drawn from ordinary cases only.
- The unattended-qualia loophole (pp. 256-257) sits beside ch06's claim that unattended fringes are experienced and available. Could experience outside attention depend on substrate? He calls the position unsatisfying, not refuted.
- Note 11's pseudonormal vision (p. 367) suggests actual inversion between behaviourally similar people. What organisational difference does the principle require between them, and is it of the kind the argument assumes?
- The argument for similar (not identical) organisation is one paragraph (p. 247). How similar is similar enough, and does the answer depend on ch08?
- Could a fundamental psychophysical law be discontinuous? The continuity premise (p. 240) is an induction from physics.
