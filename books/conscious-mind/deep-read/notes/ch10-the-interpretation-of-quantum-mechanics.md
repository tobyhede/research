# ch10: The Interpretation of Quantum Mechanics (pp. 310–334)

## Question this chapter answers

Can a theory of consciousness help with the interpretation of quantum mechanics? Chalmers argues that it can, indirectly. The central problem of quantum mechanics can be restated as a question about how a superposed physical world gives rise to discrete experience. His psychophysical principles predict that a superposed brain state is associated with distinct subjects each having a discrete experience, which is the piece Everett's interpretation was missing. He concludes that the Everett interpretation is the most attractive on theoretical grounds and the hardest to believe.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The calculus | p. 311 | Quantum mechanics taken only as a recipe for predicting measurement results, with no commitment about what the world is like. |
| Wave function / state vector | p. 311 | The quantum state of a system: a superposition of basic values, each with a complex magnitude. A perfectly determinate state, though not a classical one (pp. 312-313). |
| Superposition | p. 311 | A state that is a combination of simpler states. A vector can be decomposed in many ways, and no basis is preferred by nature (p. 312). |
| Schrödinger equation | p. 313 | The linear, deterministic differential equation governing the wave function under almost all circumstances. |
| Measurement postulate | p. 313 | The collapse or projection postulate: when a measurement is made, the wave function collapses probabilistically into an eigenstate of the measured quantity, with probability given by squared amplitude (p. 314). |
| Interpretation | pp. 314-315 | An account of what happens in the real world such that the calculus works. |
| Hidden variables | p. 320 | Determinate values at the basic level, not captured by the wave function, which explain macroscopic discreteness without collapse. Bohm's theory is the example. |
| GRW | p. 320 | Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber's theory: each particle has a tiny chance per second of a spontaneous position collapse, which makes macroscopic systems nearly always discrete. |
| Decoherence | p. 318 | The tendency of a superposed wave function to resolve into a superposition of distinct macroscopic states with minimal interference between them. It does not say why only one component is actual. |
| Everett interpretation | p. 322 | The view that the physical state of the world is completely described by the wave function and its evolution completely by the Schrödinger equation. |
| Splitting-worlds interpretation | p. 323 | The reading on which the world literally divides at each measurement. Chalmers rejects it as needing a new principle and a preferred basis, and denies it is Everett's. |
| One-big-world interpretation | p. 324 | Chalmers's name for the reading he defends: one superposed world, with any splitting only in the minds of observers. |
| Miniworld / maxiworld | p. 324 | The discrete world a single mind perceives, and the superposed world that actually exists. |
| Relative-state interpretation | p. 324 | Everett's own name: a discrete state counts as the state of the world only relative to an observer. |
| Preferred basis | p. 324 | A privileged decomposition of the wave function into components. Quantum mechanics supplies none. |
| Maximal phenomenal state / maximal physical state | p. 326 | A phenomenal state characterising a subject's entire experience at a time; a physical state fully characterising a system's intrinsic physical state at a time. |
| Superposition principle | p. 326 | If the theory predicts that maximal physical state P gives rise to maximal phenomenal state E, it predicts that a superposition of P with orthogonal states also gives rise to E. |
| Brute indexicality | p. 328 | The fact that I am this mind and not another; used to answer why my mind is not superposed and why I am in this branch. |
| Measure on the space of minds | pp. 330-331 | A weighting of observers, by squared amplitude, intended to recover the quantum probabilities. |

## Leading propositions

- Quantum mechanics cannot explain consciousness; quantum theories of consciousness face the same explanatory gap as classical ones (pp. 310-311).
- The link may run the other way: a theory of consciousness can bear on the interpretation of quantum mechanics (p. 311).
- The formalism without the measurement postulate is counterintuitive but not paradoxical; reality at the basic level is wavelike and determinate (pp. 312-313).
- The trouble comes from the measurement postulate. Measurement, measuring apparatus, classical system and macroscopic system are not terms that can appear in a fundamental law (pp. 315-316).
- The only tenable criterion for a literal collapse is that it occurs when a quantum system affects a consciousness, and this presupposes dualism (p. 316).
- Chalmers rejects that view all the same: it conflicts with ubiquitous experience and with the persistence of low-level superpositions, there is no good theory of how consciousness would cause collapse, and collapse is nonlocal and unlike the rest of physics (p. 317).
- The measurement postulate cannot be derived from the Schrödinger equation, because linearity carries microscopic superpositions up to macroscopic ones (p. 318).
- Refusing to interpret the calculus either gives up on knowing what the world is like or, in its stronger form, gives up on an independent reality (p. 319).
- GRW and Bohm add complexity behind a simple calculus, privilege position, and fit badly with relativity (pp. 320-321). Chalmers compares them to an evil-demon hypothesis (pp. 321-322).
- The simplest interpretation is that the Schrödinger equation is all (p. 322).
- Everett left unexplained why each component of a superposed brain state should have its own observer; that depends on a theory of consciousness (pp. 324-325).
- The central question of quantum mechanics can be put as: given that the physical structure of the world is superposed, why do we experience it as discrete? (p. 325).
- Chalmers's theory answers it. By the account of implementation in ch09 and organizational invariance, a superposed system implements whatever computation its component implements, so it gives rise to the same subject (pp. 326-327).
- No preferred basis is needed, and the theory does not claim these are the only minds a superposed system has (p. 328).
- The most substantial objection is that the Everett view cannot make sense of probabilities (p. 330). A measure on the space of minds proportional to squared amplitude would recover them, but what it corresponds to objectively is unresolved (pp. 331-332).
- The Everett interpretation is the simplest, is local and is compatible with relativity (p. 332); Chalmers finds it the most appealing and cannot wholeheartedly believe it (p. 333).
- His own ranking of confidence: fairly confident that experience is fundamental, weakly that it is ubiquitous, torn on Everett (p. 333).

## Arguments

**A. Against a physical criterion for collapse (pp. 315-316)**

- **Premises**: (p. 315) If collapse is an objective process, there must be clear objective criteria for when it occurs. (pp. 315-316) Measurement, apparatus, classical and macroscopic are vague or arbitrary and cannot figure in a basic law. (p. 316) A microscopic criterion is ruled out by experiment; a high-level physical criterion would be arbitrary.
- **Unstated premise**: that fundamental laws must be stated in precise, non-arbitrary, basic terms. This is the same standard applied to psychophysical laws in ch08.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 316) No physical criterion for collapse is acceptable.

**B. The conditional case for consciousness-caused collapse, and its rejection (pp. 316-318)**

- **Premises**: (p. 316) Consciousness is determinate and non-arbitrary. (p. 316) If dualism is true, consciousness is not one more high-level physical property, so the criterion can be fundamental.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 316) This is the only literal interpretation with any currency.
- **Rejection, premises**: (p. 317) Chalmers has argued that experience may be ubiquitous; then collapse would happen constantly at the lowest level, against the evidence. (p. 317) No theory says which effect on consciousness causes collapse. (p. 317) Collapse is instantaneous, discontinuous, time-asymmetric and nonlocal.
- **Unstated premise**: the first objection has force only if the speculative ch08 is right, which Chalmers elsewhere rates below even odds. The other two are considerations of elegance, and he calls them far from knockdown (p. 317).

**C. Linearity blocks a free derivation of collapse (p. 318)**

- **Premises**: (p. 318) The Schrödinger dynamics are linear. (p. 318) If an up electron leads to one macroscopic state and a down electron to another, a superposed electron leads to a superposed macroscopic state.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 318) Without further basic principles, superposition must be expected at the macroscopic level. Decoherence organises the superposition but does not select one component.

**D. The simplicity argument against GRW and Bohm (pp. 320-322)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 320-321) Both privilege position and sit badly with relativity. (p. 321) GRW leaves low-amplitude tails, so the pointer is still superposed. (p. 321) Bohm's theory is extremely nonlocal. (pp. 321-322) Both put a complicated mechanism behind a simple and anomaly-free calculus.
- **Unstated premise**: that simplicity of basic laws is strong evidence of truth, strong enough to outweigh the cost of an ontology with vastly more in it. The comparison with a deceiving demon assumes the simple calculus is the right description to begin with.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 322) These interpretations should be taken seriously, but a simpler picture is preferable.

**E. The superposition principle (pp. 325-327)**

- **Premises**: (p. 326) Consciousness arises from implementing an appropriate computation (ch09). (p. 326) Implementation is a mapping from physical substates to formal states under which causal relations mirror formal relations. (p. 326) For a system in a superposition of P with orthogonal states, define a substate as the one whose projection onto P is the original substate. (pp. 326-327) Because the Schrödinger equation is linear, the transitions among the projected substates mirror those of the original system. (p. 327) Organizational invariance (ch07).
- **Unstated premises**: that a component of a wave function defined by projection counts as a physical substate with causal relations in the sense ch09 requires, and that there are no further constraints on implementation (for instance amplitude) that the component fails. Also that the argument does not prove too much: the same construction seems to yield a subject for every component under every decomposition, which Chalmers accepts with the answer "perhaps" (p. 328). The construction also treats all components alike however small their amplitude, which is where the probability problem comes from.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 327) Even if the world is in a giant superposition, the theory predicts subjects who experience a discrete world. So the Schrödinger equation plus independently motivated psychophysical principles predicts the manifest image.

**F. Replies to objections (pp. 327-329)**

- Splitting and conservation objections apply to the splitting-worlds reading only (p. 327).
- Preferred basis: the derivation does not need one (p. 328).
- Why is my mind not superposed, and why am I in this branch? Brute indexicality: it is like asking why I am not a mouse (p. 328). The interesting minds are the coherent ones, which decoherence picks out (pp. 328-329).
- Personal identity: Parfit's reductionism means there is no further fact about which descendant is me (p. 329).

**G. The probability problem (pp. 330-332)**

- **Premises**: (p. 330) On the Everett view both outcomes occur whatever the amplitudes, so a 90:10 case and a 50:50 case produce the same pair of observers. (p. 330) Everett's measure on observers reproduces the frequencies, but neither the Schrödinger equation nor the psychophysical laws make that measure the correct one. (pp. 330-331) Albert and Loewer's infinitely many minds with stochastic intrapsychic laws recover the probabilities at the cost of ad hoc laws and irreducible facts of personal identity.
- **Proposal**: (pp. 331-332) Take the antecedent likelihood of being a given mind to be proportional to squared amplitude. Chalmers ties this to the implicit measure used in inductive reasoning.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 332) The interpretation of probabilities remains the most significant difficulty. The argument is left open.

**H. The comparative verdict (pp. 332-333)**

- Wigner, Bohm and Everett are ranked for craziness (Bohm least, Everett most) and for theoretical virtue (the reverse) (p. 332). The other two also contain an uncollapsed wave function in a central role, which narrows the gap in plausibility (p. 333).

## Evidence offered

- The exposition of the formalism: spin, position and momentum as vectors, linearity, collapse and squared amplitudes (pp. 311-314).
- Schrödinger's cat, with the cat anaesthetised to keep its consciousness out of the picture (pp. 316-317).
- Albert (1992) for the linearity argument; Daneri, Loinger and Prosperi for the failed thermodynamic derivations; Gell-Mann and Hartle for decoherence (p. 318).
- Bell's theorem for the nonlocality of any hidden-variables theory (p. 321). Endnote 6 says this is the only place Bell and EPR matter to the chapter, and that the Everett view is the only local interpretation compatible with the result (pp. 370-371).
- Albert and Loewer, and Lockwood, for the non-splitting reading of Everett; DeWitt as the source of the splitting reading (p. 324). Endnote 7 claims the one-big-world reading is the common one among physicists (p. 371).
- Penrose's remark that a theory of consciousness is needed before the many-worlds view can be squared with observation (p. 325).
- Parfit on personal identity (p. 329); Hofstadter's statement of the branch objection (p. 329).
- Endnote 4 answers Albert's charge that consciousness is as vague as measurement: there is plausibly a fact of the matter about whether a system is conscious (p. 370).
- Endnote 9 gives Chalmers's betting strategy: use a quantum device weighted against Everett, so that if Everett is true one descendant still survives (p. 371).
- No new physics or derivation is offered; the chapter is an argument about which existing interpretation fits best with the book's psychophysical principles.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Depends on ch09 for the definition of implementation and the thesis that implementing the right computation suffices for consciousness, and on ch07 for organizational invariance. Argument E is an application of both; if either fails, the superposition principle has no support beyond the gesture at the double-aspect principle (p. 327).
- Depends on ch03 for the claim that quantum mechanics cannot explain consciousness (p. 311), and on ch04 for dualism, which is what would make the Wigner criterion fundamental (p. 316).
- Uses ch08: ubiquitous experience is the first reason for rejecting consciousness-caused collapse (p. 317), and lower-level experiences are one source of other minds in a superposition (p. 328).
- Tension with ch04 and ch05: the book's dualism is epiphenomenalist in tendency, with the physical domain causally closed. Consciousness-caused collapse is the one place in physics where a dualist could give consciousness a causal role, and Chalmers declines it. He is consistent with causal closure, but the rejection leans mainly on the speculative ch08 and on elegance.
- Tension within the chapter: the Everett view is favoured for simplicity, yet making sense of its probabilities needs a measure on minds that is either a further basic fact or unexplained (p. 332). If it is a further basic fact, the simplicity advantage over GRW's two constants shrinks.
- The appeal to brute indexicality (p. 328) answers a question about experience with a refusal to explain, in a book whose starting complaint is that experience is left unexplained. The cases differ, since the indexical fact is not a fact about what exists, but the move is worth marking.
- The closing confidence ranking (p. 333) is the book's own summary of how firmly each part is held: dualism very likely, ubiquity weakly, Everett undecided.

## Open questions

- What does the measure on the space of minds correspond to? Chalmers asks this himself and leaves it (p. 332).
- Does a low-amplitude component implement a computation as fully as a high-amplitude one? Argument E says yes, which is what generates the probability problem.
- How many minds does a superposed brain have? The chapter allows minds for other decompositions and for the whole system (p. 328); with ch08's proliferation of information spaces this multiplies subjects without limit.
- Whether the objection from ubiquitous experience to the Wigner view could be met by a constrained double-aspect principle (ch08, p. 283). Chalmers does not consider this.
- I cannot assess the physics claims (the adequacy of the linearity argument for projected substates, the status of decoherence, the relativistic difficulties of GRW and Bohm) beyond what the text states; they are reported, not verified.
