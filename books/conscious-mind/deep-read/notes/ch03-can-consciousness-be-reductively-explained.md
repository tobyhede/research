# ch03: Can Consciousness Be Reductively Explained? (pp. 91–117)

## Question this chapter answers

Can any explanation given wholly in physical terms account for conscious experience? Chalmers answers no. Reductive explanation of a phenomenon requires that it logically supervene on the physical, in the a priori sense fixed in ch02. Five arguments (two from conceivability, two from epistemology, one from analysis) are offered to show that consciousness does not so supervene. Sections 3 to 7 then apply the result to cognitive models, neurobiological theories, new physics and evolutionary accounts, and conclude that only a nonreductive explanation remains open (p. 117).

The chapter is explicitly restricted to explanation. Ontology (whether materialism is false) and a posteriori necessity are put off to ch04 (p. 92).

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reductive explanation | p. 91 | An explanation given wholly in physical terms. It requires an a priori implication from the physical facts to the high-level facts, that is, logical supervenience according to primary intension (p. 107). Its failure is marked by a further question that the physical story cannot answer (p. 103). |
| Logical supervenience (of consciousness on the physical) | p. 91 | All the microphysical facts entail the facts about consciousness. The necessity involved here is the a priori version, where primary intensions are central; a posteriori necessity is set aside for this chapter (p. 92). |
| Global / local supervenience | p. 91 | Global: the microphysical facts of the whole world entail the facts about consciousness. Local: the microphysical facts about an individual entail them. Chalmers argues locally for convenience and holds that for consciousness the two plausibly stand or fall together. |
| Zombie / zombie twin | p. 92 | A being physically identical to a conscious being, molecule for molecule and in all the low-level properties of a completed physics, that lacks conscious experience altogether. It is functionally and psychologically identical to the original, and conscious in the functional senses only (p. 93). |
| Zombie world | p. 92 | A world physically identical to ours in which there are no conscious experiences at all. |
| Phenomenal zombie / psychological zombie | p. 93 | Psychological zombies (the Hollywood kind) are functionally impaired and lack a psychological sort of consciousness. Phenomenal zombies are physically and functionally identical to us and lack experience. Only the second kind is at issue. |
| Functional organization | p. 94 | The pattern of causal organization embodied in the mechanisms responsible for producing behaviour. It can be realised in nonstandard ways (the population of China, silicon chips). |
| Silicon isomorph | p. 95 | A being organised like me with silicon chips where I have neurons. |
| Conceivability / conceptual coherence | pp. 93, 96 | A description is conceivable when sufficient reflection finds no contradiction in it. The relevant possibility is evaluated by primary intensions, so the a posteriori-necessity gap between conceivability and possibility does not apply (p. 96). Used interchangeably with logical possibility and with intelligibility (p. 93). |
| Inverted spectrum / inverted twin | p. 97 | A physical duplicate whose colour experiences are systematically swapped relative to mine (red experience where I have blue, and so on) while his behaviour and colour words are the same. |
| Epistemic asymmetry | p. 99 | Our knowledge that consciousness exists comes primarily from our own case, with external evidence at best secondary. No such asymmetry holds for life, economies or heights. |
| Functional analysis (of consciousness) | p. 102 | An analysis on which all there is to being conscious is playing a certain functional role (reportability, perceptual discrimination, availability of information). Held to be the only remotely tenable analysis for reductive purposes, and to fail. |
| Structural analysis | p. 103 | An analysis of consciousness as some structure, for instance biochemical. Held to be even more clearly inadequate. |
| Awareness | p. 103 | The functional notion from ch01: accessibility of information in later processing and in the control of behaviour. Distinct from conscious experience. |
| Explanatory gap | p. 104 | Levine's term (1983), adopted for the gap between the physical level and conscious experience that follows from the logical possibility of the same physical facts with different facts about consciousness. |
| Further fact | p. 104 | A fact not explainable by telling the physical story; the accompaniment of a physical process by consciousness is such a fact and must in a sense be taken as brute. |
| Bridging principles / bridge laws | pp. 104-105 | Principles linking physical facts with consciousness. In ordinary reduction the connecting principles are themselves logically supervenient on low-level facts; for consciousness they must be taken as primitive (p. 105). |
| Structure and function; structure and dynamics | pp. 104, 113 | What physical explanation can deliver. At the high level Chalmers speaks of structural and functional properties (p. 104); for fundamental physics he speaks of structure and dynamics (pp. 113-114). Consciousness is said to be neither. |
| Psychological criterion (for consciousness) | p. 111 | The indirect test neuroscience uses to detect consciousness: focus of attention, control of behaviour, most often verbal report. |
| Physics | p. 114 | Stipulated for the purposes of the argument as the fundamental science developed to explain observations of the external world. A wider construal (the science from which everything follows) is rejected as trivialising the question. |
| Taking consciousness seriously | p. 107 | Accepting that first-person experience forces an explanandum on us that third-person observation does not. Its denial is the only consistent way round the chapter's intuitions. |

## Leading propositions

- No explanation given wholly in physical terms can account for conscious experience (p. 91).
- To show this it is enough to show that consciousness is not logically supervenient on the physical, in the a priori sense (pp. 91-92).
- A zombie, a physical duplicate with no experience, is conceptually coherent; the mere intelligibility of the notion establishes the failure of logical supervenience (p. 93).
- The burden of proof lies on whoever claims a description is logically impossible; they must point to where the contradiction lies (p. 94).
- Nonstandard realisations of my functional organisation could coherently lack experience, and nothing about neurons in particular could conceptually force experience in, so the zombie is equally coherent (p. 95).
- A posteriori necessity is irrelevant here; even conceivability of the water-is-not-H2O kind suffices against reductive explanation (p. 96).
- A physically identical world with inverted experiences is coherent. This is weaker than the zombie case: it would leave the existence of consciousness explainable but not the specific character of experiences (pp. 97-99).
- Knowledge of all the physical facts would not lead one to postulate consciousness; eliminativism is unreasonable only because of our own acquaintance with experience (p. 99).
- If consciousness were logically supervenient there would be no epistemic asymmetry and no problem of other minds (pp. 99-100).
- Mary knows all the physical facts and does not know what it is like to see red, so facts about colour experience are not entailed by the physical facts (p. 100).
- There is no analysis of the concept of consciousness, functional or structural, whose satisfaction physical facts could imply (pp. 101-103).
- Since consciousness does not logically supervene, any physical account leaves a further question: why are these processes accompanied by experience? (pp. 103-104).
- Physical explanation suits structure and function; explaining consciousness is not just a matter of explaining structure and function (p. 104).
- An explanation that adds bridging principles to the physical facts is possible but is not reductive (p. 104).
- Physical facts stay relevant: they can give the physical basis of consciousness and help explain its structure (pp. 104-105).
- Life escapes all five arguments; the vitalist's doubt was about whether mechanisms could perform functions, and it dissolved when the functions were explained (pp. 105-106).
- Cognitive models (Baars, Dennett) explain awareness, reportability and attention, and leave untouched why these are accompanied by experience (pp. 108-110).
- Neurobiological theories (Crick and Koch, Edelman) presuppose a link between a psychological criterion and experience, so they cannot explain that link (pp. 111-113).
- New physics will not help: from structure and dynamics only more structure and dynamics follows (pp. 113-114).
- Evolution cannot explain why conscious creatures and not zombies evolved, because selection acts on functional role and the bridging principles are constant over time (pp. 115-116).
- The problem is with the reductive strategy as such, not with the details of current theories; a nonreductive explanation remains possible (pp. 116-117).

## Arguments

**A. The master argument: from failure of logical supervenience to failure of reductive explanation (pp. 91-92, 103-104, 106-107)**

- **Premises**: (p. 107) Reductive explanation of a phenomenon in physical terms requires an a priori implication from the physical facts to the relevant high-level facts. (p. 92) The supervenience relevant to explanation is the a priori one from ch02. (pp. 92-103) Consciousness does not logically supervene on the physical (arguments B to F). (pp. 103-104) Where entailment fails, the further question of why the processes are accompanied by experience always remains.
- **Unstated premises**: that the ch02 account of reductive explanation (explanation as a priori entailment) is the right standard and not merely one standard; that an explanation leaving a conceivable further question is thereby not an explanation of the phenomenon. Chalmers half-states the second on p. 107, where explanation is said to be tied to the limits of human capacity as conceivability is.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 103) No reductive explanation of consciousness can succeed; the accompaniment of physical processes by experience is a further fact (p. 104).

**B. Argument 1: zombies (pp. 92-97)**

- **Premises**: (p. 93) The description of a physical duplicate without experience appears coherent; no contradiction can be discerned. (p. 94) Where no reasonable analysis of the terms points to a contradiction, there is a natural assumption in favour of logical possibility, and the burden lies on the denier. (pp. 94-95) Indirect support: it is coherent that the China-brain or a silicon isomorph lacks experience, so functional organisation does not entail experience. (p. 95) Substituting neurons for silicon cannot conceptually force experience into the conception, because implementational differences are not the sort of thing conceptually relevant to experience. (pp. 95-96) Unimagined neural detail does not matter: it could be relevant only through higher-level properties, and those can be imagined at a coarse level. (p. 96) The a posteriori-necessity gap is irrelevant because explanation runs on primary intensions. (p. 96) The other way conceivability fails is a priori misdescription, as in the Fermat case, and sufficient reflection reveals it.
- **Unstated premises**: that failure to find a contradiction on reflection is good evidence that there is none (ideal and actual reflection are not distinguished); that the concept of consciousness is clear enough for its primary intension to be applied to the zombie scenario; that the intuition is shared widely enough to carry the burden-shifting (he says almost everybody can conceive it, p. 94); that the step from functional isomorph to physical duplicate needs no further argument, because nothing physical beyond function could be conceptually tied to experience.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 95) Consciousness fails to supervene logically on the physical.
- **Status he gives it**: partly a brute intuition, but no more so than the mile-high unicycle (pp. 93-94). He concedes that some find conceivability arguments hard to adjudicate and that arguments 3 to 5 may be the stronger foundation (p. 97).

**C. Argument 2: the inverted spectrum (pp. 97-99)**

- **Premises**: (p. 97) Any positive fact about experience that fails to hold in a physically identical world defeats logical supervenience. (p. 97) A physical duplicate with red and blue experiences swapped is coherent; nothing in the neurophysiology dictates which experience accompanies which processing. (pp. 97-98) Objection from asymmetries in colour space (Harrison, Hardin) answered three ways: a dissociation of feel from role is odd but coherent; the mapping need not use our colour space; and a creature with a symmetrical two-colour space is enough (Shoemaker). (p. 98) Materialists who grant inversion with functional organisation fixed must grant it for a physical replica, in the sense of conceivability relevant to explanation.
- **Unstated premise**: that the extra neurophysiological detail fixed in a full duplicate is no more able than function to logically determine the quality of experience. This is stated as a claim on p. 98 and not argued separately.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 98-99) Consciousness does not logically supervene. The conclusion is strictly weaker than B: someone could accept inversion and reject zombies, in which case the existence of consciousness could be reductively explained and only its specific character could not.

**D. Argument 3: epistemic asymmetry (pp. 99-100)**

- **Premises**: (p. 99) Our grounds for believing in consciousness come solely from our own experience; complete physical knowledge would not lead us to postulate it. (p. 99) The physical facts give only inconclusive indirect evidence (creatures that claim to be conscious), from which an eliminativist conclusion would be the natural one. (p. 99) There is a problem of other minds and no problem of other lives or other economies. (p. 100) A logically supervenient property can be detected straightforwardly on external evidence, with no special role for the first-person case.
- **Unstated premises**: that the asymmetry is in what the facts entail and not only in how concepts of experience are acquired or deployed; that in-principle third-person detectability holds for every logically supervenient property (asserted, with memory as the one worked example).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 100) No collection of facts about complex causation in physical systems adds up to a fact about consciousness.

**E. Argument 4: the knowledge argument (pp. 100-101)**

- **Premises**: (p. 100) Mary knows everything about the neural and physical processes of colour vision and has never seen colour. (p. 100) She does not know what it is like to see red, and no reasoning from the physical facts will tell her. (p. 100) If the physical facts entailed the facts about experience, she could in principle derive them. (pp. 100-101) Parallel cases: the physical facts about a bat or a mouse, or the circuits of a colour-categorising computer, leave open whether there is experience and of what kind. (p. 101) What Mary gains is knowledge of a fact, since she discovers something about the way the world is.
- **Unstated premise**: that Mary's new knowledge is knowledge of a new fact and not an old fact under a new mode of presentation, or a new ability. Chalmers names the ability reply (Lewis, Nemirow) and defers it to ch04 (p. 101); he says most other replies concede the point about a priori entailment.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 101) The physical facts do not logically entail the facts about conscious experience.

**F. Argument 5: absence of analysis (pp. 101-103)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 101-102) Entailment by physical facts needs an analysis of the concept whose satisfaction physical facts could imply. (p. 102) The only remotely tenable candidate is a functional analysis. (p. 102) Functional analyses miss what it means to be a conscious experience; they trivialise the problem, since explaining report or discrimination could be done without explaining experience. (p. 102) Functional concepts are semantically indeterminate (does a mouse have beliefs?), while whether a mouse has experience is not a matter for stipulation. (pp. 102-103) A functional analysis collapses the distinction between awareness and consciousness. (p. 103) A structural analysis is worse: whatever consciousness is, biochemical structure is not what the word means. (p. 103) Other high-level concepts lack crisp definitions and still have rough functional analyses; consciousness does not get into the ballpark.
- **Unstated premises**: that functional and structural analyses exhaust the analyses that could ground physical entailment; that the determinacy of facts about experience (p. 102) is itself secure, when it is a first-person intuition of the same kind as those under dispute.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 103) The concept of consciousness is irreducible, characterisable only in terms of concepts that themselves involve consciousness, so no entailment from physical facts can get started.

**G. Replies to five objections (pp. 105-108)**

- *Standards too high* (p. 105): ordinary bridge laws are not further facts; they logically supervene on low-level facts or can be derived from them. For consciousness the bridging principles must be primitive. Life fails all five tests that consciousness passes: no non-living replica is conceivable, no inverted life, complete physical knowledge gives the biological facts, no asymmetry, and life is functionally analysable.
- *Vitalism* (pp. 105-106): **Premises**: vitalism was mostly driven by doubt that mechanisms could perform the functions of life; that doubt already concedes that function is what needs explaining; the vital spirit was an explanatory construct and can be eliminated by a better explanation; experience is an explanandum, not a construct. **Conclusion**: the analogy fails. The residual vitalist doubt tied to inner life is a doubt about consciousness and has never been overturned (p. 106).
- *Conceivability and possibility* (pp. 106-107): for explanation, conceivability is what matters; if the zombie is conceivable the further question always arises. Whether materialism turns on a priori links is left to ch04.
- *Circular intuitions* (p. 107): he grants that the arguments rest on intuition. The main one is that first-person experience forces an explanandum that third-person observation does not; given that premise most of the arguments follow. The only consistent alternative is to deny the phenomenon.
- *Explanation must stop somewhere* (pp. 107-108): accepted. But taking a psychophysical link for granted is to treat it as fundamental, like a law of physics, which is the nonreductive position.

**H. Against cognitive and neurobiological models: the presupposed-link argument (pp. 108-113)**

- **Premises**: (p. 108) A cognitive model explains how functions are performed; for any model it is a further question why its realisation is accompanied by consciousness. (p. 108) Even an experience meter would establish only a correlation. (p. 111) Neuroscience detects consciousness through a psychological criterion such as report. (p. 111) A theory that gets its purchase by assuming a link between the psychological property and experience does nothing to explain that link.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 109-113) Baars's global workspace, Dennett's two models, Crick and Koch's 40-hertz oscillations and Edelman's re-entrant circuits are at best theories of awareness, reportability, binding or perceptual awareness. Chalmers notes that several of these authors do not claim more (Koch, p. 112; Edelman, p. 113); Dennett is the one who claims the functional account leaves nothing out (p. 110).

**I. Against new physics: structure and dynamics (pp. 113-115)**

- **Premises**: (p. 113) The basic elements of any physical theory come down to the structure and dynamics of physical processes. (p. 114) From structure and dynamics only more structure and dynamics follows. (p. 114) If physics is widened to include laws introduced specifically for consciousness, the explanation is no longer reductive. (pp. 114-115) Quantum proposals (Penrose and Hameroff, nonlocality, collapse by observation) either leave it logically possible that the process occurs without consciousness or assume consciousness in order to explain something else.
- **Unstated premise**: that phenomenal facts are not themselves structural or dynamical facts. This is the conclusion of F restated; the argument has no independent support for it.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 115) Physics-based theories are no better placed than neurobiological and cognitive ones for reductive explanation.

**J. Against evolutionary explanation (pp. 115-116)**

- **Premises**: (p. 115) Selection acts on functional role; the zombie twin performs every function and leaves as many copies of its genes. (p. 116) The principles by which consciousness arises from physical systems are constant over space-time and independent of evolutionary history.
- **Unstated premise**: that the question an evolutionary account is asked to answer is why experience accompanies function at all, not why the functional systems that are in fact conscious were selected. Given natural supervenience, selection for the function would bring experience with it; Chalmers's point is only that this presupposes the bridging principles.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 115) Evolution alone cannot explain why conscious creatures and not zombies evolved.

**Position versus argument.** What he holds: consciousness is real, not logically supervenient, and explicable only nonreductively. Why he says he holds it: the five arguments. What he admits underlies the five arguments: one premise, that there is a first-person explanandum (p. 107). The arguments are therefore better read as five ways of bringing out one intuition than as five independent supports; he says himself that there is some element of redundancy among them (p. 92).

## Evidence offered

- **Thought experiments**: the zombie twin and zombie world (pp. 92-93); the mile-high unicycle as the model of an obvious logical possibility (pp. 93-94); Block's population of China realising a brain (p. 94); the silicon isomorph (p. 95); the Fermat counterexample scenario as a model of misdescription (p. 96); red-blue inversion and the two-colour creature (pp. 97-98); Mary in the black-and-white room (p. 100); the bat and the mouse (pp. 100-101); the colour-categorising computer (p. 101); the experience meter (p. 108); the replica that pops into existence a million years ago (p. 116).
- **Contrast cases**: life, learning, heat, inheritance, as phenomena where function or structure is all there is to explain (pp. 103, 105, 109). The five-point comparison with life on p. 105 is the chapter's main control case.
- **Analogy**: defining world peace as a ham sandwich, for what a functional definition of consciousness does (p. 102); laws of physics taken as fundamental, for the psychophysical link (p. 108).
- **Cited authors, as sources of arguments**: Block 1978 and 1995 (pp. 93-94); Jackson 1982 and Nagel 1974 (p. 100); Levine 1983 for the explanatory gap (p. 104) and Levine 1991 on warm blue (p. 98); Shoemaker 1982 (p. 98); Horgan 1978 on bridge laws (p. 105). Endnotes add Kirk 1974 and Campbell 1970 as earlier zombie and imitation-man discussions (p. 346), Gunderson 1970 on an investigational asymmetry (p. 346).
- **Cited authors, as targets**: Harrison 1973 and Hardin 1987 on colour-space asymmetry (p. 97); Lewis 1990 and Nemirow 1990 on the ability reply (p. 101); Baars 1988 (p. 109); Dennett 1978c, 1991, 1993a (pp. 109-110); Churchland 1995, Johnson-Laird 1988, Shallice (p. 110); Crick and Koch 1990 (p. 111); Edelman 1989, 1992 (pp. 112-113); Penrose 1994, Hameroff 1994 (p. 114); Lahav and Shanks 1992, Hodgson 1988, Stapp 1993 (p. 115).
- **Testimony of the targets**: Koch's interview remark that subjective feelings may have no scientific solution (p. 112); Edelman's statement that a theory starts from the assumption that qualia exist (p. 113). Both are used to show that the neuroscientists do not claim a reductive explanation of experience. Endnote 12 quotes Edelman declining ontological mysteries and reads his quantum-field analogy as compatible with the nonreductive view (p. 347).
- **Empirical material**: almost none is used as evidence. The 40-hertz binding hypothesis and the microtubule proposal are described, not assessed (pp. 111, 114). He says the problem does not lie in the details and that the details did not need to be considered (p. 116).
- **Historical claim, bare**: that vitalism was mostly driven by doubt about whether mechanisms could perform the functions of life, and that such doubts melted away as explanations appeared (p. 106). No historical source is cited; endnote 8 only names Churchland and Dennett as the ones who press the analogy (p. 346).
- **Endnotes that matter**:
  - Note 3: Jacoby's point that conceivability arguments threaten functionalism no more than materialism generally. Chalmers accepts the parity and reverses the moral: it counts against materialism (p. 346).
  - Note 4: the axis swap described in the text would exchange red with yellow, not blue; he calls the detail inessential (p. 346).
  - Note 5: Hardin concedes that such an inversion is outlandish but not conceptually incoherent (p. 346).
  - Note 7: Mary could still have colour experiences in the room (rubbing her eyes), so stipulate colour-blindness from birth instead (p. 346).
  - Note 9: on Dennett's Consciousness Explained. Chalmers locates the crucial argument in the claim that what needs explaining is how things seem, and says this equivocates between a psychological and a phenomenal sense of seem; the point is developed in ch05. He adds that Dennett often runs natural and logical possibility together, and that Dennett's case for machine consciousness is compatible with the nonreductive view (pp. 346-347).
  - Note 10: Crick and Koch have since looked beyond the 40-hertz oscillations; the same considerations apply (p. 347).
  - Note 13: if consciousness were logically supervenient, collapse-by-consciousness interpretations of quantum mechanics could not get started, since there would be no ground for treating consciousness specially in the laws (p. 347).
- **Confident language**: any attempt to demonstrate entailment is "doomed to failure" (p. 101); functional analyses "fail miserably" (p. 102). Compare the more guarded "it does not seem to hold" (p. 107) and "there is good reason to believe" (p. 114).

## Concessions and qualifications

- Zombies are probably not naturally possible; any replica of me in the real world would likely be conscious (p. 93).
- The claim of logical possibility comes down in some ways to a brute intuition (p. 94).
- He suspects the China-brain would in fact be conscious, and argues so in ch07 (p. 95).
- Some find conceivability arguments hard to adjudicate; arguments 3 to 5 may be a stronger foundation (p. 97).
- Inverted spectra establish less than zombies (pp. 98-99).
- Physical facts give some indirect evidence for consciousness, namely the reports of organisms (p. 99).
- There is probably a continuum of experience from the very faint to the very rich (p. 102).
- Physical accounts remain relevant and can explain the structure of experience (pp. 104-105).
- All the arguments are in a sense based on intuition (p. 107).
- Something must be taken for granted in explaining consciousness (pp. 107-108).
- The models criticised are useful as accounts of cognition and give indirect insight into consciousness (pp. 109-110).
- Fundamental physics might still play a key role in a nonreductive theory of consciousness (p. 115).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch01**: the split between phenomenal and psychological concepts, and between consciousness and awareness, is what lets him say the zombie is psychologically identical and conscious in the functional senses (p. 93) and that functional analysis collapses two notions (p. 103). If the ch01 split is rejected, arguments B and F lose their footing.
- **Depends on ch02**: the whole chapter runs on ch02's thesis that reductive explanation requires a priori entailment and that the relevant modality is fixed by primary intensions (pp. 92, 96, 107). The chapter gives no new defence of that thesis. The technical note on p. 92 and the reply on p. 96 both say that a posteriori necessity can be ignored here only because of it.
- **Feeds ch04**: the same zombie and inversion scenarios become premise 2 of the argument against materialism (p. 117). Three things are explicitly deferred there: a posteriori necessity (p. 92), the replies to Jackson including the ability reply (p. 101), and whether materialism turns on a priori links (p. 107). The taking of the psychophysical link as fundamental (p. 108) is ch04's naturalistic dualism in outline.
- **Feeds ch05**: the zombie is said to report and introspect exactly as I do (p. 93), and the physical facts are said to include organisms that claim to be conscious (p. 99). That is the raw material of the paradox of phenomenal judgment. Endnote 9's charge that Dennett equivocates on seeming is argued in ch05 (pp. 346-347).
- **Feeds ch06 and ch07**: physical accounts explaining the structure of experience (p. 105) is the programme of ch06; the suspicion that the China-brain and silicon isomorph are in fact conscious (p. 95) is argued in ch07. Note the two-level use of these cases: logically they may lack experience, naturally they would have it.
- **Term shift (my observation)**: the explanatory target moves from structure and function (p. 104) to structure and dynamics (pp. 113-114, 116). The first pair describes high-level explananda, the second the content of fundamental theory. The slide matters because the claim that function is entailed by physics is argued in ch02, while the claim that physics contains nothing but structure and dynamics is asserted here.
- **Term shift (my observation)**: conceivable, coherent, intelligible, logically possible and conceptually possible are used as equivalents throughout pp. 93-96. That equivalence is the ch02 thesis; a reader who distinguishes prima facie from ideal conceivability will find the zombie argument compressed at exactly the point where it is contested.
- **Internal tension (my observation)**: the five arguments are presented as together making a strong case (p. 92), yet Objection 4 concedes they all fall out of one premise (p. 107). Their number adds less than it appears; an opponent who denies the first-person explanandum denies all five at once.
- **Internal tension (my observation)**: Argument 3 says the physical facts would make eliminativism the natural conclusion (p. 99). The reports that constitute the indirect evidence are themselves physically explained, so the first-person acquaintance that blocks eliminativism does no work in producing them. Chalmers takes this up as the central difficulty of ch05.
- **Internal tension (my observation)**: the determinacy claim (either there is something it is like to be a mouse or there is not, p. 102) sits beside the admission of a continuum from faint to rich experience (p. 102). The first is used to distinguish consciousness from vague functional concepts; the second invites the question of whether the lower end of the continuum is sharp.

## Open questions

- What is the standard of reflection for conceivability? The Fermat case (p. 96) shows that a scenario can seem coherent and be incoherent, with the error lying in the a priori domain. How would one tell that the zombie case is not like that, other than by failing to find the contradiction?
- Is the burden-of-proof rule on p. 94 neutral? A materialist could say the same rule favours the coherence of a world in which the physical facts necessitate experience.
- Does the inference from functional isomorph to physical duplicate (p. 95) beg the question against someone who holds that the concept of experience is tied a priori to biological or physical make-up? Chalmers says such a tie is not available (p. 96); is that shown or asserted?
- Argument 3 needs every logically supervenient property to be detectable from the third person in principle (p. 100). Is that true of indexical or perspectival facts, which ch02 treats as a separate case?
- Does the claim that physics yields only structure and dynamics (p. 114) survive the view, mentioned in ch05 (p. 172), on which phenomenal properties constitute the intrinsic nature of the physical? If physics is silent on intrinsic nature, the stipulated definition of physics on p. 114 is carrying much of the load.
- The vitalism reply rests on a historical claim about what motivated vitalists (p. 106). Is it accurate? Synthesis should mark this as unverified from the text.
- The chapter says a nonreductive explanation would need radical changes in how we think about the structure of the world (p. 117). Which changes, and are they supplied in ch04 and ch06 to ch08?
