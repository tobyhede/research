# ch06: The Coherence Between Consciousness and Cognition (pp. 200–232)

## Question this chapter answers

If consciousness cannot be reductively explained, what would a theory of it look like, and where can such a theory start given that experience cannot be measured from outside? Chalmers's answer: a theory is a set of psychophysical laws, found by inference to the best explanation from first-person data plus plausibility constraints. The first such laws are high-level principles of coherence between consciousness and cognition: consciousness goes with awareness (direct availability for global control), and the structure of each mirrors the other. These principles already underwrite empirical consciousness research, and simplicity supports treating them as laws of nature with no extra ingredient.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nonreductive theory | p. 200 | A theory that takes consciousness as fundamental and says how it relates to everything else, without deriving its existence from something more basic. Explains specific instances of consciousness, not why consciousness exists. |
| Psychophysical laws | p. 200 | Laws governing how consciousness depends on physical systems; they underwrite natural supervenience and are part of the basic furniture of nature, on a par with physical laws. Split into fundamental laws (simple, the ultimate target) and nonbasic or high-level laws (the starting point) (p. 201). |
| Plausibility constraints | pp. 203-204 | Non-empirical constraints on theory choice: simplicity, non-arbitrariness, homogeneity of laws in space and time, dependence of experience on internal physical state only, reliability of reports and memories. |
| Reliability principle | p. 205 | Second-order judgments about consciousness are by and large correct. Not exceptionless (pp. 205-206). |
| Detectability principle | p. 205 | Where there is an experience, we generally have the capacity to form a second-order judgment about it. |
| First-order judgment | p. 206 | A contentful state about the world (a red object is there), parallel to experience and not about experience. Later demoted: strictly these states are registrations (pp. 218-219). |
| Second-order judgment | p. 205 | A judgment about an experience (I am having an auditory sensation). |
| Awareness | p. 206 | The psychological correlate of consciousness. Initial sense: information directly accessible and available for deliberate control of behaviour and for verbal report (p. 206). Revised 1: access must be direct, excluding nonoccurrent thoughts (pp. 208-209). Revised 2: direct availability for global control, dropping the report requirement (p. 212). Revised 3: read dispositionally, available whether or not accessed (p. 215). Revised 4: its contents are first-order registrations, not judgments (p. 219). |
| Principle of structural coherence | p. 211 | The structure of consciousness is mirrored by the structure of awareness and conversely. Covers explicit structure (visual-field geometry), implicit structure (difference structures such as colour space), intensity and resolution (pp. 209-211). |
| Difference structure | p. 210 | A space of differences between possible experiences, matched by a space of differences between representations. |
| Access consciousness | p. 214 | Block's term: content poised for reasoning and for rational control of action and speech. Roughly Chalmers's initial awareness, with more weight on rationality. |
| First-order / second-order functional accounts | p. 217 | First-order: consciousness goes with a first-order cognitive state playing a restricted role. Second-order: it requires a second-order state (higher-order thought or perception). |
| First-order registration | pp. 218-219 | A representational state that carries content, is available to the subject and plays a role in the system, but need not be endorsed or conceptualised; the immediate product of perceptual and introspective processes before rational integration. |
| Bridging principle / epistemic lever | p. 222 | A pre-experimental principle giving a physical-level criterion for the presence of consciousness; not itself testable from the third-person viewpoint. |

| X-factor | p. 229 | A hypothetical extra ingredient beyond awareness required for consciousness (nationality, location, identity, biology, absence of homunculi). |

## Leading propositions

- A theory of consciousness can exist without reductive explanation: it admits consciousness as fundamental and states psychophysical laws, which explain specific instances of experience but not why experience exists (p. 200).
- The laws are brute at some level, as physical laws are; this is the price of a theory, and the aim is to take as little for granted as possible by systematising under simple laws (p. 201).
- High-level, nonbasic laws come first and constrain the fundamental laws, as thermodynamics preceded statistical mechanics (pp. 201-202).
- The data problem is real but not fatal: each of us has first-person data, and inference to the best explanation plus simplicity and other plausibility constraints narrows the candidates (pp. 202-204).
- The assumption that reports by and large reflect experience cannot be proved; it is an inference from one's own case and a methodological constraint without which no theory is possible (pp. 203-204).
- A theory of consciousness will always be more speculative than other science, because rigorous intersubjective testing is impossible (pp. 204-205).
- Reliability and detectability hold as regularities with exceptions (pp. 205-206).
- The basic coherence is between consciousness and first-order states: when we have an experience we are aware of its contents, and with awareness suitably restricted the converse holds too (pp. 206-209).
- Every detail and every structural relation in experience is represented in awareness, because each is available to guide report and behaviour (pp. 209-211).
- Awareness is best characterised as direct availability for global control; blindsight, dreaming and Block's cases can be handled under that characterisation (pp. 212-215).
- Pathological cases cannot count against the coherence, because judgments about the presence or absence of experience in them are drawn on functional grounds (p. 214).
- The account is a nonreductive functionalism about the cognitive basis of consciousness; among functional criteria, first-order availability accounts fit experience better than higher-order thought accounts (pp. 215-218).
- Structural coherence licenses a nonreductive explanation of the structure of experience from processing accounts; it does not explain intrinsic quality or why there is experience at all (pp. 220-221).
- Any empirical conclusion about experience rests on a bridging principle that precedes the experiments; the awareness principle is the general one, and reportability is a special case (pp. 222-223).
- Proposed neural or cognitive correlates are correlates only in so far as they subserve global availability; empirical work cannot yield a universal principle that contradicts the bridging principle it relies on (pp. 224-227).
- The coherence principles are plausibly laws of nature, though neither fundamental nor perhaps strict (p. 228).
- Awareness is necessary for consciousness on grounds of parsimony, and sufficient because any further X-factor is arbitrary and adds complexity without explanatory gain (pp. 229-231).
- The same inference gives the best available answer to the problem of other minds and extends experience to dogs, mice and perhaps flies; below that the notion of awareness becomes indeterminate (pp. 231-232).

## Arguments

**A. A nonreductive theory is possible and scientific in form (pp. 200-202)**

- **Premises**: (p. 200) Consciousness supervenes naturally but not logically on the physical (from Part II). (p. 200) Natural supervenience must be underwritten by laws. (p. 200) Physics takes mass, space and time as fundamental and still explains specific phenomena by laws. (p. 201) All explanation takes something for granted; nothing privileges physical laws as the only things that may be taken for granted.
- **Unstated premises**: that the dependence of experience on the physical is regular enough to be captured by a small set of simple laws (he says we have good reason to believe basic structure is simple, p. 201, without giving the reason for the psychophysical case); that explanation by subsumption under brute laws is explanation enough.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 200-201) A theory of consciousness consists of psychophysical laws, ideally few and simple, which explain particular experiences given physical facts.

**B. The data problem does not make the theory pseudoscience (pp. 202-205)**

- **Premises**: (p. 202) Each person knows their own experiences and the associated physical processes; that is a set of regularities to be explained. (p. 202) No set of simple laws yet generates even these, so the constraint is not trivial. (p. 203) Rival theories compatible with the data (solipsism, odd-numbered-years theories) are ruled out as they are elsewhere in science, by arbitrariness and complexity. (pp. 203-204) The reliability of reports and memories gives third-person data. (p. 204) Untestability bites only if two equally simple, equally plausible theories both fit.
- **Unstated premises**: that first-person knowledge of one's own experience is itself reliable enough to serve as data (this rests on ch05's account of acquaintance); that simplicity is truth-conducive for psychophysical laws as it is taken to be for physical ones; that one subject's case is a fair sample.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 204-205) The search for a theory is legitimate, though the result will be more speculative than other sciences.

**C. Where there is consciousness there is awareness (pp. 206-208)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 206-207) I can comment on and direct behaviour by each detail of what I experience. (p. 207) Systematic availability of information implies an internal state carrying that content. (p. 207) The same holds for hallucination, pain, emotion and thought.
- **Unstated premises**: that there is no experienced detail that is unavailable for report or control. The argument moves from the fact that I can report the details I attend to, to the claim that every detail is represented. Block's Sperling case (p. 215) is a challenge to exactly this step.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 207-208) When we have an experience we are aware of its contents; a first-order state accompanies every experience.

**D. Where there is (suitably restricted) awareness there is consciousness (pp. 208-209)**

- **Premises**: (p. 208) Reportable, behaviour-guiding content generally has a corresponding experience. (p. 208) Counterexample: stored beliefs (Clinton is president) are accessible without experience. (pp. 208-209) Stored beliefs differ functionally: they must be called up, so access is less direct. (p. 209) Build directness into the definition; then occurrent thoughts qualify, and there is something it is like to think them.
- **Unstated premises**: that for every case of availability without experience some functional difference can be found and written into the definition; that this is discovery of a natural correlate and not gerrymandering.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 209) With awareness appropriately defined, consciousness is always accompanied by awareness and conversely.

**E. Structural coherence (pp. 209-211)**

- **Premises**: (p. 209) Experience has detailed structure. (pp. 209-210) Each structural detail can be reflected in behavioural capacities, so it must be represented. (p. 210) Implicit structure such as the three dimensions of colour space is matched in processing (Hardin). (p. 211) Relations between experiences are communicable even if intrinsic quality is not (Schlick). (p. 211) If awareness is refined so that it is always accompanied by experience, the converse mirroring is plausible.
- **Unstated premise**: that communicability of a relation requires that the very relation be represented in the control system, with the same structure.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 211) The structure of consciousness mirrors and is mirrored by the structure of awareness.

**F. Problem cases do not break the coherence (pp. 213-215)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 213-214) In blindsight, information is available only under prompting and forced choice, so it is not directly available for global control; either there is no experience and no awareness, or weak experience and weak awareness. (p. 214) Our evidence about experience in pathologies is itself functional. (p. 214) Dream content can be treated counterfactually: it would be available if control processes were enabled. (p. 215) Block's drill and Sperling cases are cases of information available but not accessed.
- **Unstated premise**: that the dispositional and counterfactual readings are fixed in advance and not chosen case by case to save the principle.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 214) Such cases can only bolster and refine the principle, never damage it.

**G. First-order availability beats higher-order thought as the cognitive basis (pp. 216-218)***

- **Premises**: (pp. 216-217) There is little reason to think we form a second-order judgment about every detail of the visual field; a system that did would be redundant, and evolution would have no reason to build it. (p. 217) Being conscious of all our states holds only in the weak sense of acquaintance. (pp. 217-218) The intermediate dispositional version (capacity to cause a second-order judgment) demands conceptual sophistication that babies and animals may lack.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 218) Availability for global control is the better functional criterion; reductive accounts by Kirk, Dretske and Tye converge on some availability constraint, and a property dualist can treat them as candidate theories of the cognitive basis.

**H. Coherence principles do the explanatory and evidential work in empirical research (pp. 219-227)**

- **Premises**: (p. 220) Processing accounts explain the structure of awareness; structural coherence carries that over to experience. (p. 221) That leaves intrinsic quality and the existence of experience unexplained. (p. 222) Only physical processes are observed, so any conclusion about experience needs a bridging principle. (p. 225) All evidence that a process correlates with experience comes through its link to report and awareness. (p. 227) The bridging principle bears the entire burden of the inference.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 225-227) Specific correlates (40-hertz oscillations, global workspace, high-quality representations, time-on) are derivative on the awareness correlation; no such finding can support a universal principle at odds with it, and the search for a neural correlate will not yield a universal theory.

**I. The X-factor argument: awareness suffices (pp. 228-231)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 228-229) The correlations in my own case are too detailed to be chance; some law underlies them. (p. 229) Necessity: all known instances of consciousness come with awareness, and we could have no evidence of an instance without it; parsimony says there are none. (p. 229) Any law therefore has the form awareness plus something, or awareness alone. (pp. 229-230) Every candidate something, including biology and non-homuncular implementation, is arbitrary and explains none of the phenomena, whereas awareness at least helps explain phenomenal judgments. (p. 230) The pull towards a physical X-factor comes from combining the further-fact intuition with materialism; once irreducible laws are accepted the X-factor is redundant. (pp. 230-231) Simplicity favours the law without the extra clause.
- **Unstated premises**: that awareness alone is the simplest law, although awareness is admitted to be a high-level and underdetermined construct (p. 228); that the generalisation from one subject is legitimate; that the opponent's reason for favouring biology is the one Chalmers diagnoses and not, for instance, that every known conscious system is biological.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 231) Awareness gives rise to consciousness without qualification. He rates the argument as tentative and reliant on simplicity, and promises more concrete arguments in ch07 (p. 231).

**Position versus argument.** The position is a dualist functionalism: experience is not a functional state but lawfully accompanies one. The argument for it in this chapter is inference to the best explanation from first-person regularities (p. 231), which he distinguishes from proof.

## Evidence offered

- **Analogy with physics**: fundamental features taken as given and related by laws (p. 200); T-shirt laws (p. 201); thermodynamics before statistical mechanics (p. 201); unification of electricity and magnetism (p. 202); a clause in Newton's third law exempting gold, as a picture of an X-factor (p. 231).
- **Analogy with other underdetermined theory choices**: evolution against a world created fifty years ago with fossils intact; simple against jury-rigged quantum theories (p. 203).
- **First-person observation**: the red book on the table (p. 206); the occurrent thought that Clinton is president (pp. 208-209); the geometry of the visual field (p. 209).
- **Empirical work cited**: Hardin 1988 on colour space (p. 210); Weiskrantz 1986 on blindsight (p. 213); Sperling 1960 on the letter array (p. 215); Humphrey 1992 and Weiskrantz 1992 on near-superblindsight cases where experience seems present (p. 215); Logothetis and Schall 1989 on monkeys (p. 223); Crick and Koch 1990, Baars 1988, Farah 1994, Libet 1993 on correlates (p. 224); Shallice 1972 and Schacter 1989 (pp. 226-227); Akins 1993 on bats and Cheney and Seyfarth 1990 on monkeys (p. 222).
- **Philosophers engaged**: Schlick 1938 on form and content (p. 211); Block 1995 (pp. 214-215); Dennett 1993b on cerebral celebrity (pp. 216, 225); Rosenthal 1996 and Lycan 1995 on higher-order accounts (pp. 216-217); Kirk, Dretske, Tye (p. 218); Van Gulick 1993 and Clark 1993, whose claim that structure closes the explanatory gap he rejects (pp. 220-221); Dennett 1978b on dreams (p. 214).
- **Illustrative cases**: Macbeth's dagger (p. 207); the background drill (p. 215); Dretske's seven fingers judged as eight (p. 219); optical illusions as registration contradicting judgment (p. 219); the dog and the fire hydrant (p. 231).
- **From the endnotes**:
  - Note 1: reliability yields a psychophysical law only if second-order judgments are read relationally (the sort of experience usually caused by red objects); the belief using the qualitative concept does not logically supervene on the physical, so its correctness is not a psychophysical law (p. 361).
  - Note 4: structural coherence is close to Jackendoff's hypothesis of computational sufficiency (p. 361). Note 3 cites Nagel 1974 for the accessibility of structural features (p. 361).
  - Note 8: Carruthers 1992 canvasses the intermediate dispositional higher-order proposal as holding with natural necessity (pp. 361-362).
  - Note 9: the registration/judgment split parallels Dretske's phenomenal/doxastic split; Chalmers keeps three kinds of state (judgments, registrations, phenomenal states) where Dretske has two (p. 362).
  - Note 10: content of awareness and of experience is nonconceptual, against McDowell (pp. 362-363); experience is plausibly laden with representational content by its own nature, following Siewert (p. 363); that content must be narrow if experience supervenes on organisation (p. 364); phenomenal properties are not exhausted by representational content, since content might stay constant across functional isomorphs with spectrum inversion (p. 364).
  - Note 12: psychophysics has stated such bridging principles explicitly (Brindley's linking hypotheses, Teller's linking propositions, Müller's 1896 axioms), and Chalmers reads Müller's axioms as consequences of structural coherence (p. 365). Their status is disputed in psychophysics along lines that parallel the philosophy of mind, and the science proceeds without settling it (pp. 365-366).
- **Confident language against tentative language**: the correlations are such that there must be an underlying law (pp. 228-229), while the X-factor argument is called somewhat tentative (p. 231) and the principles possibly not strict (p. 228).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch04**: the whole programme presupposes natural without logical supervenience and the falsity of materialism (pp. 200, 230). The X-factor diagnosis works only for a reader who has already accepted that no physical fact explains consciousness (p. 230).
- **Depends on ch05**: judgments, first-order and second-order, are the vocabulary of the chapter, and acquaintance is invoked against Rosenthal (p. 217). Note 1 imports ch05's relational reading of phenomenal concepts (p. 361).
- **Tension with ch05 (my observation; ch05 not read in this pass, check against its note)**: ch05 held, as I understand it, that phenomenal judgments can be explained without reference to consciousness. Here the reliability of those judgments is the first datum for a theory (pp. 203-205). If the judgments would be made whether or not experience were present, their reliability is not explained by the mechanism that produces them; Chalmers treats it as a brute lawful regularity and a methodological constraint (pp. 203, 206), which is a weaker footing than the text's confident use of reports suggests.
- **Feeds ch07**: the X-factor argument is called a first version of the invariance principle, to be replaced by thought experiments (p. 231). ch07's fading and dancing qualia arguments rely on the coherence between judgment and experience set out here.
- **Feeds ch08**: coherence principles are constraints on fundamental laws (p. 228); the remark that awareness gives out lower down the information-processing scale (p. 232) is the point at which ch08's information-based speculation takes over.
- **Feeds ch09**: the dualist functionalism reached here (p. 231) is the basis for the later defence of strong AI.
- **Shift in the term awareness (my observation)**: the definition is revised four times (pp. 208-209, 212, 215, 219), each time to remove a mismatch with consciousness. The result is then described as a remarkable correlation too specific to be an accident (p. 228). Part of the fit is produced by the refinement, so the evidential weight of the correlation is less than the wording implies. Chalmers concedes that the project can only go so far without an experience meter (p. 212).
- **Dispositional reading against the directness requirement (my observation)**: the stored Clinton belief is excluded because it must be called up (pp. 208-209); the unattended drill and the unreported Sperling letters are included because the information was available though not accessed (p. 215). The text does not say what separates calling up from accessing.
- **Unfalsifiability by cases (my observation)**: Chalmers says outright that pathological cases cannot count against the principle (p. 214) and that data cannot contradict the bridging principle (p. 227). He presents this as a fact about our epistemic position; it also means the principle's support is entirely first-person plus simplicity.
- **Structure versus quality**: structural coherence fixes relational structure and leaves intrinsic quality open, as structure-preserving inversion shows (p. 221). ch07 argues that organisation nonetheless fixes quality by natural law, so the open slot is closed there by a different argument.
- **Note 10 and ch07**: note 10 uses spectrum inversion between functional isomorphs against representationalism (p. 364), while ch07 argues such inversion is naturally impossible. The two are consistent only if note 10 needs logical possibility alone; the note does not say which modality it intends.

## Open questions

- Is awareness alone really the simplest candidate law, given that awareness is a vague, high-level functional notion (p. 228)? A simplicity comparison between laws needs the laws stated in basic terms, which Chalmers defers to ch08.
- How much weight can one case bear? The basic evidence is ultimately my own case (p. 228). The homogeneity assumption (p. 204) does the generalising; what supports it other than the wish for a theory?
- Does the argument that every experienced detail is represented (pp. 206-207, 209-210) survive cases of overflow, where experience may be richer than what is accessible? The dispositional reply (p. 215) needs an independent test of availability.
- If reports are data only by a methodological constraint (pp. 203-204, 206), what would count as discovering that the constraint is false?
- Dream experience is handled by a counterfactual about disabled control systems (p. 214). Does the same counterfactual make other disconnected states aware?
- Where does awareness give out (p. 232)? The chapter attributes experience to flies and stops; synthesis should check how ch08 handles the lower bound.
- Chalmers allows that the coherence laws may have exceptions around the edges (p. 228). Is a non-strict law compatible with the X-factor argument, which rejects qualified laws as arbitrary (p. 231)?
