# ch08: Consciousness and Information: Some Speculation (pp. 259–292)

## Question this chapter answers

The coherence principles and organizational invariance (ch06, ch07) are high-level regularities. What might the fundamental psychophysical laws beneath them look like? Chalmers's answer is a sketch, which he calls a prototheory: information has two aspects, a physical one and a phenomenal one, and the basic laws connect experience to physically realized information. He flags the chapter as the most speculative in the book and the part most likely to be wrong (p. 260).

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fundamental (basic) psychophysical law | p. 260 | A law connecting basic properties of experience with simple physical features, precise enough that with the physical facts it fixes the phenomenal facts, and from which nonbasic laws such as coherence and invariance follow. Contrasted with the high-level principles of ch06 and ch07. |
| Prototheory | p. 260 | A skeleton around which a theory might be built: suggestions about the constructs in the basic laws and their broad shape, not a set of laws. |
| Information | p. 261 | Shannon's formal or syntactic notion: a state selected from an ensemble of possibilities. Not semantic information; nothing here is information about anything (p. 263). |
| Information space | p. 261 | An abstract space of information states with a basic structure of difference relations between them; fully characterised by that difference structure. |
| Relational structure | p. 263 | The difference structure among the states an element can take (two-state, n-state, continuum, general topological space). |
| Combinatorial structure | p. 263 | The internal structure of a complex state built from elements, each falling in its own subspace (a ten-bit message; a function over a continuum). |
| Physically realized information | p. 264 | Information in Bateson's sense, a difference that makes a difference: physical states grouped by their effects along a causal pathway onto a space of possible effects. Always relative to a pathway and an effect space. Deliberately left informal (p. 266). |
| Phenomenally realized information | pp. 266-267 | Experiences fall into information spaces through their intrinsic qualities and the similarity and difference relations among them, not through any causal criterion. |
| Double-aspect principle | p. 268 | Information in the actual world has a physical and a phenomenal aspect. Every phenomenal state realizes an information state that is also realized physically; conversely, for at least some physically realized information spaces, physical realization brings phenomenal realization. |
| Explanatory coherence | p. 271 | A requirement on a theory of consciousness: the reductive account of why we judge that we are conscious and the nonreductive account of consciousness should cohere, with the same features of processing central to both. |
| Phenomenal manifold / perceptual manifold | p. 276 | The space of an animal's experiences and the space of perceptual distinctions it can make and use; the hypothesis is that one corresponds to the other. |
| Protophenomenal property | pp. 280-281 | A property more basic than phenomenal properties, from which experience might be constituted; simple systems might have these instead of experience proper. Introduced in ch04. |
| Panpsychism | p. 281 | The view that everything has a mind. Chalmers accepts the label with caveats: experience may fall short of mind, rocks are not themselves subjects, and complex experience need not be a sum of simple experiences. |
| It from bit | p. 284 | The physicists' suggestion (Wheeler, Fredkin) that physics deals ultimately in information. Chalmers's reading: fundamental physical states are individuated only as positions in information spaces, by their relations and effects. |
| Microphenomenal property | p. 287 | Shorthand for the phenomenal or protophenomenal intrinsic property that, on the Russellian reading, lies behind each instantiation of a fundamental physical feature. |
| Grain problem | p. 288 | Sellars's problem, turned on the Russellian view: how smooth, homogeneous macroscopic experience could be constituted by a vast collection of microphenomenal properties. |

## Leading propositions

- The principles reached so far (awareness coherence, structural coherence, organizational invariance) cannot be fundamental laws: they are high-level, vague at the edges, and leave the psychophysical connection grossly underdetermined (p. 260).
- A final theory needs basic psychophysical laws that are precise, leave nothing underdetermined, and entail the nonbasic laws (p. 260).
- The notion that links the two domains is information in Shannon's syntactic sense, formalised as information spaces with relational and combinatorial structure (pp. 261-263).
- Information is physically realized wherever physical differences make a difference along a causal pathway; it is information only insofar as it can be processed (pp. 264-266).
- Experiences realize information spaces directly, by their intrinsic similarities and differences (pp. 266-267).
- Whenever an information space is realized phenomenally, the same space is realized physically in the experience's substrate (p. 267).
- The double-aspect principle is proposed as the basic template: information has a physical and a phenomenal aspect (p. 268). It is a template, not a theory, and leaves open which physical information spaces it applies to (pp. 268-269).
- The principle is compatible with structural coherence and organizational invariance, but does not yet predict structural coherence in full (p. 270).
- Physically realized information may be the most natural feature that is both organizationally invariant and simple enough for a fundamental law (p. 271).
- A system with direct access only to its location in an information space would judge its states to be immediate, ineffable and brutely qualitative; this is the start of a reductive account of phenomenal judgments (pp. 272-274).
- By explanatory coherence, the information states that explain the judgments are the natural candidates for the basis of consciousness (pp. 274-275).
- If the principle is unrestricted, experience is ubiquitous: a thermostat has three maximally simple phenomenal states (pp. 275-276).
- There is no principled place for experience to wink out as systems get simpler, and a fundamental property would be expected to be widespread (pp. 277, 279-280).
- The resulting view is a variety of panpsychism, but panpsychism is not the foundation of the view; naturalistic dualism with psychophysical laws is (p. 281).
- The principle may need a constraint, such as amplification or a restriction on the kind of causation; none is settled (p. 283).
- Ontologically the principle can be read deflationarily, as ordinary property dualism with a shared structure (p. 284), or seriously, with physics as information viewed from outside and experience as the same information viewed from inside (p. 287).
- A physics of pure information faces two problems: it leaves out the intrinsic nature of experience, and primitive differences with nothing they are differences in may be incoherent (p. 286). Grounding physical information in phenomenal or protophenomenal properties would answer both at once (p. 287).
- The hardest problem for that reading is deriving macroscopic phenomenology from microphenomenal properties (pp. 288-289).
- Chalmers thinks it more likely than not that the key to a fundamental theory lies elsewhere (p. 291), and that the idea's real defect is that its predictions are too unspecific, not that it is untestable (p. 292).

## Arguments

**A. Why the earlier principles are not the final theory (p. 260)**

- **Premises**: (p. 260) Fundamental laws connect simple features and are precise. (p. 260) Awareness is a high-level concept with vague boundaries; organizational invariance is stated at a level far from fundamental. (p. 260) The principles do not say what organization gives rise to experience, how simple it can be, or what the specific character of an experience will be.
- **Unstated premises**: that the psychophysical laws must have the same shape as physical laws (few, simple, exceptionless, with higher-level regularities derivable from them). This is the methodological assumption of the whole of Part III, carried from ch04 and ch06; nothing in this chapter argues for it.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 260) Simpler laws must lie beneath the principles, and the principles act as constraints on them.

**B. The double-realization argument (pp. 266-268)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 266-267) Experiences have a structure of similarity and difference, so they realize information spaces. (p. 267) Our reports and discriminations vary systematically with that structure. (pp. 267-268) So there must be physical differences in the brain that are transmitted and yield an isomorphic space of effects; that is the same information space realized physically.
- **Unstated premises**: that the structure of experience is exactly mirrored in what is available for report and control. This is the principle of structural coherence from ch06, so the observation is not independent support for the double-aspect principle; it restates a law already assumed. Also: that sameness of abstract structure is a substantive link. Any two domains related by a lawful structure-preserving map share an information space in this sense.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 268) The double life of information spaces corresponds to a duality at a deep level, and double realization may be the key to the psychophysical connection. Chalmers words this as a suggestion, not a deduction.

**C. Compatibility with the earlier constraints (pp. 270-271)**

- **Premises**: (p. 270) The structure of experience is the structure of a phenomenally realized information space; the structure of awareness is by definition a set of differences that make a difference to later processing. (p. 270) A system realizes an information space in virtue of its functional organization, so a fine-grained functional isomorph realizes the same space.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 270-271) The double-aspect principle is compatible with structural coherence and with organizational invariance. Chalmers concedes it does not predict structural coherence until the relevant information spaces are narrowed down (p. 270).
- **Unstated premise**: that information is simple enough to figure in a fundamental law even though it is defined relative to a causal pathway and a space of effects, both of which need individuating first.

**D. The argument from explanatory coherence (pp. 271-275)**

- **Premises**: (p. 271) Phenomenal judgments can be reductively explained (from ch05). (p. 271) It would be an unreasonable coincidence if the explanation of the judgments and the explanation of consciousness were unrelated. (pp. 272-274) The best sketch of the explanation of the judgments is that central processes have access only to locations in an information space, not to the stimulus or the neural medium, so the states strike the system as immediate, ineffable and qualitative.
- **Unstated premises**: that the just-so story is roughly the true explanation, which Chalmers admits it is not yet (p. 272); and that a design in which access comes as hunches is less natural (p. 273), which is an appeal to engineering plausibility.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 274-275) Information states are the natural basis for consciousness itself, and positing a phenomenal aspect of that information is what makes the judgments correct.

**E. The continuity argument for simple experience (pp. 276-279)**

- **Premises**: (p. 276) Mice are plausibly conscious, with a phenomenal manifold matching their perceptual manifold. (p. 277) As we descend to fish and slugs, either experience vanishes abruptly, or it falls out of step with perception, or it thins out along with perception; the third is at least as plausible. (p. 277) A thermostat is that information processing in its simplest form. (p. 279) It is implausible that whether a system is conscious could be indeterminate, so the cut-off cannot be treated like baldness.
- **Unstated premises**: that information processing, and not some other property that also varies along the scale (a nervous system, metabolism, a unified self-model), is what experience tracks. The argument assumes its conclusion's key variable. Also that the scale from human to thermostat is a single dimension.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 277) It is not unreasonable that a thermostat has experience; Chalmers states this is not a demonstration.

**F. Replies to the charge of craziness (pp. 277-279)**

- Each reply has the same form: the feature that seems to bar a thermostat from experience (no room for consciousness, complete understanding of its workings, it can be built without adding consciousness, no subject inside) applies equally to a brain once consciousness is granted not to be logically supervenient (pp. 278-279). So the nonreductive view removes the usual grounds for the intuition.

**G. From fundamentality to ubiquity (pp. 279-280)**

- **Premises**: (p. 279) Experience is a fundamental property (from ch04). (pp. 279-280) All other known fundamental properties occur in simple systems and throughout the universe.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 280) Other things equal, it is more plausible that experience is widespread. Chalmers grants there is no contradiction in a fundamental property being rare.

**H. The Russellian grounding argument (pp. 284-287)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 284-285) Physics individuates mass, charge and the like only by their place in a space of differences and their effects; it says nothing about what they are. (p. 286) A world of pure differences may not be coherent, and two physical properties with the same informational structure must differ in something. (p. 286) Experience has an intrinsic nature that is more than a location in an information space.
- **Unstated premises**: that there must be intrinsic properties underlying physical dispositions at all; and that the intrinsic nature we know in experience is of the same kind as whatever grounds mass or charge. Chalmers offers the match as attractive economy, not as proved.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 287) The information spaces of physics may be grounded in phenomenal or protophenomenal properties, giving a single set of information states with an inner and an outer aspect.

**I. The macroscopic problem (pp. 288-290)**

- Three responses are offered and none is endorsed outright: ground macroscopic information separately in phenomenology, which looks redundant (p. 288); let microphenomenal properties constitute macrophenomenology according to their informational, not spatial, structure (p. 289); or connect the two levels by further laws, which gives up the economy that made the Russellian view attractive (p. 289, and endnote 7 on p. 369). If all fail, retreat to the plain ontology of two realms connected by law (p. 290).

## Evidence offered

- The light switch, the dimmer and the compact disk as cases of physically realized information (pp. 264-265).
- Shannon's diagram of a communication channel, read as individuating messages by what a transmitter can distinguish (p. 266).
- Colour experience: a three-dimensional phenomenal space matched by a three-dimensional space of neural coding, inferred from reports and matching behaviour (p. 267).
- The just-so story of colour processing: cones, three axes, loss of wavelength information, categorisation, report (p. 272).
- The descending scale: humans, dogs, mice, lizards, fish, slugs, neural networks, thermostats (pp. 276-277).
- Authorities for the informational view of physics: Wheeler, Fredkin, Leckey (pp. 284-285). Endnote 4 distances the proposal from Wheeler's own view, which Chalmers thinks is closer to idealism (pp. 368-369).
- Sellars on the grain problem, Nagel on the mental part-whole relation (pp. 288-289); Foster and Lockwood on the Russellian view's difficulties (endnote 6, p. 369).
- Endnote 1 lists predecessors: Bohm, Sayre, Velmans, and Lockwood, whose discussion comes close to the double-aspect principle (p. 368).
- No empirical test is offered. Chalmers says so: the proposal lacks strong predictive power (p. 291).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Depends on ch06 for structural coherence and awareness as global availability, and on ch07 for organizational invariance; both are treated as fixed constraints the new principle must meet (p. 270).
- Depends on ch05: the claim that phenomenal judgments can be explained without consciousness becomes a positive tool here. The paradox of ch05 is softened, not removed: experience still does not enter the explanation of the judgment, but both have a common root (pp. 271, 275).
- Depends on ch04 for the Russellian suggestion about intrinsic properties and for protophenomenal properties (pp. 280-281, 286-287). The Russellian reading is also what ch04 offered as a way round epiphenomenalism; endnote 7 notes that patching the macroscopic problem with extra laws would bring epiphenomenalism back (p. 369).
- Tension with ch07: organizational invariance was argued from systems fine-grained enough to fix behaviour and judgments. Here experience is attributed to systems with no judgments at all, where the fading and dancing qualia arguments have no purchase. The extension rests on arguments E and G alone.
- Tension within the chapter: physically realized information is defined relative to a chosen causal pathway and effect space (p. 264), yet it is asked to carry a fundamental law. Chalmers notes the resulting proliferation of slightly different information spaces, and so of subjects, in one brain (pp. 282-283) and finds it more troubling than conscious thermostats (p. 283).
- Feeds ch09: if experience comes with information realized in functional organization, the right computation suffices for consciousness. Feeds ch10: ubiquitous experience is one of Chalmers's reasons for rejecting the view that consciousness collapses the wave function, and the double-aspect principle is mentioned as a second route to the superposition principle.
- The six open questions (pp. 290-291) include the unity of consciousness and which information in the brain is mine. Neither is taken up again in the book.

## Open questions

- What makes a physically realized information space one of those with a phenomenal counterpart? Amplification and restricted causation are floated and left (p. 283).
- Is the appeal to information doing work that structural coherence was not already doing? My reading is that argument B adds a vocabulary and argument D adds a heuristic; the content of the proposed law is not fixed until the constraint is supplied. Chalmers concedes as much when he calls the principle a template (p. 268).
- How does a subject arise from information? Chalmers says experiences are associated with a system and that unconscious processing belongs to different subjects (p. 282), but gives no account of what individuates a subject.
- Why should the intrinsic nature of mass or charge be anything like experience? The match of two gaps is the whole case (p. 287).
- The first open question on Chalmers's own list (p. 290): why an information space is phenomenally realized one way and not another. This is the inverted-spectrum possibility of ch07 returning as underdetermination of the laws.
