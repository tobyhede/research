# ch04: Naturalistic Dualism (pp. 117–164)

## Question this chapter answers

Chapter 3 argued that consciousness cannot be reductively explained. What follows for ontology: is consciousness itself physical? Chalmers's answer: no. The failure of logical supervenience entails that materialism is false. What is left is a property dualism in which experience depends on the physical by contingent laws of nature (natural supervenience). The chapter then defends the step from conceivability to the falsity of materialism against a posteriori necessity, compares the argument with Jackson's and Kripke's, asks whether the view is epiphenomenalism, sorts the rival positions, and answers the standard reasons for rejecting dualism.

Sections 2, 3 and 4 carry Chalmers's asterisk (technical). The chapter text ends on p. 164 with one word; Chapter 5 begins there.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Materialism | p. 117 | The doctrine that the physical facts exhaust all the facts: every positive fact is entailed by the physical facts. Carried over from Chapter 2. On this definition materialism requires logical supervenience (pp. 137-138). |
| Further fact | p. 117 | A positive fact about our world that does not hold in some physically identical logically possible world. |
| Natural supervenience (without logical supervenience) | p. 118 | Experience depends systematically and lawfully on physical structure in the actual world, but is not entailed by it. The view Chalmers develops. |
| Causal closure of the physical | p. 118 | For every physical event there is a physical sufficient cause. Qualified as more or less closed, with quantum indeterminacy as a small loophole (pp. 118, 143). Premise (4) of the master argument (p. 154). |
| Property dualism (strong sense) | pp. 118-119 | Phenomenal properties are properties of individuals not entailed by their physical properties, and not logically supervenient on microphysical properties. Distinguished from the weak sense in which any property not invoked by physics (fitness) counts; the weak sense is compatible with materialism (p. 119). Not a dualism of substances (p. 118). |
| Fundamental property / fundamental law | pp. 119-120 | Features and laws that cannot be explained by anything more basic and must be taken as primitive. |
| Protophenomenal property | p. 120 | A hypothesised nonphysical fundamental property, not itself phenomenal, on which phenomenal properties would logically supervene when many are instantiated together. |
| Psychophysical law / supervenience law | p. 120 | A fundamental law saying how phenomenal (or protophenomenal) properties depend on physical ones. It does not interfere with physical laws. Timeless, like physical laws (p. 163). |
| Naturalistic dualism | p. 121 | Chalmers's name for the view: naturalistic because everything follows from a network of basic properties and laws and nothing contradicts science; dualist because that network includes nonphysical properties. |
| Emergence (innocent vs. strong) | p. 123 | Innocent: unobvious but logically supervenient (flocking, self-organisation). Strong (British emergentist): not predictable even from all low-level facts, needing new fundamental laws; counted as property dualism. |
| Logical vs. metaphysical possibility (of statements) | p. 125 | Logical possibility is possible truth under primary intensions (1-possibility); metaphysical possibility is possible truth under secondary intensions (2-possibility). Both are evaluated over one space of worlds (p. 128). |
| Weak metaphysical necessity | p. 130 | Kripkean a posteriori necessity: it constrains how worlds are described, not which worlds exist. |
| Strong metaphysical necessity | p. 130 | A hypothesised modality on which some fully conceivable worlds are impossible for reasons independent of semantics. Chalmers denies that it exists. |
| Mode of presentation | p. 134 | The way a fact is known; in Chalmers's terms, a primary intension. |
| Epiphenomenalism (strong / weak) | pp. 143-144, 153 | Strong: experience is causally irrelevant. Weak or first-approximation: experience is explanatorily irrelevant to behaviour, whether or not it has some subtle causal relevance (p. 149). |
| Explanatory irrelevance | p. 149 | Behaviour can be explained in physical or computational terms that neither involve nor imply phenomenology. |
| Intrinsic nature of the physical | p. 146 | The nonrelational properties of the entities that physics characterises only by their relations. Physics is silent on them (Russell). |
| Nomological danglers | p. 153 | Psychophysical laws seen as hanging off the physical, not integrated with other laws. |
| Type A / B / C (and C') | pp. 158-159 | A: consciousness, so far as it exists, logically supervenes (functionalism, eliminativism). B: no logical supervenience, materialism kept. C: neither; property dualism. C': phenomenal properties identified with intrinsic properties of physical entities; more monist, grouped under C. |

## Leading propositions

- If consciousness does not logically supervene on the physical, materialism is false (p. 117).
- The resulting dualism is of properties, not Cartesian substances; the physical stays causally closed and consciousness still supervenes naturally (p. 118).
- A final theory needs new fundamental properties (phenomenal or protophenomenal) and new fundamental psychophysical laws, on the model of Maxwell's electromagnetism (pp. 120-121).
- The view is naturalistic and contradicts nothing in physical theory (p. 121). It might turn out to be a monism, but not a materialist one (p. 122).
- Kripkean a posteriori necessity does not block the argument: it changes how worlds are described, not which worlds are possible (pp. 127-128).
- The argument goes through on primary intensions alone, and for consciousness primary and secondary intensions coincide anyway (pp. 126-127).
- The hidden-essence reading of physical terms yields a view with phenomenal or protophenomenal properties as fundamental, so it is not materialism in any useful sense (pp. 129-130).
- Strong metaphysical necessity is brute, without analogy, unknowable and ad hoc, and even if granted leaves a theory of the same explanatory shape as property dualism (pp. 130-132).
- The knowledge argument and the modal argument are two sides of one argument; the sound core of Kripke's argument is the zombie part, which is the argument from failed logical supervenience (pp. 139, 142-143).
- The only coherent materialist reply to Mary is the ability analysis, and it is implausible (pp. 138-139).
- Natural supervenience makes experience explanatorily irrelevant to behaviour; whether it is causally irrelevant is open (pp. 149, 153).
- Interactionist dualism and new physics do no better: the phenomenal component can be subtracted from any causal story (pp. 151, 155).
- Of the eight options, only naturalistic dualism is tenable (p. 155).
- The deepest divide is type A against the rest, and it ends in a clash of intuitions that argument cannot settle (pp. 159-160).
- The usual reasons for preferring materialism (simplicity, induction, respect for science, fear of the supernatural, the interaction problem, evolution) have little force against this view (pp. 161-163).

## Arguments

**A. The basic argument against materialism (p. 117)**

- **Premises**: (p. 117) (1) There are conscious experiences in our world. (2) There is a logically possible world physically identical to ours in which the positive facts about consciousness do not hold. (3) So facts about consciousness are further facts beyond the physical facts. Materialism is the thesis that the physical facts entail every positive fact.
- **Unstated premises**: that logical possibility, established by conceivability in Chapter 3, is the possibility relevant to ontology (defended in section 2); that the conceived world is physically identical in the full sense and not only identical in structure (taken up under the alternative strategy, pp. 128-129); that the definition of materialism as an entailment thesis is the right one.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 117) Materialism is false. The inverted-spectrum world gives the same result. Kripke's image: after fixing the physical facts God had more work to do (pp. 117-118).

**B. From anti-materialism to naturalistic dualism (pp. 118-121, 154)**

- **Premises**: (p. 118) Nothing in Chapter 3 disturbs the systematic dependence of experience on physical structure in the actual world. (p. 118) The physical world is more or less causally closed, so there is no room for a mental substance doing extra causal work. (p. 120) The dependence cannot be derived from physical laws. (p. 121) Any lawful relationship must be supported by fundamental laws. (p. 154) Physically identical beings will have identical experiences, called a plausible assumption.
- **Unstated premises**: that the dependence observed in familiar cases is strict and universal enough to be law; that fundamental psychophysical laws will resemble physical ones in being simple (p. 121 expects this from the case of physics); that causal closure is established by current science and not only assumed by its method.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 120-121) Consciousness arises from the physical by contingent fundamental laws, with phenomenal or protophenomenal properties as new fundamentals.

**C. Minor objections (pp. 123-124)**

- Emergence: innocent emergence is logically supervenient, so it is no analogy; strong emergence needs new fundamental laws and is property dualism (p. 123).
- Two aspects: either the phenomenal aspect is entailed by the physical (then Chapter 3 applies) or it is not (then property dualism) (p. 123).
- Searle: H2O does not cause liquidity, it constitutes it; a nonliquid replica of liquid H2O is incoherent, a zombie is not (p. 124). Endnote 2 argues that Searle is better read as a property dualist and that he has the order of explanation backward on irreducibility (pp. 347-348).
- Descartes: the argument is not from conceiving a physical state without consciousness to non-identity; it is from conceiving all physical facts without consciousness to the physical facts not being exhaustive (p. 124).

**D. Against the Kripkean objection (pp. 124-128)**

- **Objection**: (pp. 124-125) Zombies are logically but not metaphysically possible, as with water that is not H2O.
- **Premises**: (p. 125) The water case is a difference between primary and secondary intensions. (p. 126) A primary intension determines a property in its own right; being watery stuff is a real property. (p. 126) Chapter 3 shows there are physically identical worlds lacking consciousness under its primary intension, and this is settled without a posteriori input. (p. 127) For consciousness the two intensions coincide: to be an experience in any world is to have a certain feel. (pp. 127-128) Kripkean necessity rules out no world; it shows only that some are misdescribed. The two intensions range over the same worlds, differing only by a centre.
- **Unstated premises**: that every primary intension that fails to be satisfied in a physically identical world marks an ontological addition, not only a conceptual one; that conceivability under ideal reflection delivers a world, so that the space of conceivable worlds and the space of possible worlds are one (this is what the type-B theorist denies).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 128) If a conceivable world is physically identical to ours and lacks something, no fact about how terms designate can make it impossible.
- **Technical notes**: a primary intension gives a centre-relative property, but fixing the centre does not help Mary (p. 126). The worry that the zombie world has no concept of consciousness at its centre is avoided by a partial zombie world with Chalmers conscious at the centre (pp. 126-127).

**E. Against the alternative strategy: hidden essences (pp. 128-130)**

- **Objection**: (pp. 128-129) Physical terms are rigidified role descriptions; electrons and mass have intrinsic natures hidden from physics, which may be phenomenal or protophenomenal. The zombie world lacks them, so it is not physically identical (Maxwell, Lockwood).
- **Premises**: (p. 129) First reply, semantic: physical concepts are defined by role across all worlds; an electron with the extrinsic properties of a proton is not coherent. (p. 129) Second reply, deeper: even granting the semantics, the world has phenomenal properties not fixed by what physics reveals; the dualism of physical and nonphysical becomes a dualism of accessible and hidden properties.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 130) The view takes phenomenal or protophenomenal properties as fundamental and is closer to dualism, idealism or neutral monism than to materialism. Chalmers calls the objection coherent and more interesting than the last (p. 129).

**F. Against strong metaphysical necessity (pp. 130-132)**

- **Premises**: (p. 130) It imposes brute constraints on the space of worlds; brute facts about our world may be tolerable but brute facts about what is possible are not. (pp. 130-131) No analogy supports it: Kripkean cases need one space of worlds; endnote 7 adds that mathematical and moral truths rule out no conceivable world (pp. 348-349). (p. 131) It could not be known: it is not a priori, and a posteriori evidence only locates our world within the space. (p. 131) It multiplies modalities: four for statements, three classes of worlds. (p. 131) God could presumably create anything logically possible. (p. 131) Even if granted, consciousness is still not derivable and primitive connecting principles are still needed.
- **Unstated premises**: that the space of possible worlds needs no explanation of its extent while a restriction on it does; that a modality with no other instance should be rejected, when the opponent may hold that consciousness is the one place it would show; that God's powers track logical possibility, which assumes the point at issue.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 131-132) There is no reason to believe in it; it saves materialism only by making it mysterious how consciousness could be physical. Endnote 6: few defend it in print, but Bigelow and Pargetter, Byrne, Levine and Loar are implicitly committed to it (p. 348). Endnote 8 gives Jackson's amoeba argument against physicalism without a priori entailment (p. 349).

**G. Against cognitive limitation (pp. 132-133)**

- **Premises**: (p. 132) The objector says the zombie world only seems coherent to limited minds, as with unprovable mathematical truths. (p. 132) In the mathematical case modal intuition is silent; no conceivable world is delivered in which the truth fails. In the zombie case there is a clearly conceivable world. (p. 133) The objector must also hold that a smarter Mary could know what red is like and that an unknowable functional or structural analysis of phenomenal concepts exists.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 133) The gap would be without parallel; the move is ad hoc and a last resort. Endnote 11 notes that it resembles Dennett's position: both blame the intuitions on impairment (pp. 349-350).

**H. The knowledge argument defended (pp. 133-139)**

- **Old fact, new mode** (pp. 134-135): the gap between water knowledge and H2O knowledge is a gap in primary intensions, so this is objection D again. Directly: whenever one knows a fact under one mode and not another, there is a separate fact one lacks, the one connecting the modes. So if Mary gains any factual knowledge, she gains knowledge of a new fact.
- **Loar** (p. 136): two predicates may share a primary intension without this being knowable a priori. Chalmers: that requires fewer worlds than we can conceive, which is strong metaphysical necessity. Endnote 15 argues that recognitional concepts still have primary intensions (p. 350).
- **Hidden essences** (p. 136): Mary lacks knowledge of intrinsic natures; this again makes phenomenal or protophenomenal properties fundamental.
- **Indexicals** (pp. 136-137): reduction fails, since full indexical knowledge does not tell Mary what red is like. The analogy fails, since no uncentered world shares our physical facts and differs in indexical facts; fixing the centre is the single loophole, and it does not settle the phenomenal facts. Endnote 17 extends the centre to mark an experience as this one (pp. 350-351).
- **Ability** (pp. 138-139): Lewis and Nemirow say Mary gains know-how only. This is internally coherent but implausible: Mary narrows the epistemic possibilities; such knowledge embeds in conditionals (Loar) and can be right or wrong (Lycan). Dennett's blue-apple reply shows only that Mary had recognitional abilities beforehand (pp. 138-139).
- **Unstated premise**: that narrowing epistemic possibilities is always learning a fact about the world, which is the very thing the ability theorist denies.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 139) A materialist must deny that Mary learns anything about the world; the implausibility of that denial counts against materialism. The knowledge argument best establishes the failure of logical supervenience; the modal argument best makes the step to the falsity of materialism.

**I. Kripke's argument assessed (pp. 139-143)**

- **Premises**: (p. 141) The token-identity arguments rest on unreliable intuitions about what is essential to a particular state. (pp. 141-142) Disembodiment shows at most that dualism is possible; materialism needs no identity across all worlds (Boyd). (p. 142) The zombie part shows that God had more to do after creating the physical, and Kripke's reply to the misdescription move is that pain's primary and secondary intensions coincide.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 143) Disembodiment, identity, essentialism and rigid designation are inessential. What works in Kripke is the argument from the failure of logical supervenience. Chalmers reads Kripke's misdescription strategy as endorsing the weak treatment of a posteriori necessity (p. 142; endnote 25, p. 352).

**J. The epiphenomenalism problem and the two-pronged reply (pp. 143-153)**

- **The problem** (p. 143; Kirk, Horgan, Seager): if a causally closed zombie world Z is possible, everything in Z has a causal explanation without the phenomenal; the same events occur here; so the phenomenal is causally irrelevant here.
- **Prong 1, perhaps not epiphenomenal** (pp. 144-148). Four options: (1) regularity or nomic accounts of causation, which Chalmers rejects as accounts but uses to explain why we infer causation (pp. 144-145); (2) overdetermination, not pursued but not ruled out (p. 145); (3) experience as what realises causation (Rosenberg), speculative and still facing the zombie world (pp. 145-146); (4) the option he favours: physics characterises entities only relationally; the intrinsic properties may be phenomenal or protophenomenal; then physical causation relates instances of those properties and experience inherits causal relevance (pp. 146-147).
- **Unstated premises in (4)**: that there must be intrinsic properties (a world of pure causal flux is said to be arguably logically impossible, p. 148); that phenomenal properties are intrinsic and nonrelational; that being the relatum of a causal relation is enough for causal relevance.
- **Prong 2, epiphenomenalism is tolerable** (pp. 152-153). Mere counterintuitiveness is no refutation. The intuition that pain causes withdrawal is explained by observed regularity. The objection from judgments about experience is the serious one and is deferred to Chapter 5. Danglers can be met by simple laws; evolution is no problem.
- **Against interactionism** (pp. 149-151): it bets against physics and cognitive science; quantum versions conflict with randomness or do the wrong kind of work; and the phenomenal properties of psychons can be subtracted leaving the same dynamics, so experience is still explanatorily superfluous.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 153) The view implies at least a weak epiphenomenalism and may lead to a stronger one; the alternatives are worse.

**K. Elimination of alternatives (pp. 154-158)**

- **Premises**: (p. 154) Four premises: experience exists; it is not logically supervenient; if so, materialism is false; the physical is closed. Each rival denies one: (i) eliminativism denies 1; (ii) reductive functionalism, (iii) nonfunctionalist reductive materialism and (iv) new-physics materialism deny 2; (v) nonreductive materialism denies 3; (vi) interactionist dualism denies 4 (pp. 154-155). (viii) Don't-have-a-clue materialism must reduce to one of the others (p. 155).
- **Verdicts**: (i) conflicts with manifest fact (p. 156); (iii) changes the subject (p. 156); (ii) misrepresents what it means to be conscious and is close to eliminativism (pp. 157-158); (iv) and (vi) bet badly on physics and allow the phenomenal to be subtracted (p. 155); (v) rests on a misuse of Kripke or on strong necessity (p. 156).
- **Unstated premise**: that the list is exhaustive and each verdict final; the argument is by elimination.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 158) Naturalistic dualism is the only view without a fatal flaw.

**L. Replies to reasons for materialism (pp. 161-163)**

- Simplicity: the razor applies when other things are equal; here the phenomena require more (p. 161).
- Induction: materialism succeeded where only structure and function needed explaining, and where our access is physically mediated; neither holds for consciousness (pp. 161-162).
- Science: closure is kept, so no science is contradicted (p. 162).
- Interaction: the two interact by psychophysical laws; asking for a further connection is as fruitless as with fundamental physical laws (pp. 162-163).
- Evolution: the laws are timeless; experience appears when systems meet their antecedents (p. 163).

**Position versus argument.** What he holds: property dualism with natural supervenience, leaning toward the intrinsic-nature (C') version without settling on it (p. 160). Why he says he holds it: the premises of A plus closure, and the failure of each alternative. The positive picture (laws, protophenomenal properties, intrinsic natures) is offered as speculation, not as established.

## Evidence offered

- **Thought experiments**: the zombie world and inverted world carried over from Chapter 3 (p. 117); the partial zombie world (p. 127); Mary (pp. 133-139); God creating the world (pp. 117-118, 131); Eccles's psychons with phenomenal properties subtracted (p. 151); the XYZ world as a case of misdescription (p. 127).
- **Analogies from science**: Maxwell taking electromagnetic features as fundamental (p. 121); Newton's critics asking how gravity acts at a distance (p. 163); matter and energy as two aspects of one kind (p. 122).
- **Appeal to current science**: closure rests on what the best evidence of contemporary science is said to show (p. 118) and on physical events seeming to be explained by other physical events (p. 149). No study is cited.
- **Cited concession**: Weinberg admits a final theory may explain only the objective correlates of consciousness (p. 120).
- **Ordinary language**: materialists say the mind arises from the brain, but not that learning arises from the brain (p. 119); endnote 1 adds Edelman's subtitle (p. 347).
- **Authors used in support**: Jackson, Lewis and White for the claim that a materialist cannot use a posteriori necessity (endnote 3, p. 348); Lockwood and Conee on modes of presentation (endnote 14, p. 350); Russell on the silence of physics about intrinsic nature (p. 146); Kirk 1974 as the most explicit earlier modal argument (endnote 12, p. 350).
- **Informal polls**: endnote 33 reports audiences splitting 2:1 or 3:1 in favour of there being something further to explain (p. 353); the text says almost one-third accept type A (p. 159).
- **Nine further positions in endnotes 34-42** (pp. 353-358): biological materialism and physicalist-functionalism collapse into property dualism or strong necessity; psychofunctionalism is settled by asking for the primary intension; anomalous monism does not exclude pointwise laws; representationalism and higher-order thought are reductive functionalism or else nonreductive; teleofunctionalism still allows a functional duplicate with the right history and no experience; emergent causation lacks evidence and leaves experience superfluous; mysterianism is premature.
- **Endnote 29** sets Chalmers against Shoemaker on four questions: reference to physical properties is fixed relationally (both yes); they are identical to relational properties (Chalmers: probably); all nomic relations are essential (Chalmers: no); intrinsic properties underlie them (Chalmers: yes) (pp. 352-353).
- **Confident language**: the Kripkean view is said to fail quite straightforwardly (p. 125); the old-fact reply is called the weakest of the major replies (pp. 135-136). Set against hedges on the positive side: the metaphysical speculation needs a pinch of salt (p. 148).

Chalmers grants that on a wider reading experience could count as physical (p. 122) and that intrinsic natures might be called physical (pp. 129, 148). So the conclusion that materialism is false depends in part on a choice of words; the stable claim is that structure and dynamics do not fix experience.
- **Shift in the term possibility (my observation)**: logical possibility is defined for statements as truth under primary intensions (p. 125), but the argument needs a claim about worlds: that every conceivable world exists in the one space (p. 128). The move from the first to the second is where type-B views resist, and it rests on section F.
- **Dualism that may be monism (my observation)**: the title position is called dualism, yet the option Chalmers is most drawn to (p. 146) is described as a monism, idealism or neutral monism (p. 148). The chapter's firm conclusion is disjunctive: standard type C or type C' (p. 160).
- **Epiphenomenalism wording (my observation)**: he declines the label (p. 153) while conceding explanatory irrelevance and calling the view epiphenomenalistic to a first approximation (p. 149). The favoured escape gives, in his own phrase about a parallel case, causal relevance with explanatory irrelevance (p. 151). The subtraction argument he uses against interactionism (p. 151) therefore applies to his own favoured view, as he admits at pp. 155-156.
- **Same shape as the rival (my observation)**: Chalmers says the strong-necessity view differs from his mainly by an ontological stipulation (p. 131) and shares its explanatory shape (pp. 156-157). That weakens the claim that the rival is far more mysterious (p. 132): if the theories are the same for explanatory purposes, the choice between laws of nature and laws of necessity is itself close to a stipulation on both sides.
- **Preface link**: the Great Divide between those who take consciousness seriously and those who do not is named again at p. 159.

## Open questions

- Section F says strong necessities could never be known because a posteriori evidence only locates our world (p. 131). Does the same reasoning show that conceivability is a reliable guide to the extent of the space of worlds? The chapter assumes it and does not argue it here. Check ch02's account of conceivability.
- Is the definition of materialism (p. 117) doing the work of premise (3)? A type-B materialist rejects the definition, not the inference. Sections D to G are then the whole case, and F is its weakest part (my judgment): no reason to believe, no analogy, and a theological premise.
- Why expect psychophysical laws to be simple and elegant (p. 121)? Physical fundamentals relate simple magnitudes; these laws would relate experience to complex physical organisation. Check how ch06-ch08 meet this.
- The elimination argument (p. 155) needs the eight options to be exhaustive. Views that reject the framing, for example by denying that conceivability arguments about consciousness are well formed, are placed under type A or under cognitive limitation. Is that placement fair?
- The final appeal is to a premise that cannot be argued for (p. 160) plus audience polls (p. 353). How much of the book's later theory survives for a reader who accepts type B? Chalmers says almost all of it (pp. 156-157).
- If protophenomenal properties jointly yield experience (p. 147) although this cannot work for standard physical properties, what makes the difference? The text says only that such properties are foreign to our conception.
- Is a world of pure causal flux really logically impossible (p. 148)? The text says arguably, and the reply to the zombie objection under option (4) depends on it.
- Causal closure carries premise (4) and the case against interactionism, yet is stated with hedges (pp. 118, 143). Does the quantum chapter discharge the promissory note at p. 118?
