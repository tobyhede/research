# ch05: The Paradox of Phenomenal Judgment (pp. 164–200)

## Question this chapter answers

If consciousness does not logically supervene on the physical, while everything we say and judge about consciousness does and can be reductively explained, then consciousness is explanatorily irrelevant to our own claims that we are conscious. Does that consequence refute the nonreductive view? Chalmers answers no. He accepts the consequence, argues that explaining the judgments is not explaining consciousness, and replies to three arguments that explanatory irrelevance is incompatible with our knowing about, remembering, and referring to our experiences. The replies turn on one idea: our epistemic and semantic relation to experience is acquaintance, not causation.

The chapter is, in his words, largely defensive (p. 164). Sections 5 to 8 are marked with an asterisk as technical.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phenomenal judgment | pp. 164-165 | Any judgment in the vicinity of consciousness. So called not because the judgments are phenomenal states but because they concern phenomenology or its objects. They are cognitive acts and fall within psychology (p. 165). |
| Judgment | p. 165 | A stipulated functional notion: the state or process that plays the causal role of a belief, with any phenomenal quality subtracted. A system judges that a proposition is true if it tends to respond affirmatively when queried, behaves appropriately, and so on (pp. 165-166). It is what I and my zombie twin have in common (p. 166). |
| Belief (about consciousness) | p. 165 | Left open at first as possibly more than a judgment. By the end of the chapter a phenomenal belief has content partly constituted by experience, so it is more than the corresponding judgment (p. 198). |
| Claim (about consciousness) | p. 165 | The verbal expression of a phenomenal judgment; a behavioural act, so open to functional explanation. |
| Deflationary reading | p. 166 | The reading on which a description of a judgment by its content (the judgment that one has a red sensation) refers only to the functional state expressed in those words, without attributing the full content a phenomenal belief has. Glossed later as the functional notion of belief (p. 190). |
| First-order judgment | pp. 166-167 | The judgment that accompanies an experience and concerns the object of the experience, not the experience. Its contents make up the contents of awareness. |
| Second-order judgment | p. 167 | A judgment about a particular conscious experience or kind of experience (that I am having a red sensation). |
| Third-order judgment | p. 167 | A judgment about conscious experience as a type (that it is baffling, ineffable, or does not exist). |
| Awareness | p. 167 | The psychological counterpart of consciousness from ch01: information accessible to the cognitive system and available for report. Shares its contents with consciousness. |
| Paradox of phenomenal judgment | p. 168 | The result that consciousness is explanatorily irrelevant to our claims and judgments about consciousness, because those can be reductively explained and consciousness cannot. Restated as four jointly held premises (p. 174). |
| Explanatory irrelevance / causal irrelevance | p. 168 | Explanatory irrelevance: a physical or functional explanation of behaviour can be given that neither appeals to nor implies consciousness (p. 169). Causal irrelevance is a separate metaphysical question left open in ch04; it is conceded temporarily in sections 5 to 7 for the sake of argument (p. 182). |
| Explanatory exclusion | p. 169 | Kim's name for the worry that any high-level property is made irrelevant by a low-level explanation. Chalmers says it does not arise where there is logical supervenience (p. 170). |
| Explanandum / explanatory construct | pp. 178-179 | An explanatory construct (God, UFOs, the vital spirit) is postulated to explain other things and can be dropped when they are otherwise explained. Consciousness is a brute explanandum, a phenomenon in its own right. |
| Extrospection | p. 181 | Observing one's own cognitive mechanisms from the outside and reflecting on what is going on. Chalmers's diagnosis of what Dennett takes for introspection. |
| Seeming (phenomenal / psychological) | pp. 181-182 | Phenomenal sense: for things to seem a certain way is for them to be experienced that way. Psychological sense: to be disposed to judge that they are that way. |
| Causal theory of knowledge | p. 184 | The view that a belief about an entity counts as knowledge only if appropriately caused by that entity. |
| Reliabilist theory of knowledge | p. 185 | The view that beliefs are justified if formed by a process that tends to produce true beliefs. |
| Core epistemic situation | p. 186 | What is held fixed across skeptical scenarios; how things are for the subject. It already includes conscious experience. |
| Acquaintance | p. 188 | The epistemic relation one stands in to an experience simply by having it. More primitive than belief; it provides evidence for beliefs and does not itself constitute belief. |
| Causal theory of memory | p. 191 | The view that to remember something is to stand in an appropriate causal relation to it. |
| Causal theory of reference | p. 192 | The view that reference to an entity requires a causal connection to it. Reinterpreted as an insight about how many primary intensions happen to be characterised (p. 192). |
| Relational concept of red experience | p. 196 | Experience of the sort typically caused, in me, by red things. Indexical; shared in primary intension by inverted subjects and even by a zombie. |
| Qualitative concept (R) | p. 197 | The concept of the quality of red experiences itself. Its primary intension differs between me and my inverted counterpart, so it is partly constituted by the experience. |
| Ineffability | p. 197 | From ch01: little can be said to communicate the intrinsic quality of an experience except by relational properties. Here read as indirect support for explanatory irrelevance (p. 198). |

## Leading propositions

- Consciousness and cognition are not detached; the relations between them are systematic, and analysing them will supply much of the material for a theory of consciousness (p. 164).
- Phenomenal judgments and claims are part of psychology and are in principle reductively explainable (pp. 165, 168).
- It follows that consciousness is explanatorily irrelevant to our claims and judgments about it (p. 168).
- This differs from ordinary explanatory exclusion: memory and temperature inherit explanatory relevance by logically supervening on the physical; consciousness cannot (p. 170).
- Experience has only an indirect explanatory relevance through its nomic connection to physical processes, and is superfluous to the basic explanation (p. 171).
- The zombie twin makes all the same claims, argues with zombie materialists, and is wrong; the explanation of his claims is also the explanation of mine (p. 171).
- The paradox arises almost as strongly for type-B materialism and for the view that phenomenal properties are the intrinsic nature of the physical (p. 172).
- Irrelevance to ordinary behaviour and to first-order judgments is only counterintuitive; irrelevance to second- and third-order judgments is the troubling case, because we know we are conscious (pp. 173-174).
- The paradox consists of four premises: causal closure, logical supervenience of judgments, failure of logical supervenience of consciousness, and knowledge of consciousness. All four are probably true (pp. 174-175).
- Phenomenal judgments are what one would expect from a reflective system with direct access to its own perceptual contents; consciousness is surprising, claims about consciousness are not (pp. 175-177).
- Explaining the judgments is the single most powerful reductive argument, and it fails, because consciousness is an explanandum and not an explanatory construct (pp. 178-179).
- Dennett's argument that his theory explains how things seem equivocates between the phenomenal and psychological senses of seeming (pp. 181-182).
- Neither a causal nor a reliabilist theory fits our knowledge of consciousness, because neither can deliver certainty (pp. 185-186).
- What justifies a belief about an experience is having the experience; the relation is acquaintance (pp. 187-188).
- The zombie's judgments are unjustified because he lacks the experiences; that he would say the same thing shows only that we are alike from the third-person point of view (pp. 189-190).
- Memory of experience needs only a causal chain between psychological states, starting from a belief justified by acquaintance (pp. 191-192).
- Reference needs a primary intension that something satisfies, not a causal connection (pp. 192-193).
- The qualitative concept of a red experience has a primary intension constituted by the experience itself, so the content of phenomenal beliefs does not logically supervene on the physical (pp. 197-198).
- The arguments from epistemology and reference turn out to be challenges, not refutations (pp. 199-200).

## Arguments

**A. The paradox (pp. 168-171, 174)**

- **Premises**: (p. 174) (1) The physical domain is causally closed. (2) Judgments about consciousness are logically supervenient on the physical. (3) Consciousness is not logically supervenient on the physical. (4) We know we are conscious. (p. 174) From (1) and (2), judgments can be reductively explained. (p. 174) With (3), consciousness is explanatorily irrelevant to the judgments.
- **Unstated premises**: that explanatory relevance requires a conceptual connection between the explaining property and what goes into the explanation; this is stated outright only in the parenthesis on type-B views, where explanatory relevance must be supported by conceptual connections (p. 172). That a nomic connection gives only derivative relevance (p. 171). That knowledge that P normally requires the fact that P to figure in the explanation of the judgment (stated as a rule of thumb on p. 174).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 174) The irrelevance stands in tension with (4). He does not call it a contradiction; he holds all four and sets out to reconcile them (p. 175).
- **Escape routes considered**: deny (1), interactionism, which ch04 argued does not help (p. 174); deny (2), but the problem returns for claims and for the sounds and marks we make (pp. 174-175); deny (3) or (4), reductionism and eliminativism, already rejected (p. 175).

**B. Why this is not ordinary explanatory exclusion (pp. 169-170)**

- **Premises**: (p. 170) When two sets of properties are conceptually related, an explanation in terms of one does not make the other explanatorily irrelevant; the high-level story retells the low-level one more parsimoniously. (p. 170) Memory and temperature are logically supervenient. (p. 170) Consciousness is not, so a physical explanation does not even imply its existence.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 170) Consciousness is independent of the explanation of behaviour in a stronger sense than any other high-level property.

**C. The zombie form of the paradox (pp. 171-172)**

- **Premises**: (p. 171) The zombie's universe is law-governed, so his claims have an explanation, and it must be physical. (p. 171) The processes in his body are exactly mirrored in mine. (p. 171) So whatever explains his claims explains mine, and that explanation does not depend on consciousness.
- **Unstated premise**: that the explanation of an event is fixed by its physical causes alone, so that an added nomic correlate in my world cannot enter the explanation in my case.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 171) The explanation of my claims is independent of the existence of consciousness. The zombie runs the same reasoning and is deluded (p. 172).

**D. The design sketch: why judgments are to be expected (pp. 175-177)**

- **Premises**: (p. 176) A reflective system would naturally have a concept of self and direct access to its own perceptual contents. (p. 176) Giving it access to its low-level parts would be inefficient and unnecessary (Hofstadter; SHRDLU). (pp. 176-177) Asked how it knows the tricycle is red, such a system would say it just looks red, and on reflection would treat this as a brute fact and wonder about it.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 177) That we judge and claim to be conscious is a natural consequence of design, explainable in computational terms without invoking consciousness. He says the speculation is not to be taken too seriously and defers the detail to ch08 (pp. 175, 177).

**E. Explaining the judgments is not enough (pp. 177-180)**

- **The objection**: (p. 178) As with belief in gods, once the belief is explained without its object, the object should be dropped; the debunking explanation even accounts for the popularity of the opposing view.
- **Premises of the reply**: (p. 178) God was hypothesised to explain evident facts; there is no separate phenomenon to point to. (p. 179) Consciousness is not postulated to explain anything; our evidence for it never lay in other phenomena. (p. 179) So showing that it does no explanatory work leaves the evidence untouched. (p. 180) Defining consciousness as whatever is responsible for the judgments is a functional definition and fails like the others, because it is logically possible to explain the judgments without explaining consciousness.
- **Unstated premise**: that we have evidence of consciousness that is not exhausted by our judgments that we have it. This is the acquaintance thesis of section 5, used here before it is argued.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 179) The mind-body problem is the problem of explaining consciousness, not the judgments. The debunking line is eliminativism and denies the evidence of experience.
- **How far he concedes**: he grants there could be a reductive explanation of why he thinks consciousness is irreducible and even of why he thinks it is an explanandum (p. 180). His answer is the principle that theories must explain what cries out for explanation, which he admits may look like stubbornness (p. 180).

**F. Against Dennett (pp. 180-182)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 180-181) Dennett says introspection reveals only judgments. If judgment is widened to include experiences, the thesis is acceptable but judgments are no longer reductively explainable; if judgments are dispositions to report, the thesis denies the data. (p. 181) Dennett has probably taken extrospection for introspection. (pp. 181-182) His central argument in Consciousness Explained is that a theory must explain why things seem as they do, and his does; but a theory of experience must explain seeming in the phenomenal sense and his explains it in the psychological sense.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 182) The argument loses most of its force. Starting from judgments as explananda leads inevitably to a reductive view, but the explananda are the experiences.
- **My note**: the dilemma on p. 181 and the equivocation charge both presuppose that the phenomenal sense of seeming picks out something beyond the disposition to judge. That is the point in dispute with Dennett, so the reply restates the disagreement more than it resolves it.

**G. The argument from self-knowledge and the reply (pp. 183-190)**

- **The objection, two versions**: (p. 183) Zombie version: my judgments are formed by the same mechanisms as the zombie's; if justification accrues solely in virtue of mechanisms, mine are no more justified than his, and his are not justified. (p. 184) Causal version: knowledge of X requires a causal relation to X; experience plays no causal role in forming the judgment; so we cannot know about experience. Shoemaker uses both to argue for reductive functionalism (p. 184).
- **Premises of the reply**: (p. 185) A reliabilist answer is available (in our world the judgments are reliably correlated with experience) but too weak. (p. 185) Justification by a reliable connection cannot deliver certainty, since one cannot rule out that the connection is absent. (pp. 185-186) We are certain we are conscious. (p. 186) Skeptical scenarios hold experience fixed; no scenario in which everything seems the same lacks consciousness, since experience partly constitutes how things seem. (p. 186) Causal connections are contingent, so the same holds for a causal account. (p. 187) Therefore access to experience is unmediated. (pp. 187-188) What justifies the belief is having the experience; change or remove the experience and the chief source of justification goes. (p. 188) Acquaintance is not knowledge, not belief, and does not make phenomenal beliefs incorrigible.
- **Unstated premises**: that certainty is a property of our epistemic position and not only of our confidence (the zombie is exactly as confident); that a fact can justify a belief without figuring in the explanation of why the belief is held; that seeming in the argument on p. 186 is taken in the phenomenal sense, which is the sense distinguished from the psychological one on pp. 181-182.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 189) Causal and mechanism-based theories of justification do not apply to knowledge of experience. I am justified and the zombie is not, because the justification accrues in virtue of my nonphysical features, the experiences themselves.
- **Replies to rejoinders**: that the belief would have been formed anyway shows only that in another case the evidence is absent, not that it fails to justify in this case (p. 189). That the zombie would say the same shows identity only from the third-person point of view (pp. 189-190). The thought that identical beliefs mean identical evidence assumes beliefs determine the epistemic situation, when experience is prior (p. 190).
- **Status**: he grants an opponent may deny certainty, and says such an opponent was an eliminativist or reductive functionalist from the start (pp. 186-187). He grants that how an experience justifies a belief is not analysed (pp. 190-191).

**H. The argument from memory and the reply (pp. 191-192)**

- **Premises**: (p. 191) The causal theory of memory is drawn from cases and need not hold across the board. (p. 191) There is a causal connection between the earlier cognitive state and the later one. (pp. 191-192) The earlier phenomenal belief may be knowledge, justified by acquaintance; a causal link from it to a later belief is sufficient for memory.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 192) A causal connection to the experience is not required to remember it. If someone insists it is, call it pseudo-remembering; nothing important is lost, since what counts as memory here is largely a semantic decision.

**I. The argument from reference and the reply (pp. 192-194)**

- **Premises**: (p. 192) Reference requires only a concept with a primary intension that the entity satisfies; the largest star in the universe is referred to without causal contact. (pp. 192-193) Causal characterisation is how many primary intensions work, not how they must. (p. 193) The existence of a primary intension never depends on a causal connection to the referent; Santa Claus and brain-in-vat concepts have one. (p. 193) Causation is central to most concepts because we refer to what we know and usually know by causal contact; knowledge of consciousness is by acquaintance.
- **Unstated premise**: that the ch02 two-dimensional account of content is correct, and that the concept of consciousness has a determinate primary intension although, as a primitive concept, it cannot be spelled out (pp. 193-194).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 193) My concept of consciousness can have a primary intension and pick out a referent without any causal connection.

**J. The content of phenomenal beliefs (pp. 194-199)**

- **On the general concept**: (pp. 194-195) The zombie's claim to be conscious is false in the intended sense, since he insists his concept is not functional. (p. 195) Whether his concept has the same primary intension as mine is tested by a being with an intrinsic nonphenomenal property in the usual relations: I would speak falsely in calling it conscious; the zombie, lacking acquaintance, could not tell the cases apart. So his concept may fall short. (pp. 195-196) Left open.
- **On specific concepts, premises**: (p. 196) The relational concept (experience of the sort typically caused in me by red things) is indexical and shared across inverted subjects. (p. 197) It is informative, not a priori, that experiences caused by red things have the quality R. (p. 197) Informativeness requires a difference in primary intension. (p. 197) Mary and her inverted twin learn different things on first seeing red, so their qualitative concepts differ in primary intension.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 198) Physically identical subjects can differ in the primary intension of a concept; the content of phenomenal belief is constituted in part by experience and no reductive account of it will succeed. So there is more to a phenomenal belief than to a phenomenal judgment.
- **Tentative extensions**: the constitution relation might explain how an experience justifies a belief that it is of this sort, and a belief formed with a concept constituted by the very experience might always be justified (pp. 198-199). He says he is unsure. It is not needed for the reply in G (p. 199).

**Position versus argument.** What he holds: all four premises of the paradox, including that experience is explanatorily irrelevant to behaviour. Why he says he can hold them together: knowledge, memory and reference do not require a causal or explanatory link to their object in the case of experience. What he does not claim: that he has given a positive epistemology. He says he has sketched the bare outline of a framework (pp. 190-191) and that a full treatment would be a lengthy separate investigation (p. 200).

## Evidence offered

- **Thought experiments**: the zombie twin who writes chapters on consciousness and argues with zombie materialists (p. 171), and who laments his own zombie twin (pp. 171-172); the reflective machine asked about the red tricycle (pp. 175-177); the skeptical scenario with flashing yellow and green experiences, ruled out automatically (p. 186); the brain in a vat (pp. 186, 193); the largest star in the universe (p. 192); the being with a nonphenomenal intrinsic property (p. 195); spectrum-inverted speakers (p. 196); Mary and her inverted twin (p. 197).
- **Analogies**: bachelor and unmarried male, for properties that are conceptually related and so do not exclude each other (p. 170); velocity of a billiard ball and molecular processes (p. 170); belief in gods and in UFOs, as cases where explaining the belief removes the reason for it (pp. 174, 178-179); Wittgenstein's beetle in a box, for the qualitative concept's small role in communication (p. 197).

- **Cited authors, as targets**: Dennett 1979 and 1991 (pp. 180-182); Shoemaker 1975a (p. 184).
- **Introspective report**: against Dennett he says his own introspection finds sensations and experiences that are not only judgments (pp. 180-181). This is testimony against testimony.
- **Empirical material**: none is used as evidence. The design sketch is offered as plausible speculation (p. 177).
- **Endnotes that matter**:

  - Note 3: he is not sure the explain-the-judgments line is found explicitly in the literature; Foss 1989 is the nearest (p. 358).
  - Note 4: on the remark that perhaps Dennett is a zombie, he adds that this would not really help (p. 358).
  - Note 5: Dretske 1995 makes the same move with seeming; the term is a poor one for stating explananda because of its ambiguity (p. 358).
  - Note 6: attacks on the Given and on sense-data refute stronger claims than his (that all knowledge derives from experience, that we perceive sense-data, that having an experience is automatically conceptualising it).   - Notes 12 and 16: the qualitative/relational distinction is tied to Nida-Rümelin's phenomenal and nonphenomenal readings of belief ascriptions (pp. 360-361).

  - Note 17: the demonstrative in the concept of this sort of experience is not the simple indexical but one whose primary intension picks out experiences of that sort in every centred world (p. 361).
  - Note 18: Conee 1985b uses the same constitution relation against Shoemaker (p. 361).

## Concessions and qualifications

- The paradox is the greatest tension a nonreductive theory faces, and a theory that does not face it cannot be fully satisfactory (p. 172).
- It is not obviously fatal (p. 172); it is counterintuitive (p. 182).
- Experience can still be cited in explaining behaviour, with indirect relevance through nomic connection (pp. 170-171).
- The design sketch is speculation (p. 177).
- The explain-the-judgments line is the most interesting a reductionist can take, and the one he would take if he were a reductionist (p. 180).
- Dennett's argument is elegant and has a ring of plausibility (p. 181).
- The appeal to reliabilism is compatible with his view but is too weak; he does not rest on it (p. 185).
- Knowledge of consciousness can perhaps be doubted in various philosophical ways, though not in the direct way external-world knowledge can (pp. 185-186).
- Phenomenal beliefs are not incorrigible; a distracted subject can judge falsely about experience (p. 188).
- The claim that all knowledge derives from knowledge of experience may have been overblown (p. 188).
- The epistemology is only an outline; how an experience justifies a belief, and when fully, is not analysed (pp. 190-191).
- A causal theory is less inappropriate for memory than for knowledge, since memory is mediated (p. 191).
- He has no settled opinions on the content of phenomenal concepts (p. 194), leaves open whether the zombie shares the general concept (p. 196), and is unsure about the weak incorrigibility thesis (p. 199).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Depends on ch01**: the phenomenal/psychological split and the pairing of consciousness with awareness. The claim that a psychological state underlies every phenomenal state (p. 173) is what makes irrelevance to ordinary behaviour tolerable. The remark that consciousness is surprising and claims about it are not expands a ch01 claim (p. 177).
- **Depends on ch02**: logical supervenience as the condition for inherited explanatory relevance (p. 170); primary and secondary intensions and centred worlds carry all of sections 7 and 8 (pp. 192-198).
- **Depends on ch03**: premise (3) of the paradox is ch03's conclusion (p. 175). The zombie of ch03 is here given a psychology and made to argue. Ch03's endnote 9 on Dennett's use of seeming is cashed out on pp. 181-182. Ch03's reply to vitalism (the vital spirit as explanatory construct) is the same move as the reply to the god analogy here (pp. 178-179).
- **Depends on ch04**: causal closure and the claim that interactionism does not escape the problem (p. 174); the taxonomy of type-B and of the intrinsic-nature view, both said to inherit the paradox (p. 172); the decision to leave causal irrelevance open (p. 168). Causal irrelevance is left open there, and this chapter proceeds as if it might hold (p. 182).
- **Feeds ch06**: first-order judgments and the sharing of content between awareness and consciousness (p. 167) become the coherence principles. Endnote 1 to ch06 says second-order judgments must be read relationally for those principles to be psychophysical laws, because beliefs involving the qualitative concept do not logically supervene (p. 361). The blindsight aside (p. 181) points to ch06.
- **Feeds ch08**: the design sketch of why a reflective system would make phenomenal judgments is to be worked out there (p. 175).
- **Shift in the meaning of judgment and belief (my observation)**: the chapter starts by subtracting phenomenal quality from belief to get judgment and saying it will not make much of the distinction (pp. 165-166). The main defence in section 5 is said not to depend on it (p. 183). Section 8 then argues that phenomenal beliefs really do contain more than judgments (p. 198) and uses this to say it is not true that the same beliefs would have been present without the experience (p. 199). The distinction grows from a labelling convenience to a substantive thesis about content that fails to supervene on the physical.
- **Shift in the strength of irrelevance (my observation)**: section 2 separates explanatory from causal irrelevance and leaves the second open (p. 168). Sections 5 to 7 concede causal irrelevance for the sake of argument (p. 182). By p. 198 the quality is said to play no direct role in governing cognitive processes, and ineffability is counted as support. The chapter drifts toward treating epiphenomenalism as the working view.
- **Internal tension (my observation)**: the reply to the debunking argument (section 3) needs evidence of experience that is independent of judgment, and the account of that evidence comes only in section 5. Section 5 in turn dismisses the denier of certainty as someone who has already left (p. 187). Each section leans on the other, and both lean on the premise ch03 called taking consciousness seriously.
- **Internal tension (my observation)**: experience justifies the belief (p. 187), yet the belief's formation, its persistence, and the subject's every assertion of being justified are explained without the experience (p. 189). The match between belief and experience then holds by psychophysical law, which is the reliabilist structure he rejected as too weak on p. 185. Acquaintance is meant to add something to that structure; the chapter says what it is not (belief, knowledge, causation) more than what it is.
- **Internal tension (my observation)**: the argument against skeptical scenarios says there is no scenario in which everything seems the same without consciousness, since experience partly constitutes how things seem (p. 186). In the psychological sense of seeming, which he distinguishes on pp. 181-182, the zombie scenario is exactly one in which everything seems the same. The argument therefore requires the phenomenal sense, and so assumes what the skeptic about consciousness questions.
- **Tension with ch03 (my observation)**: ch03 argued that a logically supervenient property is detectable from the third person. Here the content of phenomenal beliefs is said not to supervene logically on the physical (p. 198). So some of belief content joins consciousness on the nonphysical side, which narrows the claim made at the opening of this chapter and in ch01 that cognition is explained functionally (p. 164).

## Open questions

- What is acquaintance, positively? It is said to be epistemic, not conceptual, not belief, not causal (p. 188). How does a nonconceptual relation provide evidence for a conceptual state? Endnote 6 calls this a difficult question shared with perceptual knowledge (pp. 358-359). Does any later chapter return to it?
- If the zombie's certainty is functionally identical to mine, what is the certainty that the argument on pp. 185-186 appeals to: a psychological state (which the zombie has) or an epistemic status (which is what is to be shown)?
- Why does the judgment line up with the experience at all? The chapter appeals to nomic correlation (p. 185). Is the fit between what we judge about experience and what experience is like a brute feature of the psychophysical laws, and is that addressed in ch06 to ch08?
- Does the principle that explanatory relevance needs a conceptual connection (p. 172) prove too much? Fundamental physical properties are connected to their effects by contingent law, and are explanatorily relevant. What distinguishes psychophysical laws?
- The god analogy is limited by endnote 2 (p. 358). If religious experience is itself a datum, how different in kind is the theist's position from the one Chalmers claims for consciousness?
- Section 8 holds that experience gets inside the concept R (p. 198). The judgment involving R is physically realised and shared with an inverted twin. In what does my grasp of R, as opposed to G, consist, if nothing in my cognitive processing differs?
- Is the retreat to pseudo-remembering (p. 192) available for knowledge too? If so, would pseudo-knowledge by reliable correlation be all that the nonreductive view can secure, and would that be enough for premise (4)?
- He declines to engage the private language argument (p. 360). The qualitative concept is close to its target; synthesis should record that this objection is unanswered in the text.
