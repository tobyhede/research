## Question this chapter answers

What is the law of gravitation, how was it found and tested, how far does it reach, and what does it leave unexplained?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| law of gravitation | p. 68 | Every object attracts every other with a force proportional to the mass of each and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. |
| ellipse | p. 69 | The specific curve traced with two tacks and a loop of string; the set of points whose distances from two foci add to a constant. |
| Kepler's laws | p. 70 | Planets move in ellipses with the sun at a focus; the radius sweeps equal areas in equal times; the squares of the periods go as the cubes of the orbit sizes. |
| principle of inertia | p. 70 | A body left undisturbed coasts forever at uniform speed in a straight line. |
| force | p. 70 | What is needed to change the speed or direction of a body's motion; proportional to mass times acceleration (p. 71). |
| mass | p. 71 | The measure of how much force is needed for a given acceleration; a measure of inertia (p. 81). |
| falling (of the moon) | p. 72 | Departing from the straight line the body would follow with no force, without necessarily coming closer. |

| machinery | p. 79 | A mechanism that would explain why a law holds; none exists for gravitation. |

## Leading propositions

**Finding the law**

- The law of gravitation and the law of motion together say everything needed; a sufficiently talented mathematician could deduce all the consequences (pp. 68–69).
- Tycho Brahe's idea was that the debates about planetary motion would best be settled by measuring positions accurately; careful measurement is better than deep philosophical argument (p. 69).
- Kepler found three simple laws in Tycho's tables (pp. 69–70).
- Galileo found the principle of inertia; why a body keeps coasting is not known (p. 70).
- Newton added that force is needed to change speed or direction (pp. 70–71).
- So no force is needed along a planet's path; the force that matters is toward the sun (p. 71).
- Equal areas in equal times means the force is exactly radial (p. 71).
- Kepler's third law means the force falls off as the inverse square of the distance (p. 71).
- Newton generalised: everything pulls everything else (pp. 71–72).

**Testing it**

- The moon falls toward the earth each second by about the amount the inverse-square law gives from the fall of objects at the surface (p. 72).
- Newton's first calculation disagreed because of a wrong figure for the earth's size; he withheld it, and it agreed six years later with the corrected figure (p. 72).
- A bullet fired horizontally at about five miles a second falls around the earth; that is an orbit (pp. 72–73).
- A new law is useful only if more can be taken out than was put in (p. 73).
- Newton put in Kepler's second and third laws and got out the moon's motion and the elliptical orbit (pp. 73–74).
- The law explains the tides, including why there are two a day (p. 74).
- It explains why the earth is round and slightly flattened (pp. 74–75).
- The apparent irregularity of Jupiter's moons could have killed the theory; it turned out to show that light takes time to travel (p. 75).
- A law that fails in one place where it should work is simply wrong (p. 75).
- Uranus's deviations led Adams and Le Verrier to predict a new planet, which was then found (pp. 75–76).

**How far it reaches**

- Double stars orbit each other in ellipses, as the law requires (pp. 76–77).
- Globular clusters, galaxies and clusters of galaxies show gravitational attraction at ever larger scales (p. 77).
- For galaxies it cannot be proved that the law is exactly inverse square, only that there is attraction (p. 77).
- Whether a star has been seen forming is debatable (pp. 77–78).
- Cavendish measured the force between lead balls in the laboratory, which gives G and so the mass of the earth (p. 78).
- The success of this law gave later science its confidence that other phenomena might have simple laws (pp. 78–79).

**What it leaves unexplained**

- Newton made no hypothesis about the machinery of gravity, and nobody has supplied one since (p. 79).
- Physical laws characteristically have this abstract character; why mathematics describes nature without a mechanism, no one knows (p. 79).
- A mechanism of particles streaming through space gives the inverse square but would also slow the earth in its orbit, so it fails (pp. 79–80).
- No machinery has been invented that explains gravity without predicting something that does not happen (p. 80).
- Gravity has not been explained in terms of other forces, though the electrical force has the same dependence on distance (p. 80).
- The ratio of gravitational to electrical force between two electrons is a pure number of about one part in ten to the forty-second power, and where it comes from is unknown (pp. 80–81).
- The suggestion that it is tied to the age of the universe would make gravity change with time; an argument from the sun's brightness and early life counts against this, but is not very convincing (p. 81).
- Gravity is proportional to inertial mass to about one part in a thousand million (pp. 81–82).
- Newton's law is not correct: Einstein modified it, and light is deflected by the sun as the new law predicts (p. 82).
- The law has not yet been made consistent with quantum mechanics (pp. 82–83).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 70) an undisturbed body moves in a straight line + (p. 71) a planet's path deviates from the straight line at right angles to its motion.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 71) the force on a planet points toward the sun, not along the orbit.
- **Premises**: (p. 72) a body at the earth's surface, 4000 miles from the centre, falls 16 feet in the first second + (p. 72) the moon is about 60 times as far away + (p. 72) under an inverse-square law it should fall 16 feet divided by 60 squared.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 72) the predicted fall matches the fall computed from the moon's orbit and period. The two fractions are missing from the text; the prose says both come to roughly the same fraction of an inch.
- **Premises**: (p. 73) the earth's surface drops 16 feet below a tangent line over a certain horizontal distance + (p. 73) by a theorem of plane geometry that distance is the mean proportional between 16 feet and the earth's diameter, about five miles.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 73) a body moving five miles a second horizontally keeps falling and never gets closer.
- **Premises**: (p. 74) the moon's pull is balanced at the earth's centre by the motion of earth and moon about a common point + (p. 74) water on the near side is pulled more than average and water on the far side less.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 74) the water bulges away from the centre on both sides, giving two tides.
- **Premises**: (p. 79) a flux of particles partly absorbed by matter would push the earth toward the sun with an inverse-square force + (p. 80) the moving earth would meet more of them from the front.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 80) the earth would be slowed and would long since have left its orbit, so the mechanism is wrong.
- **Premises**: (p. 82) Newton's gravity acts instantaneously, so it could signal at infinite speed + (p. 82) signals cannot go faster than light.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 82) Newton's law must be wrong and needs correcting for the delay.

## Evidence offered

- Tycho's tables from Hven (p. 69).
- The apparatus in which one ball is shot sideways and another dropped at the same moment, and they collide in mid-air (p. 72).
- Gagarin's orbit at about five miles a second (p. 73).
- Roemer's observation of Jupiter's moons and the first estimate of the speed of light, dated in the text to 1656 (p. 75).
- The orbit of Sirius B about Sirius A measured from 1862 to 1904 (p. 76).
- Photographs of a globular cluster, a galaxy, a cluster of galaxies, a dust cloud and a region where two bright spots appeared within seven years (pp. 77–78).
- Cavendish's torsion balance (p. 78).
- The experiment of Eötvös in 1909, repeated by Dicke, on the proportionality of mass and weight (pp. 81–82).
- Starlight displaced near the sun during an eclipse (p. 82).

## Open questions

- Why inertia holds is not known (p. 70), and why gravity is exactly proportional to inertial mass is noted as remarkable and left unexplained (pp. 81–82).
- The origin of the enormous ratio between electrical and gravitational forces (pp. 80–81) is open; Feynman reports two speculations and endorses neither.
- The argument against a changing gravitational constant (p. 81) is called not very convincing by its own author.
- Dates as printed look wrong against the usual history: the debates about planetary motion are placed at the beginning of the fifteenth century (p. 69) and Roemer's measurement in 1656 (p. 75). This note cannot settle from the text whether the slips are Feynman's or the edition's.
- The evidence that stars have been seen forming rests on one pair of photographs, and Feynman himself calls two new stars in seven years improbable (p. 78).
- The steps from Kepler's second law to a radial force and from the third law to the inverse square are asserted, with a promise of proof later (p. 71). The proofs are outside this book.
- The quantitative moon calculation cannot be checked from this text because the two fractions are missing (p. 72). The period of the moon is given as approximately 29 days (p. 72).
