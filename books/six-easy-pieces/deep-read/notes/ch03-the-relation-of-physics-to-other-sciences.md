## Question this chapter answers

What are the fundamental problems of the other sciences, how far does physics explain them, and what can physics not do for them?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| natural philosophy | p. 43 | The older name for what physics now is, and the source of most modern sciences. |
| science (as opposed to not a science) | p. 44 | A natural science is one whose test of validity is experiment. Mathematics and love are not sciences in this sense, which is no criticism of them. |
| in principle | p. 44 | Explained by the laws without our being able to carry out the calculation; the chess player who knows the rules and cannot play well. |
| statistical mechanics | p. 44 | The method of statistics applied where mechanical laws hold and the particles are too many to follow; the science of heat. |
| activation energy | p. 47 | The energy hill between two similar substances that stops most reactions from happening. |
| enzyme | p. 48 | A very large molecule that holds smaller molecules so that a reaction can go around the hill; a protein; unchanged by the reaction. |
| protein | p. 49 | A chain of amino acids, of which there are twenty kinds. |
| DNA | p. 50 | The substance in the cell nucleus that carries the information for making enzymes and can reproduce itself; a pair of chains with complementary cross-links. |
| isotope | p. 49 | Atoms of the same element, chemically the same, with different numbers of neutrons; used as labels to trace reactions. |
| turbulent flow | p. 53 | Unstable, swirling fluid motion that cannot be analysed from first principles. |
| historical question | p. 55 | How did it get that way? A kind of question asked in biology, geology and astronomy and not, at the time, in physics. |

## Leading propositions

**Introduction and chemistry**

- Physics is the most fundamental and all-inclusive of the sciences (p. 43).
- Mathematics is not a natural science, because its test is not experiment; being not a science is not a fault (pp. 43–44).
- The rules of inorganic chemistry were ultimately explained in principle by quantum mechanics, so theoretical chemistry is physics (p. 44).
- The explanation is only in principle; it is very hard to predict what a given reaction will do (p. 44).
- Statistical mechanics was developed by physics and chemistry together to handle numbers of atoms too large to follow (pp. 44–45).
- Organic substances are the same kind of thing as inorganic ones, with more complicated arrangements of atoms (p. 45).

**Biology**

- Biology once helped physics: conservation of energy was first demonstrated by Mayer on living creatures (p. 45).
- A nerve impulse is a wave of changed permeability running along a membrane across which ions have been pumped (pp. 45–46).
- How a muscle turns a chemical signal into motion is not known (p. 46).
- Nerves and muscles are not fundamental to life; the common feature of living things is the cell and its chemical machinery (pp. 46–47).
- Most reactions in the cell would not go without enzymes, which get around the activation energy (pp. 47–48).
- An enzyme does not care which direction the reaction goes; if it did, a law of physics would be violated (p. 48).
- Experimental physics made biochemistry possible through isotope tracers (pp. 48–49).
- The spatial arrangement of atoms in some proteins has been found, but it does not show why the protein works (pp. 49–50).
- DNA is the blueprint: it copies itself because each letter on one chain fixes its complement on the other (pp. 50–51).
- How the order of units in DNA fixes the order of amino acids in a protein is the central unsolved problem in biology (p. 51).
- The most powerful assumption in the attempt to understand life is that everything living things do can be understood as the jiggling of atoms (p. 51).

**Astronomy, geology, psychology**

- Astronomy is older than physics and started it (p. 51).
- The stars are made of the same kinds of atoms as the earth, as spectroscopy shows (pp. 51–52).
- We understand the interior of the sun better than the interior of the earth (p. 52).
- Stars shine by nuclear burning of hydrogen, and the elements we are made of were made in stars (pp. 52–53).
- Weather cannot be predicted, although the equations for air are known, because the flow is unstable (p. 53).
- Mountain building, volcanoes and earthquakes are not understood; the theory is of circulating currents inside the earth (pp. 53–54).
- Physics cannot yet say how dense matter should be at the pressures in the earth's centre (p. 54).
- Psychoanalysis is not a science, because it has not been checked by experiment (p. 54).
- Nobody knows what changes in the nervous system when something is learned (pp. 54–55).

**How did it get that way?**

- Physics can help another science only when given a description of the object in the physicist's language: where the atoms are (p. 55).
- The other sciences have a historical question; physics has none at present, because the laws are not thought to change with time, though they may (pp. 55–56).
- Turbulent fluid flow is an old unsolved problem of physics that matters to all the sister sciences (p. 56).
- The division of nature into separate sciences is our convenience; nature does not know it (pp. 56–57).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 44) the rules of chemical combination follow from quantum mechanics + (p. 44) knowing the rules of chess is different from being able to play.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 44) chemistry is physics in principle and remains a separate working science in practice.
- **Premises**: (p. 47) two similar molecules are separated by an energy hill + (p. 47) a large molecule could hold the pieces so that the change goes around the hill.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 48) with the enzyme present, the reaction goes; without it, mostly not.
- **Premises**: (p. 50) only certain pairs of cross-links fit opposite each other + (p. 51) when the chains split, only the complementary units attach to each half.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 51) each half-chain rebuilds its partner, so the molecule reproduces its sequence.
- **Premises**: (p. 52) the ratio of isotopes is not changed by chemical reactions, only by nuclear ones + (pp. 52–53) the ratios found in ordinary matter are those a stellar furnace would give.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 53) our elements were very likely made in stars and thrown out in explosions.
- **Premises**: (p. 53) erosion is constantly lowering the land + (p. 53) mountains are no lower on average than in the past.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 53) there must be mountain-forming processes, and they are not understood.
- **Premises**: (p. 54) psychoanalysis has not been checked carefully by experiment + (p. 54) there is no count of cases where it works and where it does not.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 54) it is not a science, whatever its use as a medical practice.

## Evidence offered

- The Krebs cycle chart as a sample of perhaps one percent of the cell's reactions (p. 47).
- Muscle fibre in water contracts when the energy-carrying phosphate compound is added (p. 48).
- Carbon isotopes as tracers (p. 49).
- Protein structures with over a thousand atoms located, the first being hemoglobin (p. 50).
- Helium found on the sun and technetium in cool stars before either was found on earth (p. 52).
- Water going smoothly over a dam and breaking into drops, as a picture of instability (p. 53).
- The pipe: nobody can compute from first principles the pressure needed to push water through at high speed (p. 56).
- The anecdote of the man who knew why the stars shine (p. 52).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The chess distinction between knowing the rules and playing (p. 44) is taken from ch02 (ch02, p. 29) and is the hinge of this chapter.
- The atomic assumption about life (p. 51) repeats ch01's hypothesis that everything animals do, atoms do (ch01, p. 27).
- Mayer's work on living creatures (p. 45) is the historical origin of the law treated in ch04.
- The remark that an enzyme cannot prefer one direction without violating a law of physics (p. 48) relies on reversibility and conservation of energy as set out in ch04 (ch04, pp. 60–61).
- Statistical mechanics lets physics treat stellar interiors (p. 52), yet the same physics fails for the earth's interior (p. 54) and for turbulence (p. 56). The chapter's claim of fundamentality (p. 43) and its list of failures are both sincere; the reconciliation is the phrase in principle (p. 44).
- Feynman asks whether the laws of physics change with time and says there is no sign of it (p. 56). ch05 considers one concrete proposal, a changing gravitational constant, and finds the evidence against it unconvincing but leaves the matter open (ch05, pp. 80–81).
- The footnote on poets (pp. 100–101) and the closing glass of wine (pp. 56–57) answer in advance the worry that reduction to atoms diminishes things, which ch01 also addresses in its last paragraph (ch01, pp. 27–28).
- The definition of science by experimental test (p. 44) is the criterion from ch01 (ch01, p. 16) applied as a boundary.

## Open questions

- Feynman calls the atomic account of life an assumption (p. 51) and the chapter gives its fruitfulness as the reason to hold it. He offers no argument that it must be true, and none against alternatives.
- The treatment of psychology is brief and dismissive of everything but physiology (pp. 54–55). The witch-doctor comparison is an analogy, not evidence.
- From outside the book: the coding problem (p. 51) was solved in the years just after these lectures, and the account of mountain building (pp. 53–54) predates plate tectonics. The text cannot confirm either point; they are noted so that later readers do not take the 1963 picture as current.
- Which of the listed open problems were open because of missing laws and which because of intractable calculation? Feynman distinguishes the two for nuclear forces (ch02, p. 40) but not systematically here.
- The historical question (pp. 55–56) is said not to arise in physics. The origin of the universe is mentioned as part of astronomy (p. 56), which suggests the boundary was already unstable.
