## Question this chapter answers

How do things behave on the atomic scale, what is the least that must be said to describe that behavior correctly, and can it be explained?

## Leading propositions

**Setting**

- Things on a very small scale behave like nothing in direct experience: not like waves, particles, clouds or billiard balls (p. 83).
- Light and electrons were each taken first for one thing and then the other; they are like neither (pp. 83–84).
- All atomic objects behave in the same way, so what is learned from electrons applies to photons and the rest (p. 84).
- Even experts do not understand it as they would like, because human intuition is formed on large objects (p. 84).
- The two-slit phenomenon cannot be explained classically and contains the only mystery of quantum mechanics (p. 84).
- The mystery cannot be explained; one can only say how it works (p. 84).

**Three experiments**

- Bullets arrive in identical lumps and the probability with both holes open is the sum of the single-hole probabilities (pp. 85–86).
- Water waves have intensity of any size, and the two-hole intensity is not the sum of the single-hole intensities; there is an interference term (pp. 86–88).
- Electrons arrive in identical lumps, one at a time, like bullets (pp. 88–89).
- The electron probability with both holes open is not the sum of the single-hole probabilities; it has the wave pattern (p. 89).
- No account in terms of electrons taking complicated paths has reproduced the pattern (p. 90).
- The mathematics is simple: each hole has a complex amplitude, the probability is the absolute square, and with both holes open the amplitudes add before squaring (p. 90).
- So electrons arrive like particles, and the probability of arrival is distributed like the intensity of a wave (p. 90).
- In quantum mechanics the amplitudes must be complex numbers; real parts alone will not do (p. 90).

**Watching**

- With a light source that shows which hole each electron went through, every electron is seen at one hole or the other (p. 91).
- The distribution of the watched electrons shows no interference; turning off the light restores it (p. 92).
- Dimming the light does not weaken the flashes; it only lets some electrons pass unseen, and those unseen ones show interference (pp. 92–93).
- Using redder light gives a gentler jolt, but once the wavelength exceeds the spacing of the holes the flash can no longer say which hole (pp. 93–94).
- It is impossible to arrange the light so as to tell which hole and leave the pattern undisturbed (p. 94).
- Heisenberg proposed this as a general principle, and quantum mechanics depends on it; if it were ever beaten the theory would have to be discarded (p. 94).
- One may say the electron went through one hole or the other only when there is apparatus capable of telling; otherwise saying so leads to wrong predictions (pp. 94–95).
- Bullets show no pattern because their wavelength is so small that any detector averages over many wiggles (p. 95).

**Principles**

- The probability of an event in an ideal experiment is the absolute square of a probability amplitude (p. 96).
- When an event can happen in several alternative ways, the amplitudes add, and there is interference (p. 96).
- If the experiment can determine which alternative is taken, the probabilities add, and interference is lost (p. 96).
- No one has found any machinery behind this law (p. 96).
- Physics has given up on predicting exactly what will happen in a given circumstance; only probabilities can be predicted (p. 96).
- An electron with internal works that decided its hole and landing point in advance would give the sum of the single-hole patterns, which is not what is observed (pp. 96–97).
- Feynman suspects strongly, without claiming to know, that this limit is permanent (p. 97).
- Measuring the recoil of a movable plate to find the hole requires knowing the plate's momentum, which leaves its position uncertain enough to smear the pattern (pp. 97–98).
- The uncertainty principle protects quantum mechanics from contradiction (p. 98).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 89) if each electron goes through hole 1 or hole 2, arrivals divide into two classes and the total is the sum of the two single-hole distributions + (p. 89) the observed total is not that sum.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 90–91) Proposition A appears to be false.
- **Premises**: (p. 91) with a light on, each electron is seen at exactly one hole + (p. 92) the watched electrons' total distribution is the plain sum, with no interference.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 92) looking changes the distribution. Proposition A holds when the electrons are watched, and the earlier pattern belongs to the unwatched case.
- **Premises**: (p. 93) the jolt from a photon depends on its momentum, which is inversely proportional to wavelength + (p. 94) two spots closer than about a wavelength cannot be told apart.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 94) light gentle enough to preserve the pattern is too coarse to tell the holes apart. The two requirements cannot be met together.
- **Premises**: (p. 96) suppose machinery inside the electron fixes its hole and its landing point before it starts + (p. 96) what is inside the electron should not depend on whether we open or close a hole.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 97) electrons that chose hole 1 would land as in the single-hole case, likewise for hole 2, and the total would be the plain sum. Experiment says otherwise, so such machinery seems impossible.
- **Premises**: (p. 97) to tell the hole from the plate's recoil, the plate's momentum must be known beforehand to sufficient accuracy + (p. 97) by the uncertainty principle its position is then uncertain + (p. 97) the holes, and so the centre of the pattern, are then in a different place for each electron.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 97–98) the pattern is smeared by about the distance from a maximum to a minimum, and interference is lost. The quantitative step is promised for the next chapter of the Lectures and is not in this book.

## Evidence offered

- The chapter's experiments are imagined. Feynman says the bullet result is imagined (p. 85) and that the electron experiment has never been done in just this way, the scale being impossibly small; the results are known from many experiments at other scales (p. 88).
- The detector clicks: all the same size, arriving erratically, at a steady average rate, never two detectors at once (pp. 88–89).
- The observation that closing a hole can increase the count at some points and decrease it at the centre, used against complicated-path explanations (pp. 89–90).
- The three-column count with a dim light: seen at hole 1, seen at hole 2, not seen (p. 93).
- The standing of quantum mechanics as a successful theory is given as support for the uncertainty principle (p. 94).
- The claim that nobody has found a way to measure position and momentum more accurately than the principle allows (p. 98).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- ch02 asserted that there are no internal wheels and that exact prediction is impossible (ch02, p. 37). This chapter supplies the argument (pp. 96–97).
- ch02 said there is no distinction between a wave and a particle (ch02, p. 38). Here the wording is that an electron is like neither, and behaves sometimes like one and sometimes like the other only in a stated sense (pp. 84, 90).
- The uncertainty principle is given in ch02 as a relation between position and momentum that explains the size of atoms (ch02, p. 36). Here the general form is about distinguishing alternatives, and the position and momentum form is called a special case (p. 97).
- The refrain that no machinery lies behind the law (p. 96) matches energy (ch04, p. 57) and gravitation (ch05, p. 79).
- Feynman calls the loss of exact prediction a retrenchment and possibly a backward step (p. 96). In ch02 he had called the same fact horrible and then dismissed the philosophers who thought it fatal to science (ch02, p. 37). The attitude is consistent: regret without retreat.
- The argument for the uncertainty principle from the movable plate (pp. 97–98) uses the principle to defend itself. Feynman presents this openly as a consistency check, showing the relation must hold in order to keep from getting into trouble (p. 97), not as a proof.
- Lumps of light (p. 93) against ch04's statement that energy does not come in fixed blobs (ch04, pp. 59, 67): reconciled there by the fact that frequency can be anything.
- Both prefaces describe this chapter as presenting path integrals (fm01, p. 7; fm02, p. 10). The text gives the rule for adding amplitudes of alternatives (p. 96) and never mentions paths in that sense.
- Feynman's own preface doubts the success of his quantum mechanics teaching (fm02, pp. 13–14).

## Open questions

- What counts as an apparatus capable of determining the alternative (p. 96)? The rule turns on this and the chapter defines it only through examples: the light source and the recoiling plate.
- The hidden-machinery argument assumes the electron's inner state cannot depend on whether the other hole is open (p. 96). The chapter states this as something we must not forget, without defending it. From outside the book: later discussion of hidden variables centres on this kind of assumption. The text hedges with the phrase so far as we know and by saying at the present time (pp. 96–97).
- Why must amplitudes be complex (p. 90)? Called a technical point and left.
- Is the disturbance by the photon the reason interference is lost, or only an illustration of a deeper rule? The watching section argues from disturbance (pp. 92–93); the summary states the rule in terms of what the experiment is capable of determining (p. 96). The chapter does not say which is more basic.
- The chapter says the electron experiment had never been done in this form (p. 88). The book cannot say whether it has been done since.
- One inline formula in this edition gives the second single-hole probability in terms of the first amplitude and omits a square on the combined expression (p. 90). These look like misprints; the surrounding sentences and the summary (p. 96) give the intended relations.
