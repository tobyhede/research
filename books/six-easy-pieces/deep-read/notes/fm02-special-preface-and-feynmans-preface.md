# fm02: Special Preface and Feynman's Preface (pp. 8–15)

Scope note: the unit file opens with the end of Davies's Introduction (p. 8), covered in fm01, and ends with the opening of Chapter 1 (p. 15). The Special Preface by David L. Goodstein and Gerry Neugebauer (April 1989) runs pp. 8–11. Feynman's Preface (June 1963) runs pp. 11–15. Both were written for The Feynman Lectures on Physics, not for this selection, so they describe a two-year course of which this book holds six lectures.

## Question this chapter answers

Goodstein and Neugebauer: why did Feynman give these lectures, how did the course run, and what did it achieve? Feynman: what was he trying to do, for which students, and did it work?

## Leading propositions

**Special Preface (Goodstein and Neugebauer)**

- Feynman was legendary among scientists before he was publicly famous, and his teaching enlarged the legend (p. 8).
- For him the lecture hall was a theatre and the lecturer a performer (p. 8).
- His teaching method was simple: decide why the students should learn the subject and what they should know, and the method follows by common sense (p. 9).
- In 35 years at Caltech he taught undergraduates only once, in 1961–63 with a brief return in 1964 (p. 9).
- Caltech asked for the course because the existing two years of physics were turning students off (pp. 9–10).
- Feynman had only a vague outline; nobody knew what he would say until he said it (p. 10).
- The likeliest of three reasons he took it on was the challenge of reformulating physics so it could be presented to young students (p. 10).
- His treatment of quantum mechanics presents the path-integral method to beginners (p. 10).
- Many students dreaded the class and attendance by registered students fell, while faculty and graduate students filled the room (p. 10).
- The lectures did not succeed as preparation for examinations or as introductory textbooks (p. 11).
- Their main beneficiaries were his peers (pp. 10–11).
- Feynman himself thought the Lectures would be his most important contribution to physics (p. 11).

**Feynman's Preface**

- The printed lectures are edited, not verbatim, and were only part of a course with recitations and a laboratory (p. 11).
- The problem to be solved was keeping the interest of enthusiastic, able students who had been discouraged by a course with few modern ideas (pp. 11–12).
- The lectures are not a survey; they are serious, and aimed at the most intelligent student in the class (p. 12).
- He tried to make every statement as accurate as possible and to show where it fits and how it would later be modified (p. 12).
- He tried to mark what could be deduced from earlier material and what was simply being added as new (p. 12).
- He did not keep to a strict order; things were mentioned before they could be discussed fully (p. 12).
- He also wanted a core that the weaker student could hold on to (pp. 12–13).
- There was no feedback, and he does not know how good the lectures are (p. 13).
- The quantum mechanics part was not completely successful, and he now thinks it should be given later (pp. 13–14).
- His own verdict on the course is pessimistic: judged by the examinations, the system failed for most students (p. 14).
- One or two dozen students understood almost everything, and they were the ones he was aiming at (p. 14).
- The only real solution in education is a direct relationship between a student and a good teacher; lectures and assigned problems alone teach little (pp. 14–15).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 10) Feynman measured understanding by whether a thing could be explained to freshmen + (p. 10) he could not do so for the spin-statistics connection.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 10) in his view the physics community did not really understand it.
- **Premises**: (p. 10) registered students stopped coming + (p. 14) most handled examination problems poorly + (pp. 10–11) faculty kept attending.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 11) the lectures failed as a course and succeeded as a restatement of physics for physicists.
- **Premises**: (p. 13) applications in chemistry and engineering do not use the full differential-equation machinery + (p. 13) the usual route makes quantum mechanics unavailable to most students.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 13) it is worth trying to present quantum principles without partial differential equations. Feynman judges the attempt incomplete (pp. 13–14).
- **Premises**: (p. 14) it is impossible to learn much by sitting in a lecture or doing assigned problems + (p. 15) there are too many students for individual teaching.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 15) lectures are a substitute for the ideal, and may at best supply ideas to individual teachers and students.

## Evidence offered

- The 1952 note found in the Caltech archives stating his teaching philosophy (p. 9).
- The licence-plate story, used to show why an idea must not be verified with the data that suggested it (p. 9).
- Course facts: 180 students, two lectures a week, recitation groups, weekly laboratory (p. 11).
- Teaching record: 34 courses in 35 years, 25 of them advanced graduate courses (p. 9).
- Feynman's reading of the examination results (p. 14).
- Details of what was left out: three problem-solving lectures, a lecture on inertial guidance; two lectures were given by Matthew Sands (p. 14).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Feynman's stated practice of flagging what is deduced and what is added (p. 12) is visible throughout: ch04 derives the potential-energy formula and then says it must be checked (ch04, p. 62); ch05 says of a result that the proof is not given in this course (bm01, p. 101).
- His policy of mentioning things before they can be discussed (p. 12) explains the many forward references in the six chapters, most of which point to lectures outside this book (ch06, pp. 97–98).
- The publisher markets the chapters as easy and for general readers (fm01, p. 1). Feynman says he addressed the most intelligent in the class and wanted even them unable to take in everything (p. 12). The six chosen chapters are the qualitative ones, so both can hold, but the book's title is the publisher's judgment, not his.
- Goodstein and Neugebauer say ch06 presents the path-integral method (p. 10). The chapter itself gives only the amplitude rules for two alternatives (ch06, p. 96).
- Feynman's view that quantum mechanics should come later in a course (p. 14) bears on ch06, which opens by saying the subject cannot be entered properly until much later (ch06, p. 83).
- The licence-plate point about not testing an idea on the data that suggested it (p. 9) complements ch05's standard that a law must give back more than was put in (ch05, p. 73).

## Open questions

- Which of the six chapters fall in the first year that Feynman judged satisfactory (p. 13) and which in the quantum material he judged unsuccessful? ch06 is an introduction given within the first-year sequence, so the criticism at pp. 13–14 seems aimed at Volume III, but the preface does not say.
- Feynman's pessimism rests on examination performance (p. 14). The preface writers treat the same outcome as a different kind of success (p. 11). Neither offers data beyond attendance and impression.
- The preface writers' account of his motives is offered as speculation (p. 10).
