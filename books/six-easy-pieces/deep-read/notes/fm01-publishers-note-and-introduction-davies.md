# fm01: Publisher's Note and Introduction (Paul Davies) (pp. 1–8)

Scope note: the unit file opens with the table of contents and the list of Feynman's other books (p. 1), which are not covered. The Publisher's Note runs pp. 1–2. All propositions below are the publisher's or Davies's, not Feynman's.

## Question this chapter answers

Publisher: what is this book and why these chapters? Davies: who was Feynman, what was distinctive about how he did and taught physics, and what should a general reader look for in the six chapters?

## Key terms

## Leading propositions

**Publisher's Note**

- The book exists to bring a substantial but nontechnical physics primer to a wide audience, and it consists of the six easiest chapters of the 1963 Lectures (p. 1).
- Two prefaces from the Lectures are reprinted because they give context for the pieces and for Feynman (p. 1).

**Introduction (Davies)**

- Science is not impersonal; it is driven by people and subject to fashion, and the fashion is set by how leading figures think about the world (p. 2).
- Newton, Einstein and Feynman were successive icons, each with a different way of doing physics (p. 2).
- Feynman came after the revolutions of relativity and quantum mechanics and helped build on those foundations (p. 2).
- He was a theorist who stayed close to experiment, unlike Einstein, whom Davies calls contemptuous of experiment (p. 2).
- His route to a consistent QED was radically different from his contemporaries': he could more or less write down answers without the standard mathematics, helped by his diagrams (p. 3).
- His talent was to approach mainstream topics in an idiosyncratic way, not to find new things in a backwater (pp. 3–4).
- He had a contempt for rigorous formalism, and the same disrespect for authority in life (p. 4).
- The Lectures were a success with readers across the world, and this book is both a primer on physics and a primer on Feynman (pp. 4–5).
- Feynman develops far-reaching ideas from very few concepts and little mathematics, by finding the right analogy (p. 5).
- The selection is a taste of his approach, not a survey of modern physics (p. 5).
- All physics is rooted in the notion of law, but the laws are hidden in the phenomena and need experiment and theory to uncover (p. 5).
- Symmetry and conservation laws order particle physics, and time-translation symmetry directly implies conservation of energy (p. 6).
- The quantum chapter is the most challenging; quantum mechanics is the most successful theory in science and at first sight looks crazy (p. 6).
- Quantum ideas call into question the independent existence of objects with a full set of properties at all times (pp. 6–7).
- Feynman's two-slit analysis is a classic of scientific exposition (p. 7).
- The path-integral formulation may be judged his most significant contribution (p. 7).
- Feynman distrusted philosophers but produced philosophical insight without systematic philosophy; what he disliked was formalism (pp. 7–8).
- The Feynman style suited a physics that was secure in its foundations and open for exploitation; there is unlikely to be another like him (p. 8).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 2) each age follows the approach of a few dominant figures + (p. 3) Feynman's approach was intuitive and anti-formal + (p. 8) postwar physics was mature in its foundations and wide open in its consequences.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 8) the Feynman style was a fit between a personality and a period, which is why it is unlikely to recur.
- **Premises**: (p. 6) nothing in physics, apart from cosmology, distinguishes one moment from the next + (p. 6) physical processes do not depend on an absolute zero of time.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 6) energy is conserved. Davies states the implication and does not derive it.
- **Premises**: (p. 7) Feynman did mathematical physics without systematic mathematics + (pp. 7–8) he gave a skilful account of a philosophical position and then declined to commit to it.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 8) he was not contemptuous of philosophical problems, only of formalism.

## Evidence offered

- Biography: born New York 1918; QED work in the late 1940s; Nobel Prize; died 1988; Caltech students' banner (pp. 2, 4).
- The Challenger demonstration with an elastic band in ice water, as evidence of the practical thinker (p. 2).
- Davies's own conversations with Feynman about mathematics, Platonism and reductionism (pp. 7–8).
- Bohr's remark that anyone not shocked by quantum theory has not understood it, given as a report (p. 6).
- Applications of quantum mechanics from the laser to the microchip (p. 6).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Davies says the symmetry under time translation directly implies conservation of energy (p. 6). Feynman in ch04 is more careful: the link holds if one assumes the symmetry and adds the principles of quantum mechanics, and whether the symmetry is strictly true is not known (ch04, p. 67).
- Davies presents the path-integral picture of an electron exploring all routes (p. 7). ch06 does not use that language; it gives the rule that amplitudes for alternatives add (ch06, p. 96). The connection is Davies's and the special preface's (fm02, p. 10), not something the chapter states.
- Davies says the book leaves no doubt about which discipline is fundamental (p. 5). ch03 confirms the claim and also lists at length what physics cannot do for the other sciences (ch03, pp. 53–56).
- Davies writes that Feynman reads nature without the tedium of analysis (p. 4). Feynman's own preface describes trying very hard to make every statement accurate and to mark what is deduced and what is new (fm02, p. 12). The two pictures are compatible but pull in different directions.
- The gravitation quotation Davies uses is at ch05, p. 77.

## Open questions

- Davies's opening claim that science is as subject to fashion as other human activity (p. 2) sits oddly beside Feynman's insistence that experiment is the sole judge (ch01, p. 16). Davies restricts fashion to ways of thinking, not results, but does not reconcile the two.
- How much of the informal style is Feynman and how much is the editing by Leighton and Sands? The front matter does not say; fm02 notes only that the lectures were edited, sometimes extensively (p. 11).
- The claim that Feynman could write down QED answers without using any mathematics (p. 3) is rhetorical and cannot be checked from this book.
