## Question this chapter answers

Why can physics not be taught from axioms, what tests a law, and what single idea about matter explains the most?

## Leading propositions

**Introduction: what a law is**

- The results of physics can be condensed into laws that summarise all our knowledge (p. 15).
- Physics cannot be taught by stating the laws first, for two reasons: not all the basic laws are known, and stating the correct ones needs unfamiliar ideas and advanced mathematics (p. 15).
- Each piece of nature we learn is an approximation, so things are learned in order to be corrected later (pp. 15–16).
- The test of all knowledge is experiment (p. 16).
- Laws come from imagination working on the hints that experiment gives; they are guesses that are then checked (p. 16).
- An experiment can be wrong only by being inaccurate (p. 16).
- The constant-mass law is false; the true statement is that mass is constant to one part in a million below about a hundred miles a second (p. 16).
- Philosophically the approximate law is completely wrong: a very small effect can require a profound change of ideas (p. 16).
- At each stage it is worth learning what is known, how accurate it is, how it fits in and how it may change (pp. 16–17).

**Matter is made of atoms**

- If one sentence had to carry the most information to a future generation, it would be the atomic hypothesis (p. 17).
- Magnified a billion times, water is a crowd of jiggling molecules that attract one another and resist being squeezed together (pp. 17–18).
- Jiggling is heat; more heat pulls the molecules apart into steam (p. 18).
- Gas pressure is molecular bombardment. It is proportional to density at fixed temperature, and it rises with temperature (pp. 19–20).
- Slow compression heats a gas and slow expansion cools it, because molecules gain speed from an advancing piston (p. 20).
- In a solid the atoms sit in an ordered array, which is why ice is rigid and snowflakes six-sided (p. 20).
- Ice has holes in its structure, so it shrinks on melting, unlike most substances (pp. 20–21).
- Atoms never stop moving, even at absolute zero; helium does not freeze there at ordinary pressure (p. 21).

**Atomic processes**

- Evaporation and condensation go on all the time at a water surface; nothing visible changes because the rates balance (pp. 21–22).
- The molecules that escape are the more energetic ones, so evaporation cools the liquid (pp. 22–23).
- Air dissolves in water by the same process, and comes out as bubbles when the pressure is removed (p. 23).
- Salt dissolves when ions leave the crystal faster than they return; whether it dissolves or crystallises depends on the concentration (pp. 23–24).
- How solubility changes with temperature is hard to predict in general (p. 24).

**Chemical reactions**

- Burning is carbon and oxygen snapping together because they attract each other strongly; the energy appears as molecular motion (pp. 24–25).
- Every substance is some arrangement of atoms, and chemists can find the arrangement (pp. 25–26).
- Physical methods later confirmed that the chemists' arrangements were almost always right (p. 26).
- We know there are atoms because predictions made on that hypothesis come out right, and because of Brownian motion and crystal structure (p. 27).
- The most important hypothesis in biology is that everything animals do, atoms do (p. 27).
- To say we are a pile of atoms is not to say we are merely that; a non-repeating, changing arrangement may do a great deal (pp. 27–28).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 15) not all basic laws are known + (p. 15) the correct laws need unfamiliar concepts and mathematics.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 15) physics must be learned piece by piece, each piece an approximation.
- **Premises**: (p. 19) pressure is the sum of molecular impacts on the piston + (p. 19) doubling the number of molecules at the same speed doubles the impacts.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 19) pressure is proportional to density. Feynman adds that attraction and finite size make small corrections.
- **Premises**: (p. 19) hotter atoms move faster + (pp. 19–20) faster atoms hit harder and more often.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 20) pressure increases with temperature.
- **Premises**: (p. 22) the rate of leaving depends only on the jiggling of the water + (p. 22) the rate of return depends on how many vapour molecules are above the surface.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 22) blowing dry air over water makes it evaporate.
- **Premises**: (p. 22) a molecule escapes only when it has more than the usual energy + (p. 22) those that remain therefore have less average motion.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 23) evaporation cools, so blowing on soup cools it.
- **Premises**: (p. 27) if atoms are always moving, a larger ball among them is pushed unevenly + (p. 27) small particles in water are seen to jiggle perpetually.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 27) the bombardment is real, which is fairly direct evidence for atoms.

## Evidence offered

- Measured geometry of the water molecule: an angle of 105 degrees 3 minutes and an oxygen to hydrogen distance of 0.957 angstrom (pp. 18–19).
- Hexagonal symmetry of ice and the six-sided snowflake (p. 20).
- The ping-pong ball bouncing off a moving paddle, as the model for a molecule gaining speed from a piston (p. 20).
- Divers and dissolved air (p. 23).
- The odour of violets as a known three-dimensional arrangement of atoms, with its long chemical name (pp. 25–27).
- X-ray analysis of crystals agreeing with the angles between natural crystal faces to within seconds of arc (p. 27).
- Footnote 1: a diamond can be burned in air (p. 100).

- The biological hypothesis (p. 27) returns almost word for word at the end of ch03's biology section (ch03, p. 51).
- Feynman calls the atomic idea a hypothesis, a fact, or whatever one wishes (p. 17). That looseness is deliberate and matches his view that all laws are provisional (pp. 15–16), but it means the chapter never says at what point a hypothesis counts as established.
- The teaching question at pp. 16–17, whether to teach the correct strange law or the simple approximate one first, is the design problem described in his preface (fm02, p. 12).

## Open questions

- Feynman says an experiment can be wrong only by being inaccurate (p. 16). That sets aside errors of interpretation, where a correct measurement is read through a wrong theory. He does not discuss them here.
- The evidence for atoms is given as successful prediction plus two more direct cases (p. 27). The chapter does not say how the size of atoms was measured.
- The final passage (pp. 27–28) suggests that complexity of arrangement could account for a person. It is offered as a possibility in the form of a question, not as a result.
- The force law between atoms, attraction at a little distance and repulsion when squeezed, is asserted (p. 17) and its origin deferred to ch02.
