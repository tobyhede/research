## Question this chapter answers

What does it mean to understand nature, and what, in the fewest principles, is the world made of?

## Leading propositions

**Introduction: understanding as knowing the rules**

- Curiosity leads us to try to understand the variety of nature as the result of a small number of elemental things and forces in endless combination (p. 28).
- The world is like a chess game played by the gods; we observe and try to work out the rules, and the rules are what fundamental physics means (p. 29).
- Knowing every rule would still not let us explain each move, because situations are too complicated (p. 29).
- We do not yet have all the rules (p. 29).
- There are three ways to check a guessed rule: simple situations with few parts, less specific rules deduced from it, and rough approximation (pp. 29–30).
- The interesting phenomena are where the rules fail, since that is how new rules are found (p. 30).
- The aim is to see all of nature as aspects of one set of phenomena; so far amalgamation has always been possible, but new things keep turning up (p. 30).
- Whether everything can be amalgamated is not known (p. 31).

**Physics before 1920**

- The old picture: a stage of Euclidean space and time, particles with inertia, and two forces, a complicated short-range one between atoms and gravitation (p. 31).
- On that picture pressure, wind, heat and sound are all motions of particles (pp. 31–32).
- The short-range force is electrical: an inverse-square force far stronger than gravity, with two kinds of charge (p. 32).
- Matter is a fine-grained mixture of positive and negative charge, balanced so exactly that we rarely see the force (p. 32).
- Chemical properties depend only on the number of electrons (p. 33).
- Direct attraction between charges is an inadequate idea; the field is better, because shaking a charge sends an influence much farther than the inverse square would allow (pp. 33–34).
- Light, radio waves, x-rays and gamma rays are the same thing at different frequencies (pp. 34–35).

**Quantum physics**

- Einstein changed the stage to space-time, and curved space-time represents gravitation (p. 35).
- Newton's laws are wrong for atoms; things on a small scale behave like nothing in direct experience (pp. 35–36).
- The uncertainty principle explains why atoms have the size they do: an electron confined to the nucleus would have a large momentum and escape (p. 36).
- It is not possible to predict exactly what will happen; there are no hidden internal wheels (p. 37).
- It is not a requirement of science that the same conditions give the same result. The only requirement is that ideas be tested by experiment (pp. 37–38).
- Waves behave like particles and particles like waves; quantum mechanics unifies field and particle (p. 38).
- Quantum electrodynamics holds the rules for all ordinary phenomena except gravitation and nuclear processes (p. 38).
- It is in principle the theory of all chemistry, and of life if life reduces to chemistry (p. 39).
- It predicted the positron; every particle has an antiparticle (p. 39).

**Nuclei and particles**

- Nuclear forces are far stronger than chemical ones, as an atomic bomb is to TNT (p. 39).
- Yukawa's theory of nuclear forces predicted a particle; the pion fits, but nobody has been able to calculate the theory's consequences in almost twenty years (pp. 39–40).
- About thirty particles are known and their relations are not understood (p. 40).
- Gell-Mann and Nishijima classified them using strangeness, as Mendeléev classified the elements (p. 40).
- The electron and muon differ, as far as anyone can tell, only in mass, and nobody knows why the heavier one exists (p. 42).
- There are four kinds of interaction: nuclear, electrical, beta-decay and gravity, in decreasing strength. The laws of two are known, one partly, one not (pp. 42–43).
- Summary: outside the nucleus we seem to know everything; inside it quantum mechanics still holds, but the particles are not understood (p. 43).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: (p. 29) situations in nature are too complicated to follow move by move + (p. 29) the rules can still be found by watching.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 29) understanding has to mean knowing the rules, and we limit ourselves to that question.
- **Premises**: (p. 32) plus and minus charges close together cancel at a distance + (p. 32) close up, a third charge rearranges them and attraction wins.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 32) atoms feel little force when apart and a strong one when close, and the basis of the force between atoms is electrical.
- **Premises**: (pp. 33–34) a shaken charge affects a distant charge after a delay, and the effect falls off more slowly than the inverse square + (p. 34) corks in water show the same thing when one is jiggled.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 34) direct interaction must be replaced by a field that carries waves.
- **Premises**: (p. 36) an electron sitting on the nucleus would have a precisely known position + (p. 36) the uncertainty principle then requires a large, uncertain momentum and so a large kinetic energy.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 36) the electron cannot stay there; atoms are much bigger than nuclei.
- **Premises**: (p. 37) a pendulum's plane of swing turns in Stockholm and not in Quito, even in a closed room + (p. 37) science survives this.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 37–38) no rule about what results must look like can be laid down in advance; we take what experiment shows and formulate laws to fit.

## Evidence offered

- Two grains of sand thirty metres apart would feel a force of three million tons if their charges were unbalanced (pp. 32–33).
- The frequency ladder from mains pickup through radio, radar and visible light to gamma rays and cosmic rays (pp. 34–35).
- Sizes: the flattened figures give the atom as about a hundred thousand times wider than the nucleus; in a room-sized atom the nucleus is a speck (p. 36).
- The atom that emits light at an unpredictable moment (p. 37).
- A list of things that follow from quantum electrodynamics, from billiard-ball collisions to the colour of neon signs (p. 38).
- Table 2-3 as text: coupling strengths of roughly 1 for mesons to baryons, a hundredth for photons to charges, a hundred-thousandth for weak decays, and forty powers of ten smaller for gravity (pp. 42–43). Footnote 2 defines strength as a dimensionless measure of the coupling (p. 100).
- Particle masses in Mev for the lambda, the omega and the electron (pp. 41–42).

## Open questions

- Feynman says that if one knows the rules one understands the world (p. 29), and also that what can be explained with the rules is very limited (p. 29). The chapter does not settle whether understanding without the ability to compute deserves the name. ch03 lives with the gap.
- The claim that identical conditions need not give identical results (p. 37) is illustrated with examples, the aurora and the pendulum, in which the conditions are in fact different. They show that sameness of conditions is hard to define, which is a weaker point than quantum indeterminacy.
- The remarks on philosophers (p. 37) name no philosopher and quote none.
- The particle survey (pp. 39–43) is of historical interest only. From outside the book: the quark model and later theory reorganised these particles, and the strong and weak laws listed as unknown or partly known were found. This note does not verify those later developments from the text, because the text predates them.
- Whether the amalgamation can be completed, and whether the puzzle has a border, is left open on purpose (p. 31).
