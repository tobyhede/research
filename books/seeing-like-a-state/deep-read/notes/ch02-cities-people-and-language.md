# ch02: Cities, People, and Language (pp. 46–69)

## Question this chapter answers

How did states make towns, persons, speech and movement readable from the centre, and what general character do the resulting "state simplifications" have?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Illegibility as a political resource | p. 46, p. 47 | An unplanned town privileges local knowledge over outside knowledge. Needing a local guide is the test; where one is needed the community has a margin of insulation from outside control. |
| Native tracker / local guide | p. 46 | The insider an outsider must rely on to read an illegible place, name system or language. Reappears for surnames (p. 54) and French (p. 62). |
| Grid city | p. 48 | A settlement of straight lines, right angles and repetition (Chicago), easy to administer and police. Its order is visible from above and outside, not at street level (p. 49). |
| God's-eye view | p. 49 | The helicopter or scale-model perspective from which geometric order is grasped; the view of the planner and the ruler. |
| Miniaturization | p. 49 | Here: scale models on which patron and planner look down, so that plans are judged for sculptural and visual order. |
| Formal order | pp. 49-50 | Visual regimentation with a ceremonial or ideological quality. It has no necessary relationship to the order of life as residents experience it. |
| Permanent patronym / surname | p. 55 | An inherited family name. In almost every case a state project to identify citizens unambiguously; distinguished from a personal second name (p. 57). |
| Administrative fiction | p. 57 | A surname invented by a recording clerk that has no social existence outside the document until state validation of property gives it one (p. 58). |
| Official language | p. 61 | Possibly the most powerful simplification and a precondition of others; in France a domestic colonisation that devalued local knowledge. |
| Legrand Star | pp. 63-64 | The plan for canals and then railways radiating from Paris; centralisation of traffic as "hardwiring" of legibility. |
| State simplifications | p. 66 | Facts that are interested, documentary, static, aggregate and standardised. Not simple-minded: simplified in being schematic and in collapsing distinctions (p. 67). |
| Project of legibility | p. 66 | The never-completed effort, immanent in statecraft, to make society readable; undermined by rivalry within the state, technical obstacles and resistance. |
| Internal colonization | p. 69 | Scott's description of modern statecraft: shaping a people and landscape to fit the techniques of observation, glossed as a civilising mission. |
| Direct rule | p. 65 | Tilly's term: taxation and administration without local intermediaries, made possible by state simplifications. |

## Leading propositions

- An unplanned town such as Bruges is legible to its inhabitants and confusing to strangers; it works like a difficult dialect (p. 46).
- Illegibility "has been and remains a reliable resource for political autonomy" (p. 47).
- Other things equal, a city laid out on a simple repetitive logic is easiest to administer and police (p. 47).
- Geometric order is evident from above and outside, has no necessary relation to lived order, and suits the market as a standardised commodity (pp. 49-50).
- On the relation between formal spatial order and social experience "we must remain agnostic" for now (p. 50).
- Haussmann's rebuilding of Paris followed the logic of scientific forestry, but aimed less at revenue than at governability, health and grandeur, and its central purpose was security against insurrection (p. 51).
- The same works made Paris a far healthier city for those not expelled (p. 52).
- Legibility in Paris was achieved by segregating the population by class and function, and it displaced the poor to a new illegible periphery, Belleville (p. 53).
- Permanent surnames were, after nature and space, the last precondition of modern statecraft, and almost always a state project (p. 55).
- The spread of second names tracks the reach of the state: Florence, then secondary towns, then countryside (p. 56); in England the south and the upper classes first (p. 58).
- State initiative created surnames; it did not just record them (p. 56).
- Colonial naming compresses the process: the 1849 Philippine decree assigned surnames from an alphabetised catalogue (pp. 58-59).
- The Philippine registers failed to record earlier names, so the state "blinded its own hindsight" (p. 60).
- Imposing an official language was a gigantic shift in power toward those who had mastered the code (p. 62).
- The radial road and rail plan served the centre, raised costs, and weakened lateral ties (pp. 63-64).
- State simplifications are indispensable; they allowed the move from indirect to direct rule and can discover new truths, as with disease surveillance (pp. 64-65).
- An illegible society hinders any effective state intervention, "whether the purpose of that intervention is plunder or public welfare" (p. 65).
- Legibility amplifies capacity for discriminating intervention; the Amsterdam map of 1941 guided deportation, and the same capacity could have been used to feed (p. 66).
- The modern state tries to create a population and terrain with the standardised characteristics that are easiest to monitor and manage (p. 68).
- State categories stick because the state can insist on them; in dictatorial settings facts on paper can be made to prevail on the ground (p. 69).

## Arguments

**A. Illegibility protects; legibility exposes (pp. 46-54)**

- **Premises**: (p. 46) Local spatial knowledge is unequally shared between insiders and outsiders. (p. 47) Riots, the Casbah and the bazaar show that this inequality has political value. (pp. 51-52) Haussmann's avenues were designed to move troops into insurrectionary quarters. (p. 54) In 1871 they did.
- **Unstated premises**: that the autonomy protected is worth protecting. The examples chosen are popular movements; the same opacity shelters local bosses and, on Scott's own account, the highest death rates in France (p. 52).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 47) Illegibility is a resource for political autonomy, and urban legibility is first a matter of control.

**B. Formal order is not lived order (pp. 49-50)**

- **Premises**: (p. 49) The grid's symmetry is perceived only from a representation or from the air. (p. 49) Plans at toy scale are judged for visual properties. (p. 50) What eases administration is no guarantee of working for citizens.
- **Unstated premise**: that the planner's perspective and the resident's can diverge systematically. Scott marks this as provisional, to be argued in ch04.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 50) Remain agnostic about the relation of formal spatial order to social experience.

**C. Names follow the state (pp. 55-61)**

- **Premises**: (p. 55) Tax rolls, conscription lists and deeds need a fixed identity. (p. 55) If surnames are a legibility project they should appear first in precocious states; Qin China fits. (pp. 56-57) Within Tuscany and England, incidence falls with distance from officialdom. (p. 58) State validation of inheritance gave people a reason to adopt the official name.
- **Unstated premise**: that correlation with state reach shows state causation and not the effect of commerce, literacy and population density, which follow the same gradient.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 55, p. 61) The surname was a first and crucial step in making individuals officially legible.
- **Endnote evidence on the mechanism**: Matthews on English copyhold supplies a causal link the body only sketches: tenure was recorded on the manorial court roll, and "This system provided a direct incentive to men to keep the same surname" (p. 299) in an endnote. Note that the manorial court is a seigneurial institution, not the crown, so "state" here stretches to any record-keeping authority. The Chinese anchor is softer than the body suggests: how far Qin and Han registration was realised "remains an important question" (p. 299) in an endnote, with Jenner and Woodside disagreeing.

**D. The general claim (pp. 64-69)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 64-65) Officials must reduce detail to categories that permit summary, comparison and aggregation. (pp. 66-67) Such facts are interested, written, static, aggregate, standardised. (p. 68) Once set, categories treat classified cases as homogeneous. (p. 69) The state is the institution best equipped to make its categories stick.
- **Unstated premise**: that the tendency to remake society to fit the grid is a property of states as such and not of particular regimes; Scott hedges by extending it to "many large hierarchical organizations" (p. 69).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 68) The modern state's continually frustrated goal is to reduce social reality to something resembling the administrative grid of its observations.

## Evidence offered

- Cities: Bruges about 1500, Chicago about 1893, Mumford on the baroque city and the commercial uses of the grid (pp. 46-50); Descartes on towns laid out by an engineer (p. 47).
- Paris 1853 to 1869: barricades nine times in the twenty-five years before 1851; cholera killing 18,400 in 1831; annexation of the inner suburbs in 1860; Belleville at sixty thousand by 1856 (pp. 51-53). Sources named in the text include T. J. Clark; the chapter leans on urban historians and Haussmann's own words.
- Names: the Florentine catasto of 1427, where one-third of households in Florence, one-fifth in secondary towns and one-tenth in the countryside declared a second name (p. 56); England, where 90 percent of men shared six Christian names (p. 57); the Claveria decree, forecast to cost twenty thousand pesos and yield one or two hundred thousand a year (pp. 59-60); Jewish emancipation statutes in Austria, France and Prussia (pp. 60-61).
- Language and traffic: Eugen Weber on France (pp. 61-63); the Paris to Strasbourg line through Brie, and the military failure of the radial lines in 1870-71 (p. 64).
- General: Tilly on direct rule (p. 65, p. 68); the Centers for Disease Control (p. 65); the Amsterdam statistical map of May 1941 and sixty-five thousand deported (p. 66); Porter on precision preferred to accuracy (p. 68).
- Endnotes for this chapter (pp. 295-304) were read.
- Sources named in the endnotes: "This part of my discussion is greatly indebted to Merriman’s careful account." (p. 297) in an endnote, with Pinkney, Gaillard, Mumford and T. J. Clark on Paris. The Florentine material is "drawn entirely" (p. 299) in an endnote from Herlihy and Klapisch-Zuber; the Philippine case rests on Abella's edition of the 1849 catalogue (endnote, p. 300); transport on C. O. Smith (endnote, p. 302). Scott says Benedict Anderson's analysis of census and map "has greatly influenced my thinking here" (p. 298) in an endnote.
- The grid's legibility has limits. Parts of grid-planned Manhattan and Chicago remain ungoverned: "No amount of formal order can overcome massive countervailing factors such as poverty, crime, social disorganization, or hostility toward officials." (p. 296) in an endnote. The census undercount of African-Americans was six times that of whites.
- The Casbah's illegibility "was not insurmountable" (p. 295) in an endnote; police work, torture and informers broke the FLN there.
- Age, like the surname, was a state project: Florentine declarations cluster just below eighteen (fiscal adulthood and conscription) and just above sixty (end of capitation), and "the designation of age, in the strict, linear, chronological sense, originates as a state project" (p. 299) in an endnote.
- Weber's figure, given only in an endnote: in the last quarter of the nineteenth century half of the French reaching adulthood had a native tongue other than French (endnote, p. 301).
- The Legrand Star's military cost: reservists in 1914 routed through Paris, Moltke's six lines against France's funnel, and a network "left headless" (p. 302) in an endnote once Paris was surrounded. The page range given for Smith, "pp. 685–71" (p. 302) in an endnote, is a citation slip.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The chapter is the most even-handed in the book on what legibility buys: sewers, epidemiology, vaccination, equal citizenship (p. 52, p. 65). The verdict on Haussmann's Paris is mixed, which the later chapters on Brasilia (ch04) are not.
- Haussmann is said to foreshadow "many of the paradoxes of authoritarian high-modernist planning" (p. 51). Paris met three of the Introduction's four conditions, yet those uprooted "could, and did, strike back" (p. 51); it is a test case for the fourth.
- The agnosticism about formal order and social experience (p. 50) is dropped in ch04.
- The claim that the state remakes society to fit its grid (p. 68) is the bridge from Part 1's description to Part 2's high modernism.
- The Amsterdam map (p. 66) is the book's clearest statement that legibility is an amplifier whose moral sign depends on the purpose supplied.
- An endnote completes the Amsterdam story: because many citizens hid Jews, deportation as an administrative exercise eventually failed, and the occupiers had to rely on "Dutch collaborators who became their local trackers" (p. 303) in an endnote. This ties back to the native-tracker figure (p. 46). The Nazi name decrees of the 1930s, after which "a person’s name alone could single out him or her for deportation or execution" (p. 301) in an endnote, are the surname section's dark counterpart.
- Larry Lohmann objected that officials "are not necessarily any more abstract or narrow of vision in their representation of reality than laypeople are" (p. 303) in an endnote, and would have had Scott drop "simplification"; Scott says he resisted. This is the key term's main recorded challenge, and Scott's reply (that official facts serve institutional roles) shifts the meaning from "thin" toward "interested".
- An endnote lists three problems with official categories: arbitrary coding (French coders differ on up to 20 percent of occupations), state power provoking an off-the-books reality (1970s unemployment), and collectors' interests (Vietnam body counts) (endnote, p. 303). These anticipate ch09's account of how data are shaped.
- Haussmann's plan also made "the fortunes of the small coterie who used their inside knowledge of the plan to profit from real-estate speculation" (p. 297) in an endnote, a market beneficiary of legibility that the body does not mention.

## Open questions

- Scott says the state "blinded its own hindsight" (p. 60). How often do legibility projects destroy information the state needs? The theme is not developed in the body; an endnote offers one further case, French officials keeping duplicate civil registers after the Commune's burning of the Hôtel de Ville destroyed the originals in 1871 (endnote, pp. 300-301).
- Names, an endnote concedes, do not make people legible by themselves: they need citizens to carry and produce them, and "The ultimate identity card, then, is an ineradicable mark on the body" (p. 298) in an endnote. How much of legibility depends on compliance secured through entitlements or penalties is left open.
- An endnote objects to the Philippine decree's genealogical rationale: "As if the Filipinos did not have perfectly adequate oral and written genealogical schemes to achieve the same end." (p. 300) in an endnote. Scott asserts this without evidence on Filipino practice.
- If opacity protects against both plunder and welfare (p. 65), how should the two be weighed? The chapter states the trade-off and does not resolve it.
- Is "internal colonization" (p. 69) an analysis or a rhetorical framing? It carries a judgment that the chapter's evidence on vaccination and citizenship cuts against.
