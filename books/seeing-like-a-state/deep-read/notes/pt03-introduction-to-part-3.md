# pt03: Introduction to Part 3: The Social Engineering of Rural Settlement and Production (pp. 143–151)

## Question this chapter answers

Why do states, from precolonial kingdoms to modern developmentalist regimes, try to fix rural people in concentrated, planned settlements, and what do such schemes achieve even when they fail as schemes of production? Scott answers with a short, schematic Southeast Asian history that introduces "state spaces" and "nonstate spaces" and frames the two long cases that follow, Soviet collectivization (ch06) and Tanzanian ujamaa villages (ch07) (p. 145).

The unit runs: legibility as the condition of intervention (pp. 143-144); the state as younger than society, and domestication as its project (pp. 144-145); precolonial Southeast Asia, population control and flight (pp. 145-146); state and nonstate spaces, Leach's Burma, the Meratus (pp. 146-148); counterinsurgency settlements (p. 148); the plantation paradox in colonial Malaya and FELDA schemes after independence (pp. 148-150); demobilization of communities as the lasting effect (pp. 150-151).

Only the Part 3 portion of p. 143 (after the end of ch05) and of p. 151 (before ch06 begins) is covered. Endnotes are on pp. 325-328.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legibility (as condition of manipulation) | p. 143 | Organizing the units of intervention (citizens, villages, trees, fields, houses, age groups) so they can be identified, observed, recorded, counted, aggregated and monitored. The depth of knowledge needed scales with the depth of the intervention (pp. 143-144). This sharpens fm01's definition (fm01, p. 7) into a proportionality rule. |
| Younger state | p. 144 | Most states are newer than the societies they administer, so they meet settlement, social and production patterns that grew up independent of state plans and are opaque to it, often on purpose (p. 144). An endnote adds that societies also carry strata of earlier state projects (endnote, p. 325). |
| Domestication / social gardening | p. 144 | Scott's preferred name for what the centre calls a civilizing process (Elias's phrase): making the countryside, its products and inhabitants identifiable and accessible to the centre (pp. 144-145). Echoes Bauman's gardening state (ch03, pp. 74-75). |
| Sedentarization, concentration, radical simplification | p. 145 | The three recurring elements of domestication, applied to both settlement and cultivation (p. 145). |
| State spaces | p. 146 | Dense, quasi-permanent settlements producing an easily appropriated surplus of grain (usually wet rice) and labour; rulers build and expand them by irrigation, war captives, forced settlement and codified religion (p. 146). In a market economy the concept is "suitably modified" to cover plantations (p. 148). |
| Nonstate spaces | p. 146 | Sparse, mobile populations practising shifting cultivation or mixed economies, hard to appropriate from (p. 146). Common features: impenetrable terrain, dispersed or migratory people, poor surplus prospects (p. 147). Refuges for runaways, rebels and pretenders (p. 147). Human agency helps make them, so the idea is not geographically determinist (endnote, p. 327). |
| Vote with one's feet | p. 145 | Flight as the subject's sanction in a land-rich, people-poor region; literal in Southeast Asia (p. 145); "voted with their oars" on rivers and coasts (endnote, p. 326). |
| Planned settlement schemes (FELDA) | p. 149 | Malaysia's large, grid-laid, bureaucratically run rubber and palm oil settlements of the 1960s and 1970s; economically costly, politically and administratively rewarding; "soft" civilian versions of counterinsurgency new villages (pp. 149-150). |
| Demobilized community | p. 151 | A state-designated settlement that must build its own cohesion from scratch, and so is, by definition, easier to control from above and outside (pp. 150-151). |

## Leading propositions

- Any substantial state intervention requires inventing visible units, and the greater the manipulation, the greater the legibility required (pp. 143-144).
- What Proudhon deplored as being ruled was, from another angle, the hard-won and tenuous achievement of modern statecraft (p. 144).
- Societies are usually older than their states and present a diversity, complexity and unrepeatability that is opaque to the state; old cities such as Bruges or a Middle Eastern medina defy mapping, and any map dates quickly (p. 144).
- A state with minimal demands needs little knowledge; an ambitious state must become both far more knowledgeable and far more intrusive (p. 144).
- Large-scale redesign of rural life is better seen as domestication than as civilizing, and its common elements are sedentarization, concentration and radical simplification (pp. 144-145).
- A great continuity of purpose joins precolonial, colonial and independent regimes in Southeast Asia; what changes is the modern state's growing capacity to realize it (p. 145).
- In precolonial Southeast Asia control of people, not land, was decisive; wars were more often about captives than territory, and rulers had to tax below the level that would provoke flight (p. 145).
- Appropriation follows the same locational logic as markets (von Thünen, Christaller, Skinner), favouring concentration because of transport costs (p. 145).
- Thai tattooing of commoners and Spanish-era Philippine runaways (remontados) show the exceptional measures needed to fix mobile subjects (pp. 145-146).
- State spaces are not just given ecologies; rulers create and extend them (p. 146).
- Leach's Burma: the kingdom is best read as horizontal slices of topography, the wet-rice valleys being the state and the hill strata nonstate spaces (pp. 146-147).
- Nonstate spaces serve the court as the image of barbarity against which civility is measured, and practically as refuges (p. 147).
- Modern development requires creating state spaces, and their expansion is ubiquitous and often traumatic for those absorbed, as with the Meratus of Kalimantan (p. 147).
- Rebellion carries the state/nonstate distinction to its logical end: Malayan new villages, Vietnamese strategic hamlets, and concentration camps, where control matters more than appropriation; defoliation with Agent Orange can be read as making forest legible (p. 148).
- Colonial preference for plantations over smallholders cannot be explained by efficiency, since smallholders out-compete estates for almost every crop except perhaps sugarcane (p. 148).
- The paradox dissolves once plantations are seen as efficient units of taxation, labour discipline, surveillance and political control (pp. 148-149).
- Metropolitan interests were only one factor; if they were the main one, the policy would have lapsed at independence, and it did not (p. 149).
- FELDA schemes made little economic sense against cheap voluntary pioneer settlement but paid off in legibility, political selection, electoral control, and control of crops and prices (pp. 149-150).
- The development rhetoric of such schemes was not insincere but was silent about appropriation, security and political hegemony (p. 150).
- Planned settlements rarely work as intended, but their effect lies as much in what they replace as in how far they meet their own goals (p. 150).
- Concentration has almost always disrupted or destroyed prior communities with nonstate sources of cohesion; the new community is a demobilized one (pp. 150-151).

## Arguments

**A. Legibility scales with intervention (pp. 143-144)**

- **Premises**: (p. 143) Interventions such as vaccination, taxation, conscription and schooling act on units that must be identified and counted. (p. 143) The knowledge required is roughly commensurate with the depth of intervention. (p. 144) The woodsman taking occasional firewood needs no detailed knowledge of the forest; a state taking a few carts of grain needs no detailed map.
- **Unstated premises**: that intervention is the state's motive and legibility its means, rather than legibility sometimes generating the ambition to intervene. That the proportionality holds across very different kinds of intervention; vaccination and conscription are listed alongside each other although their knowledge demands differ. The forestry analogy (ch01) returns here, and with it the assumption that a population relates to the state as a forest to a forester.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 144) "the greater the manipulation envisaged, the greater the legibility required to effect it."

**B. Settlement policy follows appropriation, not production (pp. 145-148)**

- **Premises**: (p. 145) Population was scarce and flight easy, so rulers competed for people. (p. 145) Concentration lowers the cost of collecting grain, labour and soldiers. (p. 146) Rulers actively build state spaces. (p. 147) Leach's reading of Burma shows the kingdom as the dense wet-rice zone. (pp. 147-148) Indonesian officials try to settle the Meratus along roads so that the government can see and instruct them.
- **Unstated premises**: that a single locational logic explains precolonial, colonial and modern policy; Scott presents the history as "schematic" (p. 145) and the continuity as given rather than tested. That appropriation is the dominant aim; the endnotes add long-distance trade in gems, metals and resins (endnote, p. 326) and choke points that break the inland model (endnote, p. 327), which qualify it. That the Meratus' own reading of the scheme (to be seen by government) reveals its real purpose, while the officials' language of religion and order is taken as cover; both come from one ethnography (Tsing).
- **Conclusion**: modern development is the expansion of state spaces (p. 147), and Indonesian plans for the Meratus are "a synoptic project of legibility and concentration." (p. 148)

**C. The plantation paradox (pp. 148-150)**

- **Premises**: (p. 148) Smallholders, with low fixed costs and flexible family labour, consistently undersold estates. (p. 149) Malayan officials kept favouring estates after learning this, most blatantly in the Stevenson scheme. (p. 149) Estates specialize in one crop, so output and profit are easy to assess and tax. (p. 149) Estates concentrate residence and labour under central control. (p. 149) The preference persisted after independence. (pp. 149-150) FELDA settlements were grid-laid, numbered, registered, politically selected and electorally useful.
- **Unstated premises**: that persistence after independence rules out metropolitan interest as the main cause; a new national elite could have its own estate or patronage interests, which Scott's FELDA material in fact shows (selection by ruling-party recommendation, p. 150) without counting it as a separate cause. That officials' motive can be read from effects; no document from colonial or Malaysian officials stating a fiscal or security rationale is cited. Endnote 18 adds a labour-supply reason (Malays would not work estates, so Indian and Chinese labour was imported) (endnote, p. 327), which is a different explanation from legibility. The smallholder efficiency claim carries no citation beyond the sugarcane exception (endnote, p. 327).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 150) The FELDA dividend was "less an economic return than a dividend in expanding state spaces."

**D. Planned settlement demobilizes (pp. 150-151)**

- **Premises**: (p. 150) Planned settlements rarely work as planned. (p. 150) They nonetheless replace prior communities whose cohesion came from nonstate sources. (p. 150) Such communities had their own histories, ties, mythology and capacity for joint action. (pp. 150-151) A new settlement must build cohesion from scratch.
- **Unstated premises**: that cohesion and capacity for joint action are mostly lost in resettlement and slowly rebuilt, rather than carried along by kin and village networks that move together. That demobilization is an effect the state values even when not intended; Scott says the effect lies in replacement without saying whether it was aimed at. The endnote's evidence is Huntington's Vietnam paper, read as "morally obtuse but sociologically correct" (endnote, p. 328), and an inference about early industrial protest coming more from declining rural communities than from the new proletariat (endnote, p. 328), stated as a belief, without sources.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 150-151) The new community is "a community demobilized, and hence a community more amenable to control from above and outside."

## Evidence offered

- **Theory of location**: von Thünen, Christaller and Skinner (p. 145; endnote, p. 325), used to give the appropriation logic a general economic form.
- **Southeast Asian history**: population density of about five per square kilometre in 1700 (p. 145), uncited here; Thai tattooing and bounty hunters (pp. 145-146); Philippine remontados (p. 146); The Glass Palace Chronicle (Queen Saw's admonition) and Adas on peasant avoidance (endnote, p. 326). Endnote 7 widens flight to post-plague Europe, US slavery and Russian runaway serfs (endnote, p. 326).
- **Leach on Burma** (pp. 146-147), reported as an implicit version of Scott's own logic, without a bibliographic note in this unit.
- **Tsing on the Meratus** (pp. 147-148; endnotes, p. 327): the main ethnographic case, including Indonesian official vocabulary and Meratus interpretations. Endnote 13 adds a logging and revenue motive (endnote, p. 327).
- **Counterinsurgency**: Malayan new villages, Vietnamese strategic hamlets, concentration camps, Agent Orange (p. 148); Scott's own recollection of Philippine settlements in Tarlac and Pangasinan with occupants' names posted on each house (endnote, p. 327).
- **Colonial Malaya**: rubber estates versus smallholders and the Stevenson scheme (pp. 148-149), asserted without citations in this unit; sugarcane as the exception explained by milling bottlenecks (endnote, p. 327); Java's Culture System and cash head taxes as other routes to a legible sphere (endnotes, pp. 327-328).
- **FELDA**: description of costs, selection, wage dependency and electoral use (pp. 149-150), partly from Scott's own fieldwork in Kedah in the late 1970s (p. 150). This is the one first-hand source in the unit; it is a villager perception (needing a ruling-party recommendation), not documentary evidence.
- **US South**: Hahn on fencing, trespass and vagrancy laws used to close the commons to freed slaves (endnote, p. 327), a non-Asian nonstate-space example.
- **Huntington**: a circa 1970 conference paper as evidence for the demobilization logic (endnote, p. 328).
- **Perspective of the sources**: Scott relies on Western ethnographers (Leach, Tsing) and historians, and on his own fieldwork; state voices appear mainly through labels reported by Tsing (p. 148) and through a hostile reading of Huntington. No planner's own rationale for FELDA or for plantation policy is quoted. The unit is explicitly "schematic" (p. 145), and its economic claims about smallholders and FELDA costs are asserted rather than documented here.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Legibility becomes a proportionality rule.** fm01 called legibility a central problem of statecraft (fm01, p. 7). Here it is the condition of all intervention and scales with its depth (pp. 143-144). The benign examples (vaccination, literacy, sanitation) sit beside conscription and catching criminals, which keeps the point neutral; the rest of the unit then uses only coercive or extractive cases.
- **Back from high modernism to plain statecraft.** ch03-ch05 developed high modernism as an ideology. This unit barely uses the term. Its causal story is older and simpler: appropriation and control, shared by precolonial kings, colonial officials and independent governments (p. 145). This makes the Part 3 cases potentially overdetermined: ch06 and ch07 will need to show what high-modernist ideology adds beyond the appropriation logic set out here.
- **Two explanations of failure.** The unit promises to explain both the political logic of collectivization and ujamaa and "the reasons behind their manifold failures as schemes of production" (p. 145). The plantation argument, though, shows schemes that succeed politically while failing economically, which suggests "failure" is partly a matter of which goal one measures. The closing claim that effects lie in what schemes replace (p. 150) shifts the test away from stated goals.
- **Simplification and nonstate sources.** The demobilization argument (pp. 150-151) links to ch05's point that a vanguard produces a kind of working class (ch05, p. 143): here the state produces a kind of community, weaker in joint action. It also anticipates the endnote's reversal of the Marxist expectation that the new proletariat, not the old village, is the source of protest (endnote, p. 328), drawing on Marx's "potatoes in a sack".
- **Opacity "often purposely so" (p. 144).** This is the first strong statement in the book that illegibility can be a strategy of the governed, and the nonstate-space material develops it. It connects to Scott's earlier work on resistance (outside knowledge: Weapons of the Weak, 1985) and anticipates his later book on Southeast Asian hill peoples (outside knowledge: The Art of Not Being Governed, 2009), which expands this exact frame.
- **Normative hedge.** Scott says the superseded communities may have been "objectionable" on normative grounds (p. 150) and calls the development rhetoric not intentionally insincere (p. 150). These hedges mark the argument as about effects, not bad faith, but the unit gives no case where concentration served the settled population.
- **Forest and city analogies continue.** The woodsman (p. 144) and "Like the scientific forest or the grid city" (p. 150) tie Part 3 to ch01 and ch02-ch04, and Bruges and the medina are cited back to chapter 2 (p. 144).
- **Details that look wrong or garbled in the text.** (a) "³1 prefer" (p. 144): a note marker fused with "I prefer". (b) A comma splice joins "autonomous frontier settlement" to "FELDA schemes were" (p. 150), apparently a lost full stop. (c) Plantations are called "publicly-owned businesses" (p. 149); in context this probably means publicly listed companies, since the estates were mostly private. (d) The Proudhon title is rendered as Q’est-ce que c’est la propriété? (endnote, p. 325); outside knowledge: the title is Qu'est-ce que la propriété? (e) "The Eighteenth Brumiare" (endnote, p. 328); outside knowledge: Brumaire. (f) The Proudhon passage on p. 144 contains an ellipsis inside Scott's own quotation, so it cannot be quoted in full in this note. None of these affects the argument.

## Open questions

- Is the appropriation logic of this introduction sufficient to explain collectivization and ujamaa, and if so, what work is left for high-modernist ideology in ch06 and ch07?
- Does the persistence of plantation preference after independence (p. 149) rule out interest-based explanations, or only metropolitan ones? The FELDA patronage evidence (p. 150) points to domestic political interest.
- How strong is the smallholder-efficiency claim (p. 148)? It is central to the plantation paradox but is asserted without sources here, apart from the sugarcane exception (endnote, p. 327).
- Is demobilization (pp. 150-151) intended by planners, a side effect they welcome, or simply a consequence? The Huntington endnote (p. 328) is the only evidence of anyone wanting it.
- If nonstate spaces are partly made by human agency (endnote, p. 327), how should one weigh the freedom of the hills (endnote, p. 327) against the poverty and insecurity of mobile life? The unit presents the hill view sympathetically but gives no evidence on welfare in either space.
