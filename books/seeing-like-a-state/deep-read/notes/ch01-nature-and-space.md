# ch01: Nature and Space (pp. 12–46)

## Question this chapter answers

How does a state with narrow fiscal and security interests come to know, and then to reshape, forests, measures and landholding, and what is lost when it does?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tunnel vision / narrowing of vision | p. 13 | Bringing a few aspects of a complex reality into sharp focus so they can be measured and calculated. The source of both the power and the blindness of state knowledge. |
| Synoptic view | p. 13 | An aggregate view of a selected reality, obtained by combining standardised observations. Achieved in forestry only by restricting attention to commercial wood (p. 16). |
| Fiscal forestry | p. 14 | The state's view of the forest as revenue: the actual tree replaced by an abstract tree standing for a volume of timber or firewood. |
| Scientific forestry | p. 13, p. 15 | The forest management developed in Prussia and Saxony about 1765 to 1800, a branch of cameral science; used by Scott as a parable for knowledge in institutions with sharply defined interests. |
| Normalbaum | p. 16 | The standardised tree of the yield tables. An abstraction that planting practice then turned into reality (p. 17). |
| Bracketing | p. 19 | Assuming constant every variable except those bearing on the yield and cost of the chosen species. What lies "outside the brackets" returns later. |
| Legible / illegible | p. 13, p. 23 | Readable by an outsider with standard categories. Local measures and tenure are illegible to the state "in their raw form" (p. 23) but perfectly legible to those who live in them (p. 33). |
| State fictions / shorthand | p. 23 | Partly fictional standard descriptions which, backed by records, courts and coercion, transform what they describe. |
| Customary measures | pp. 23-26 | Units that are human in scale, relational, tied to an activity and to local interests: days of ploughing, seed sown, a farm of one cow. |
| Politics of measurement | p. 26 | Measurement as a field of power: sticky feudal dues are raised by altering baskets, heaping and the strickle. |
| Metrical revolution | p. 29 | Kula's term for the triumph of the metric system, enabled by market growth, popular and Enlightenment sentiment, and Revolutionary and Napoleonic enforcement. |
| Unmarked citizen | p. 30 | The abstraction of uniform, homogeneous citizenship on which uniform measures depend. |
| Freehold tenure | p. 34 | The "heroic simplification" of the liberal state: land owned by a legal individual holding uniform, transferable title enforced by the state. |
| Cadastral map | p. 34 | The survey of all holdings tied to a register of taxpayers. Thin by design: boundaries only, the inside left blank (p. 39). |
| Facts on paper / facts on the ground | p. 44 | The official record against actual practice; a shadow tenure system always persists beside the register. |

## Leading propositions

- Scientific forestry is offered as a metaphor for how state bureaucracies and large commercial firms know and manipulate their objects (p. 13).
- The crown's interest in forests resolved into one number, annual revenue yield; flora, fauna and nearly all human uses of the forest fell outside the frame (pp. 13-14).
- Utilitarian vocabulary sorts nature by use: crops and weeds, timber and trash trees, game and vermin (p. 15).
- What is distinctive in the state's logic is its narrowness, its elaboration, and the degree to which the state can impose it on the reality observed (p. 15).
- The decisive step was not measuring the forest but creating a forest that was easier to measure: cleared underbrush, fewer species, even-aged trees in straight rows (pp. 16-17).
- Commercial logic and bureaucratic logic coincided in the new forest (p. 17).
- Order became an aesthetic: neatness was taken as the sign of good management (pp. 17-18).
- The scheme was a resounding success for the first rotation, about eighty years, and became the world standard (pp. 18-19).
- The second rotation showed a fall of one or two site classes, a production loss of 20 to 30 percent; the first had been living off the soil capital of the old forest (pp. 19-20).
- Monocultures are as a rule more fragile than polycultures; diversity acts like an insurance policy (p. 21).
- No administrative system can represent a social community except by schematic abstraction, and state agents have no reason to want more (pp. 21-22).
- The premodern state lacked the information and grid to tax in proportion to capacity to pay; it was "all thumbs and no fingers" (p. 23).
- Unlike trees, taxable subjects are political actors who resist (p. 23).
- There is no single correct answer to a question of measurement apart from the local concern that prompts it; a vague local measure can carry more information than an exact average (p. 25).
- Every act of measurement was marked by power relations (p. 26).
- Illegible measures compromised food supply, the Achilles heel of the early modern state (p. 28).
- Uniform measures depended on uniform citizenship, and that was an emancipatory simplification (pp. 30-31).
- The metric system was slow to enter daily practice (p. 31).
- Customary tenure is a living, negotiated tissue of practices; codifying it would freeze it, and each village would need its own code (p. 33).
- Customary tenure should not be romanticised; it is riven with inequalities of gender, status and lineage (p. 33).
- The modern state presupposes a simplified, uniform property regime legible from the centre (p. 34).
- The state's case against communal tenure rested on the correct observation that it was fiscally illegible; the claim that it was less productive is not well supported (p. 37).
- The Stolypin reform produced tidy farms mainly on new land; elsewhere there were paper consolidations, and wartime procurement showed that the countryside was still illegible (p. 39).
- State simplifications are more static and schematic than the phenomena they typify (p. 41).
- Fiscal formulas change the facts they record; the door-and-window tax changed how houses were built (p. 42).
- Imposed freehold shifted power to those who could read the new system, most sharply in colonies (p. 43).
- Cadastral mapping came earlier where a strong state faced a weak civil society, and often earlier in colonies than in the metropole (p. 44).
- Society can modify, subvert and block the categories imposed on it (pp. 43-44).

## Arguments

**A. The forestry parable (pp. 13-21)**

- **Premises**: (p. 14) A fiscal interest reduces the forest to saleable wood. (p. 16) Tables built on that reduction give a synoptic view. (pp. 16-17) State power then reshapes the forest to match the tables. (p. 19) Rigour was bought by bracketing every variable but yield and cost. (pp. 19-20) The bracketed processes (soil building, nutrient cycles, symbiosis) were what sustained yield.
- **Unstated premises**: that the German decline is typical of simplification in general and not a matter of spruce on unsuitable sites; that a forester's hundred-year feedback loop resembles the feedback available to a tax office or a planner. The ecological mechanism is said to be "not entirely understood" (p. 19), so the causal account rests on a few authorities.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 20) Dismembering a complex, poorly understood set of relations to isolate one element of instrumental value succeeds in the short run and fails later, even on its own terms.
- **Endnote qualification**: German forestry was not one doctrine. An endnote records a losing anti-utilitarian school (Karl Geyer, the Mischwald and natural regeneration) and a vetoed plan by Brandes for community forests in India, and concludes that state officials "tended to select out of the mixed heritage of German forestry those elements most favorable to legibility, management, and revenue" (p. 286) in an endnote. The causal agent is thus state selection among forestry ideas, not forestry science as such.

**B. From forest to society (pp. 21-23)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 21-22) Social practice is as indigestible to administration as the natural forest. (p. 22) Officials abstract only what serves taxation, control and conscription. (p. 23) Backed by power, the shorthand transforms the practices it describes.
- **Unstated premise**: that the forestry analogy holds although people, unlike trees, answer back; Scott flags this himself (p. 23), which means resistance is part of the model from the start.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 23) The pattern of local practice against state routine found in measures and cadastres will recur throughout the book.

**C. Why standard measures won (pp. 29-31)**

- **Premises**: (p. 29) Long chains of anonymous trade need common units. (pp. 29-30) Feudal manipulation of measures made the Third Estate want one measure. (p. 30) Revolutionary citizenship removed the legal basis for unequal measures. (p. 29) Napoleonic state building enforced the result.
- **Unstated premise**: none hidden; this is the chapter's clearest case of a state simplification that the population wanted. It shows legibility serving the weak against local power-holders.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 30-31) Homogeneous measures were part of an emancipatory simplification.

**D. Why freehold and the cadastre (pp. 31-40)**

- **Premises**: (p. 33) A faithful code of custom is impossible to write, would freeze practice, and would differ village by village. (p. 35) Lalouette's attempt to codify practice failed because selection was political and because it threatened the centralisers. (pp. 36-37) Communal tenure cannot be mapped to a taxpayer. (p. 34) Freehold supplies one owner per parcel.
- **Unstated premises**: that fiscal convenience, more than a market in land or pressure from owners, drove the change. The chapter's own evidence points both ways: maps came into use "especially when a brisk market in land developed" (p. 40).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 37) The state imposed a property system in line with its grid instead of bringing the map into line with reality.

**E. The limits of the map (pp. 40-44)**

- **Premises**: (p. 41) A cadastral map is a still photograph of a moving current. (p. 41) Averages hide the variation farmers actually live with. (p. 42) Everything outside the fiscal use of a field is ignored. (p. 44) Paper owners may not be effective owners.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 44) Local practice must never be assumed to conform to state theory.

## Evidence offered

- Forestry: Lowood on the calculating forester and Beckmann's nail-box inventory (p. 16); Plochmann on second-rotation decline and the fragility of pure stands (p. 19, p. 21); "forest hygiene" measures such as nest boxes and implanted ant colonies (p. 20). The German secondary literature carries the whole case; Scott did no primary work here.
- Measures: Kula's synthesis is the main source (p. 24, pp. 26-28). Paris pinte against provincial pintes, seventeen aunes (p. 24); the bushel enlarged by one-third between 1674 and 1716 (p. 27); heaped bushels worth 25 percent more rent (p. 27); cahiers of the Third Estate (p. 30). Malaysian and Ethiopian examples come from Scott's own experience (p. 24).
- Tenure: a hypothetical Southeast Asian village which Scott calls an amalgam, "not unrealistic" (p. 32). It is illustration, not evidence. Real cases: the failed French code rural (p. 35), Danish ejerlav and Norwegian gard (p. 36), Novoselok and Khotynitsa before and after Stolypin, from Yaney (pp. 37-39).
- Transformation: the door-and-window tax (p. 42); the permanent settlement in India and Vietnamese intermediaries in the Mekong Delta (p. 43); Petty's survey of Ireland (p. 44); Jefferson's grid and the Torrens system (pp. 44-45).
- Endnotes for this chapter (pp. 284-295) were read.
- Sources named in the endnotes: "The following account is largely drawn from Lowood’s fine analysis." (p. 284) in an endnote. Taxation comes from Collins, measures from Kula and Alder, cadastres from Kain and Baigent, and the Russian tenure case from Yaney (endnote, pp. 288-293). Scott calls his own forestry education "admittedly limited" (p. 284) in an endnote and credits Ramachandra Guha.
- The mechanism of second-rotation decline, given in full only in an endnote, is specific: spruce planted on former hardwood soil, roots turning shallow as soil compacts, and acid raw humus building up (endnote, p. 287). Scott says he confirmed the pattern with David Smith and adds corroborating cases: Langston on the American inland West, Sivaramakrishnan on India, where three principles of scientific forestry "were overthrown by accumulating evidence" (p. 287) in an endnote, and Japan's postwar cedar monoculture (endnote, p. 288). He also concedes that naturally pure stands exist "in constrained ecological conditions" (p. 288) in an endnote.
- On tenure, an endnote retracts part of the body's picture of the Russian commune: "Here I am guilty of conveying a false sense of uniformity." (p. 292) in an endnote. Many villages did not redistribute land. Endnotes also back the body's doubt about the productivity of communal tenure with Hutterite farms and Yaney's finding that interstripped Mennonite land was as productive as consolidated land (endnote, p. 292).
- The Peasant Bank could lend only against identifiable plots, which leads Scott to note that "the modern credit system requires a legible property regime for its functioning" (p. 293) in an endnote.
- Citation and factual slips in the endnotes: the 1785 Austrian choice of gross income as a tax base is attributed to "Austria’s Franz Joseph" (p. 293) in an endnote; Franz Joseph was not born until 1830, and the ruler in 1785 was Joseph II (outside knowledge). Baigent is spelled "Biagent" in several notes (endnote, pp. 292-295). "Georgy Zhuvkov" in the list of hunting "monarchs" (p. 284) in an endnote is probably Todor Zhivkov (outside knowledge).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The forestry parable is the template for ch08 (agriculture) and is the only place in Part 1 where a simplification is shown failing on its own terms. Measures and freehold are shown as contested and partial, not as failures.
- The metric system and equal citizenship (pp. 30-31) are Scott's own example of a simplification that emancipates. This supports the Introduction's claim that element one is neutral, and it sits awkwardly with the tone of loss elsewhere in the chapter.
- The cadastral rule of thumb, strong state and weak civil society (p. 44), anticipates elements three and four of the Introduction.
- Commercial and bureaucratic logics are said to be synonymous in the forest (p. 17) and the land market is a co-driver of mapping (p. 40). This prepares the claim that capitalism is also a simplifier (fm01, ch10).
- "Miniaturization" appears in passing as a property of the estate map (p. 40); the term gets its main use in ch07.
- The body says state and commercial forestry logics were "virtually identical" (p. 16). An endnote nearly adds that the state's view "might be longer and broader than that of private firms" (p. 285) in an endnote, and then withdraws it because war and fiscal crisis make states as shortsighted. A later endnote admits the fiscal argument "assumes that the crown wanted to maximize its proceeds in the long run" (p. 289) in an endnote. The long-run rationality of the state is an assumption, not a finding.
- Argument C leans on the reformers' belief that uniform measures were the precondition of a national market (p. 29). An endnote cites Alder against it: the lack of a standardising central authority "does not seem to have impeded the growth of national markets in Britain, Germany, or the United States" (p. 290) in an endnote. So the French metric victory owes more to revolutionary state power than to market necessity.
- The emancipatory reading of equal citizenship (pp. 30-31) is qualified in an endnote: it "gave the state, for the first time, direct access to its subjects" (p. 290) in an endnote, including universal conscription. This links to Tilly's direct rule in ch02.
- Illegibility also served the state. Tax evasion gave the fiscal regime a flexibility that "helped states to avoid even more rebellion" (p. 288) in an endnote. This complicates the picture of illegibility as purely a cost to the centre.
- On the cadastre's drivers, endnotes add weight to the market side: full registers, freehold and a land market give legibility "as advantageous to the land speculator as it is to the tax collector" (p. 294) in an endnote, and the Mexican ejido case shows the state making land legible to create a national land market (endnote, p. 292).
- Scope: Scott says in an endnote that he left out the simplification of time because it would have made the chapter too long and Thompson has treated it (endnote, p. 294).

## Open questions

- Was the second-rotation decline general across German forestry or concentrated on particular soils? The body cites one main authority, Plochmann; the endnotes add a personal confirmation and parallel cases (endnote, pp. 287-288) but no German data beyond Plochmann's, and his mechanism is specific to spruce on former hardwood soil.
- How much of the cadastre's spread is explained by the state's needs and how much by owners and markets? An endnote adds that aristocratic holders of large fiefs also commissioned surveys to find taxable land (endnote, p. 292).
- The chapter gives no measure of what the state gained in revenue from legibility, so the benefits side of the ledger is asserted, not shown.
