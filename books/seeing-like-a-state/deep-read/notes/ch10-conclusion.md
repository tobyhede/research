# ch10: Conclusion (pp. 271–284)

## Question this chapter answers

What, in the end, went wrong with the great high-modernist schemes, and what follows for anyone who plans? Scott's answer: the schemes were tragedies of hubris and genuine but flawed benevolence (p. 271); their planners thought themselves far smarter and their subjects far more stupid than they were (p. 272). Concretely, the schemes ignored the radical contingency of the future (pp. 272-273), planned for abstract, standardized citizens (pp. 274-275), and imposed a single-purpose "first map" on sites that live by an unplanned "second map" (pp. 275-276). Formal orders are subsystems parasitic on informal practice, and this holds for markets as well as command economies (p. 279). The positive lessons are four planning rules of thumb (pp. 273-274) and a case for "mētis-friendly" institutions that are multifunctional, plastic and open to modification by their members, with democracy, common law and language as models (pp. 280-284).

The unit runs: Proudhon and Schumacher epigraphs, tragedy, Western origins of the faith, the one-sentence diagnosis (pp. 271-272); "It's Ignorance, Stupid!": contingency, the Yale nutrition circular, Marglin, Hayek and Kropotkin, Hirschman, four rules (pp. 272-274); "Planning for Abstract Citizens": the generic subject, standardization as first premise, the single metric (pp. 274-275); "Stripping Reality to Its Essentials": singularity, the two maps, institutional neurosis, the self-fulfilling subject, the anarchist claim (pp. 275-277); "The Failure of Schematics and the Role of Mētis": the 1991 agrarian congress, East German fixers, formal systems as subsystems, markets included (pp. 277-279); "A Case for Mētis-Friendly Institutions": four tempering considerations, diversity in nature and institutions, family farms, Emilia-Romagna, Jefferson's yeoman, the Vietnam and Iwo Jima memorials, housing, democracy, common law, language (pp. 279-284).

Only the ch10 portion of p. 271 (after ch09's closing sentences) is covered; p. 284 ends with the start of the endnote section (bm01), not covered. Endnotes for ch10 (notes 1-21) are on pp. 367-369. Unlike earlier chapters, the ch10 body text keeps its superscript note numbers, so matching is direct.

## Leading propositions

- The high-modernist episodes were tragedies of hubris and of genuine benevolence with a fatal flaw (p. 271).
- They did most damage in the socialist bloc and revolutionary Third World because authoritarian power, unimpeded by representative institutions, could nullify resistance (p. 271); but the ideas were Western (p. 271).
- Planners' power was least contested in war, revolution, economic collapse or new independence (p. 271).
- The plans resembled absolutist schemes of legibility, but the scale and the instruments of statecraft were new (pp. 271-272).
- One-sentence diagnosis: planners overrated themselves and underrated their subjects (p. 272).
- Planners routinely ignore the radical contingency of the future (p. 272); social analysis tends to make history look necessary (p. 272); faith in historical laws dissolved awareness of contingency (p. 272).
- Even with correct laws, schemes this comprehensive would have indeterminate outcomes, with incalculable second- and third-order effects and exogenous shocks (p. 273), and their scale made missteps irreversible (p. 273).
- Hayek and Kropotkin agree on this point and no other (p. 273); Hirschman made a comparable case (p. 273).
- Four rules of thumb for development planning: small steps, reversibility, planning on surprises, planning on human inventiveness (pp. 273-274).
- Standardizing the subjects of development was implicit even in egalitarian goals (p. 274); planners from Le Corbusier to Nyerere planned for generic subjects (p. 274).
- Abstraction is the first premise of large-scale planning, because standardized units yield definitive quantitative answers (p. 274); the same holds for nature (p. 274). The single metric is invaluable until it becomes hegemonic (pp. 274-275).
- Despite egalitarian impulses, high-modernist schemes repose little confidence in the skills of ordinary people (p. 275).
- High modernism's clarity comes from assuming only one thing is going on at each site (p. 275); every site is a host of interconnected activities (p. 275).
- The first map alone is misrepresentative and nonsustainable: monocropped forests, Taylorist factories, Brasilia, stripped-down public housing (p. 276).
- Thin maps backed by authoritarian power partly succeed in reshaping society (p. 276); the result is an impoverished, unsustainable social system (p. 276).
- Resistance prevents such schemes from being fully realized (p. 276); Le Corbusier's worker barracks and the layouts of kolkhoz, ujamaa and Ethiopian villages were designed for central administration and control (p. 276).
- Single-purpose institutions approach asylums and prisons and breed institutional neurosis (pp. 276-277).
- High-modernist designs diminish the skills and initiative of their beneficiaries and thereby produce the subjects their plans assumed (p. 277).
- Authoritarian engineering damaged structures of mutuality and practice essential to mētis; the kolkhoz destroyed agricultural skills (p. 277). There is "something to" the anarchist claim that the state undermines capacities for self-governance (p. 277).
- Yet the kolkhozniki survived by initiative and wits, as a woman at a 1991 congress insisted (pp. 277-278).
- East German factories depended on an improvising fixer and an off-books barterer absent from the organization chart (p. 278); such cases are "the rule" (p. 278).
- All formal orders are subsystems dependent on, even parasitic on, a larger system (p. 279); the thinner the order, the more fragile (p. 279).
- This is not simply a case for the invisible hand: the market is also a formal system dependent on law, coercion, trust and community, and the economy is a subsystem of a finite ecosystem (p. 279).
- Apparent anomalies (slums, illegal trade, a floating labour force, landraces) are indispensable to formal order (p. 279).
- At best the imposed orders were fragile; at worst they did untold damage (p. 279).
- Four considerations temper this condemnation: the old orders were often unjust; the schemes carried emancipatory ideas; representative institutions forced accommodation; and resistance forced abandonment or restructuring (pp. 279-280).
- Scott grants the benefits of division of labour and hierarchy for some tasks but argues for mētis-shaped institutions (p. 280).
- Diverse natural systems are less productive in the short run but more stable and less dependent on external inputs (p. 280); simplification can be stable in modest, predictable environments (p. 280); external costs accumulate before activities become unprofitable (pp. 280-281).
- Fixed routines suit stable task environments, which are rare (p. 281); families, small communities, small farms and family firms have proved adaptable (p. 281).
- Diversity pays even in large organizations and markets: Magnitogorsk versus diversified cities, off-the-books provisioning, Emilia-Romagna family firms (p. 281).
- Measured by broader criteria, the test is what kind of person an institution fosters; Jefferson's yeoman is the model (pp. 281-282).
- Any social form can be tested by how far it enhances the skills, knowledge and responsibility of its members (p. 282).
- The Vietnam Memorial completes its meaning through visitors' participation; the Iwo Jima memorial delivers a fixed message (pp. 282-283).
- The test is insufficient alone: narrow, stable purposes may justify hierarchical routines, though at human cost (p. 283). It fits wherever quality depends on participation, as in housing and neighborhoods (p. 283).
- Democracy, common law and language are existing models of institutions continually modified by their members (pp. 283-284).

## Arguments

**A. The diagnosis: hubris, contingency and contempt (pp. 271-273)**

- **Premises**: (p. 271) The schemes were motivated by a genuine desire to improve the human condition. (p. 271) Their ideas were Western; authoritarian power let them proceed. (pp. 271-272) New instruments of statecraft magnified absolutist ambitions. (p. 272) Planners rarely start from incomplete knowledge. (pp. 272-273) Comprehensive, long-horizon plans have indeterminate outcomes even if their theories are right. (p. 273) Exogenous shocks and the scale of intervention made errors uncorrectable.
- **Unstated premises**: that the failures share one cause located in the planners' minds. The single-sentence diagnosis (p. 272) is epistemic and attitudinal; it leaves out the state power and weak civil society that fm01 and ch03 made necessary conditions, though p. 271 reinstates power as the reason the damage was worst under authoritarian rule. That contingency is the main failure mode: the argument from second-order effects (p. 273) would apply equally to any large irreversible choice, including leaving things alone, and Scott does not consider the cost of not acting. The Yale circular (p. 272) is a charming illustration, not evidence about planners. The Hayek-Kropotkin agreement (p. 273) is asserted without citations (no endnote for either; the Kropotkin quotation is unsourced).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 272) "the progenitors of such plans regarded themselves as far smarter and farseeing than they really were and, at the same time, regarded their subjects as far more stupid and incompetent than they really were."

**B. Four rules for planning under ignorance (pp. 273-274)**

- **Premises**: (p. 273) We cannot know the consequences of interventions in advance. (p. 273) Small creatures survive falls; irreversible acts have irreversible consequences. (p. 273) Ecosystems are poorly understood. (p. 274) Plans can design in flexibility. (p. 274) Later participants will develop insight to improve on the design.
- **Unstated premises**: that time and political latitude exist for incrementalism. Endnote 4 concedes the reversibility rule can be waived when catastrophe is imminent (endnote, p. 368), yet p. 271 says planners' power was greatest precisely in war, revolution, collapse and new independence, so the rules are weakest exactly where high modernism flourished. Scott does not address this. The rules are addressed to development planners (p. 273), not to firms or markets. They restate ch09's Lindblom and Hirschman incrementalism (ch09, pp. 258-259), so mētis here is a method available to planners, not knowledge held by subjects; only the fourth rule brings the subjects back. Endnote 5 extends reversibility to capital punishment (endnote, p. 368), a sign the principle is meant generally. The Haldane image comes third-hand, via Penrose reviewing Dyson (endnote, p. 368).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 273) "Given this postulate of ignorance, prefer wherever possible to take a small step, stand back, observe, and then plan the next small move."

**C. Abstract citizens and the single map (pp. 274-277)**

- **Premises**: (p. 274) Planners from Le Corbusier to Nyerere planned for generic subjects. (p. 274) Standardization raises the resolving power of planning. (p. 274) Economics achieves precision through one metric. (p. 275) Taylorism, collective farms, ujamaa and planned cities inscribe movements into designs. (p. 275) Every site hosts many activities. (pp. 275-276) The unplanned second map comes to supersede the first. (p. 276) Forests, factories, Brasilia and public housing fail on the first map alone. (pp. 276-277) Total institutions produce neurosis.
- **Unstated premises**: that the abstraction of planning is the cause of the dullness of planned environments, rather than budget, density or poverty. Pruitt-Igoe and Cabrini Green (p. 276) are named without discussion or citation, and they are mentioned nowhere else in the book; outside knowledge, their failures are usually also attributed to funding cuts, maintenance and segregation. The Levittown claim (p. 276) is unsourced. The comparison of single-purpose institutions to asylums and prisons (pp. 276-277) is analogical; Scott calls the effect "a mild form" (p. 277), and the evidence is Colin Ward's anarchist essay (endnote, p. 368). The single-metric passage concedes the tool is "invaluable" (p. 274) and that problems arise "only" when it becomes hegemonic (p. 275), which narrows the critique of economics to its institutional dominance; Schumacher's longer statement in endnote 8 ties private enterprise's strength to "ruthless simplification" (p. 368) in an endnote, a stronger charge than the body makes. The "unmarked citizens" of liberal theory (p. 274) are bracketed with planners, but the book ends by praising liberal democracy (pp. 283-284); Scott does not say how the abstract citizen of liberal rights differs from the abstract citizen of planning.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 277) "the logic of social engineering on this scale was to produce the sort of subjects that its plans had assumed at the outset."

**D. Damage and survival of mētis (pp. 277-278)**

- **Premises**: (p. 277) The kolkhoz destroyed many peasant agricultural skills. (p. 277) It abolished local tyrannies and also institutional autonomy. (pp. 277-278) Specialists in 1991 despaired of the kolkhozniki's skills; a woman from Novosibirsk retorted that they had survived by their wits. (p. 278) Outward compliance followed broken resistance.
- **Unstated premises**: Scott sets two claims side by side without weighing them: that high modernism deskills its subjects and produces dependent people (p. 277), and that the subjects keep their initiative (p. 278). The reconciliation seems to be that agricultural mētis was lost while survival mētis grew, but this is not stated. The evidence is Scott's personal notes from a 1991 congress (endnote, p. 368), a single remark. The anarchist claim (p. 277) is endorsed only as "something to", consistent with fm01's denial that the book is anarchist (fm01, p. 12).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 277) authoritarian social engineering "did, at the very least, damage many of the earlier structures of mutuality and practice that were essential to mētis."

**E. Formal systems, markets included, are parasitic subsystems (pp. 278-279)**

- **Premises**: (p. 278) Two East German factories relied on unofficial fixers and barter. (p. 278) Command economies limped on informal economies. (p. 279) The market depends on law, coercion and antecedent norms of trust and cooperation. (p. 279) The economy is a subsystem of the ecosystem (Daly). (p. 279) Cities rely on slums, welfare systems on unprotected labour, hybrids on landraces.
- **Unstated premises**: that dependence on a larger system is a defect specific to thin orders. Every system, thick or thin, depends on an environment; the claim with bite is the comparative one that thinner orders are more fragile (p. 279), which is asserted, not shown. The market caution is a single paragraph and argues only that markets, like plans, depend on what they do not create; it does not show markets destroy mētis or homogenize, which is the stronger claim fm01 made (fm01, p. 12). The East German evidence comes from Müller's book on "a Berlin Collective Enterprise" (endnote, p. 368); the title fits a collective rather than two state factories, so cannot verify that the cited pages describe the case as given. The examples of "mētis to the rescue" (p. 279) include a floating population with few protections and slum-dwellers providing services, which shows the formal order exploits informal labour; calling this mētis stretches the term from skill to any unrecognized activity, the same slide noted in ch09.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 279) "all socially engineered systems of formal order are in fact subsystems of a larger system on which they are ultimately dependent, not to say parasitic."

**F. The case for mētis-friendly institutions (pp. 279-284)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 279-280) Four considerations soften the condemnation. (p. 280) People domesticate confining systems; autonomy and diversity attract. (p. 280) Diverse ecosystems are more stable (Daly). (p. 281) Fixed routines suit only stable environments. (p. 281) Small farms outlast many corporate and state farms (Netting). (p. 281) Emilia-Romagna family firms thrive on civic traditions (Putnam). (pp. 281-282) Jefferson's yeoman shows enterprise trains citizenship. (pp. 282-283) The Vietnam Memorial versus Iwo Jima. (p. 283) Housing succeeds through user flexibility. (pp. 283-284) Democracy, common law and language adapt continually.
- **Unstated premises**: that adaptability and the cultivation of citizens' skills align with survival in competition. The family farm and Emilia-Romagna cases (p. 281) are success stories within markets, so markets here appear as selectors for mētis-friendly forms, not as homogenizers. That the attractiveness of self-employment and mixed-use neighbourhoods (p. 280) shows a general preference for autonomy rather than a class-specific taste. The family farm's flexible labour includes "the exploitation of its children" (p. 281), a parenthetical concession that the mētis-friendly unit may carry its own domination, echoing fm01's admission that practical knowledge is entangled with domination (fm01, p. 12). The memorial comparison is an aesthetic judgment measured by visits (p. 282); endnotes 19-20 record that Lin's ordering was controversial and that many veterans' organizations opposed the wall and wanted the figurative statue now nearby (endnotes, p. 369). That makes the participatory memorial a single designer's choice imposed over organized objection, which complicates its use as a model of form shaped by the people engaged in it. The ending's models (democracy, common law, language) are liberal and evolved institutions, which aligns Scott with Hayek's spontaneous order and Burke more than the text acknowledges.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 283) "An institution, social form, or enterprise that takes much of its shape from the evolving mētis of the people engaged in it will thereby enhance their range of experience and skills."

## Evidence offered

- **Recapitulation of earlier chapters**: scientific forestry, Taylorism, Brasilia, Jacobs, kolkhoz, ujamaa, Ethiopian villagization, landraces (pp. 271, 275-276, 279). The conclusion adds no new historical case of scale.
- **New illustrations**: the Yale nutrition circular (p. 272); the 1991 Moscow agrarian congress, from Scott's personal notes (pp. 277-278; endnote, p. 368); two East German factories via Müller (p. 278; endnote, p. 368); Magnitogorsk (p. 281); Emilia-Romagna via Putnam (p. 281; endnote, p. 369); the Vietnam and Iwo Jima memorials, Scott's own observation (pp. 282-283).
- **Authorities**: Stephen Marglin, forthcoming (p. 273; endnote, p. 367); Hirschman (p. 273; endnote, p. 367); Hayek and Kropotkin, uncited (p. 273); Haldane via Penrose via Dyson (p. 273; endnote, p. 368); Leopold via Worster (p. 273; endnote, p. 368); Schumacher (p. 271; endnote, p. 368); Colin Ward (endnotes, pp. 368-369); Herman Daly's unpublished Yale seminar paper (p. 279; p. 280; endnotes, p. 368); Netting on smallholders (endnote, p. 368); Mingione on informal economies (endnotes, pp. 368-369); Jefferson (p. 282); Jacobs (pp. 276-277, 280). Arun Agrawal is thanked for the point that simplification can be stable in predictable environments (endnote, p. 368).
- **Perspective of the sources**: ecological economists (Daly, Schumacher), anarchist writers (Ward, Kropotkin, Proudhon in the epigraph), development critics (Marglin, Hirschman), with Hayek invoked once on contingency. The conclusion's positive programme leans heavily on anarchist and small-is-beautiful traditions while ending on liberal-democratic institutions. Several pieces are unpublished or forthcoming (Marglin, Daly) or personal (the congress, the memorials).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Does the conclusion restate the book's causal model?** fm01 named four necessary elements: administrative ordering of nature and society, high-modernist ideology, an authoritarian state, and a prostrate civil society (fm01, pp. 9-10). ch03 reduced them to three, folding legibility into high modernism (ch03, p. 71), and ch09 closed with a two-part combination of universalist epistemic pretension and authoritarian social engineering (ch09, p. 269). The conclusion never enumerates elements. All four are present implicitly on p. 271: "schemes of legibility and standardization" (p. 271) and the instruments of statecraft (legibility), the Western faith in progress (ideology), authoritarian power "unimpeded by representative institutions" (the state), and the moments of war, revolution, collapse or independence when other coordination failed (fm01's conditions for a prostrate civil society, fm01, p. 10). The phrase "civil society" does not appear in the chapter. But the headline "single sentence" diagnosis (p. 272) is purely epistemic and attitudinal: arrogance about one's own knowledge and contempt for subjects' knowledge. Power and civil society drop out of the summary at the moment Scott condenses the argument, and the chapter's sections (contingency, abstraction, single maps, mētis) are all about knowledge. The structural model of fm01 survives only as the explanation of why damage was worst in some places (p. 271) and in the third and fourth tempering considerations, representative institutions and resistance (p. 280). The drift from four elements to an epistemic diagnosis is real.
- **Markets and capitalism: is fm01's promise kept?** fm01 promised that the conclusions are "as applicable to market-driven standardization" (p. 12) as to bureaucratic homogeneity, and called global capitalism "perhaps the most powerful force for homogenization" (p. 12), both in fm01. The conclusion delivers in three places: the single metric of economics, invaluable until hegemonic (pp. 274-275); one paragraph saying the market is itself a formal system dependent on trust, law and the ecosystem (p. 279); and the point that external costs accrue before activities become unprofitable (pp. 280-281). Against this, the positive case uses markets as evidence for mētis: family farms outlast corporate and state farms "in competitive economies" (p. 281), and Emilia-Romagna firms seem "ideally suited to postindustrial capitalism" (p. 281). Contract farming and the plantation (ch09, pp. 268-269) and industrial agriculture (ch08) are not recapitulated except through landraces (p. 279). The four planning rules address "development planning" (p. 273). The promise is kept only thinly: the conclusion argues that markets depend on what they cannot create, not that they homogenize or destroy mētis, and it leaves the impression that competition rewards mētis-friendly forms, closer to Hayek than fm01's disclaimer would suggest.
- **Sincere planners versus control and appropriation.** The chapter opens by insisting the planners were not cynical but animated by "a genuine desire to improve the human condition" (p. 271), and the one-sentence diagnosis is a failure of humility, not of motive (p. 272). ch09 had said the logic of destroying mētis is "one of control and appropriation" (p. 266), in ch09, and pt03 said development rhetoric was silent about appropriation and political hegemony (pt03, p. 150). The conclusion itself slips between the two: five pages after denying cynicism, Scott says the layouts of the kolkhoz, ujamaa village and Ethiopian resettlement "were designed, above all, to facilitate the central administration of production and the control of public life" (p. 276), and cultivated natural capital gains "ease of appropriation" (p. 280). The reconciliation could be that control was a sincere means to egalitarian ends, or that control belongs to states and firms while sincerity belongs to intellectuals, but the chapter states neither. The tragic framing therefore covers the high-modernist intelligentsia (Le Corbusier, Lenin's view, Nyerere) better than the fiscal and capitalist actors of ch01, ch08 and ch09.
- **Mētis as lost and as indestructible.** The chapter claims high-modernist designs deskill people and create dependent subjects (p. 277), and also that the kolkhozniki kept their initiative and that any plan will be "utterly remade by popular practice" (p. 280). ch09 had the same double position: destruction as a project "never fully successful" (ch09, p. 266). The conclusion does not say which mētis is destroyed and which persists.
- **Contingency rules and crisis conditions.** p. 271 says planners were least contested in war, revolution, collapse and new independence; endnote 4 waives reversibility when catastrophe looms (endnote, p. 368). The rules are thus inapplicable in the very conditions that produced high modernism. ch05's Lenin and ch07's Nyerere both justified haste by emergency.
- **The anarchist thread.** fm01 denied that the book is an anarchist case against the state (fm01, p. 12). The conclusion opens with Proudhon (p. 271), grants "something to" the classical anarchist claim (p. 277), and draws institutional neurosis and the playground parable from Colin Ward's Anarchy in Action (endnotes, pp. 368-369). It closes, however, on democracy and common law (pp. 283-284), so the position is anarchist in sensibility and liberal in institutions.
- **Liberal abstraction.** The abstract subject of planning is compared to the "unmarked citizens" of liberal theory (p. 274), yet the models of mētis-friendly institutions are liberal democracy and common law (pp. 283-284), and ch09 counted equal citizenship among the state simplifications worth having (ch09, p. 269). Scott does not distinguish the abstraction that grounds rights from the abstraction that grounds plans.
- **Links back.** The two maps (pp. 275-276) generalize ch04's Jacobs versus Le Corbusier and ch07's thin versus thick; "Planned Brasilia" underwritten by unplanned Brasilia restates ch04. The subsystem formula (p. 279) generalizes ch09's parasitism law (ch09, p. 243) and ch08's landraces. Natural capital (p. 280) returns to ch01's forest. The language ending (p. 284) is the third use of language as the model of mētis (ch09, pp. 251, 263; ch02 and ch07 on standard languages). Jefferson's yeoman (p. 282) recalls the freehold-tenure material of ch01 and ch02. The Hayek-Kropotkin agreement (p. 273) is the closest the book comes to naming its kinship with Hayek's dispersed-knowledge argument, noted as absent in ch09.
- **Details that look wrong or garbled in the text.** (a) Missing apostrophes: "the planners grasp" (p. 273), "factory hands contribution" (p. 275). (b) "stupifying" for stupefying (p. 277). (c) "Zamiatin" is a variant transliteration of Zamyatin (p. 272). (d) Endnote 1 cites Marglin as forthcoming and endnote 13 cites Daly's unpublished seminar paper (endnotes, pp. 367-368); cannot verify. (e) Endnote 12's source is about "a Berlin Collective Enterprise" (endnote, p. 368) while the body speaks of two East German factories (p. 278); possible mismatch, cannot verify. (f) The Kropotkin quotation and the Hayek attribution have no source (p. 273). (g) The congress is placed "Not long after the decisive political opening in 1989" (p. 277); the endnote dates it June 1991 (endnote, p. 368), consistent with "then still the Soviet Union". (h) The Schumacher quotation in endnote 8 contains ellipses and an unbalanced inner quotation mark in the source (endnote, p. 368), so it is not quoted here. (i) The body text of this chapter keeps superscript note numbers, which the earlier chapters lost; quotes here avoid them.

## Open questions

- Is the conclusion's epistemic diagnosis (p. 272) a refinement of fm01's four-element model or a retreat from it? Would the four rules have prevented any of the book's cases without the political conditions (representative institutions, resistance) of p. 280?
- How do incrementalist rules apply under the crises that, by Scott's own account, gave planners their power (p. 271; endnote, p. 368)?
- Can sincere benevolence (p. 271) and designs made "above all" for control (p. 276) be the same actors' motives, and if so, how?
- If competitive markets reward family farms and Emilia-Romagna firms (p. 281), in what sense is global capitalism the strongest homogenizing force (fm01, p. 12)? What would a market-side version of the four rules look like?
- What is the criterion that separates the abstract citizen of liberal theory (p. 274) from the standardized subject of planning, given that the book ends by endorsing liberal democracy (pp. 283-284)?
- Which forms of mētis does high modernism destroy, and which does it provoke (pp. 277-278)? Does survival improvisation count as the same knowledge that sustained agriculture?
- Is "mētis to the rescue" (p. 279) about knowledge at all, when the examples include unprotected labour and slums whose contribution is cheapness rather than skill?
- Does the Vietnam Memorial, imposed by a designer over organized veterans' objections (endnotes, p. 369), fit the test of a form shaped by the people engaged in it (p. 283)?
