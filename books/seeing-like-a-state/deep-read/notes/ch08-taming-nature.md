# ch08: Taming Nature: An Agriculture of Legibility and Simplicity (pp. 206–242)

## Question this chapter answers

Why has a model of modern scientific agriculture that apparently succeeded in the temperate, industrializing West so often foundered in the Third World, even though colonial modernizers, independent states and international agencies kept pressing it (p. 207)? Scott's answer is that the failures were "systemic" (p. 207), not the product of bad administration or predation. He names four elements: assumptions inherited from temperate agriculture, the bending of schemes to serve officials' power and status, a productionist shortsightedness that ignores long-run and third-party effects, and the inability of experimental method to handle certain kinds of complexity (p. 207). The chapter moves the book's argument from the state's simplifications of society (ch01-ch07) to the simplifications built into a body of science itself, and it ends by setting up ch09's account of practical knowledge (p. 242).

The unit runs: epigraph and introduction, the four systemic elements, and a disclaimer that this is no attack on agronomic science (pp. 206-208); early agriculture and landraces (pp. 208-209); twentieth-century agriculture, mechanization and the supermarket tomato (pp. 209-211); unintended consequences: genetic vulnerability, pesticides, the 1970 corn blight, and agriculture's "dark twin" (pp. 211-213); the catechism of high-modernist agriculture and the National Geographic farm of the future (pp. 213-214); modernist faith versus local practices: polyculture (pp. 214-221), shifting cultivation (pp. 221-223), fertilizer versus fertility (p. 223), unauthorized innovation (pp. 223-224); institutional affinities (pp. 224-226); the simplifying assumptions of agricultural science: isolation of variables, blind spots, weak peripheral vision, shortsightedness (pp. 226-231); the simplifying practice of scientific agriculture: yields, experimental plots versus fields, fictional versus real farmers (pp. 231-237); two agricultural logics, the Andean potato (pp. 237-240); conclusion (pp. 240-242).

Only the ch08 portion of p. 206 (after ch07's closing sentence on capital-city slums) and of p. 242 (before Part 4 and ch09 begin) is covered. Endnotes are on pp. 347-358 (ch08 notes 1-111).

## Leading propositions

- Bureaucratic abstractions are "too coarse, too static, and too stylized" to represent the world (p. 206); high-modernist agriculture uses abstractions of the same order and, failing to represent real farms, simplifies them instead (p. 206).
- The simplified field and the simplified forest share one logic; agriculture came first and served as forestry's model (p. 206). The preconditions were a commodity market and competitive pressure on states and entrepreneurs (p. 206).
- Narrowing attention to one dependent variable gives great analytical power to raise yields, but creates blind spots that eventually blindside production agronomy (pp. 206-207).
- Failures of the model in the Third World, especially Africa, were systemic and would have happened under the best administrative assumptions (p. 207).
- Four elements explain them: inherited temperate assumptions, schemes bent to serve officials' power, productionist neglect of long-run and external effects, and the method's inability to handle complexity (p. 207).
- The chapter is not an attack on agronomic science, whose knowledge even traditional cultivators use; the target is its imperial pretension to exclude other knowledge (p. 208).
- Cultivation always simplifies, and cultivars become dependent on human management (p. 208); but early farmers also fostered diversity through landraces, as insurance against drought, pests and disease (pp. 208-209).
- All modern crops derive from landraces; scientific breeding before about 1930 was selection among them (p. 209). Endnote: modern varieties depend on traditional agriculture, so Boyce's "complements" is too weak (endnote, p. 349).
- Twentieth-century agriculture produced unprecedented standardization and genetic narrowing (p. 209), driven by market competition, planting density, fertilizer response and supermarket uniformity (p. 209), and above all by mechanization (pp. 209-210).
- The crop was bred to suit the machine (p. 210); the supermarket tomato is the diagnostic case (p. 210).
- Monoculture and genetic uniformity invite epidemics, diversity breaks them (p. 211), and pesticide use is an integral consequence of this vulnerability (pp. 211-212).
- Pesticides breed resistance and create new crop diseases (p. 212).
- In 1970, 71 percent of corn acreage was in six hybrids, most with Texas cytoplasm, which the blight attacked (p. 212). An exotic Mexican landrace provided the fix (p. 212).
- Standardized agriculture therefore depends on a dark twin of informal practice (pp. 212-213).
- The American model was hegemonic from 1945 to 1975 (p. 213), carried by personnel moving from the TVA and USDA to the UN, FAO and USAID (p. 213).
- Large integrated projects were preferred partly because they could be planned as nearly pure technical exercises, like the Soviet collective farm designed in a Chicago hotel room (p. 213), and they suited local elites wanting show projects (p. 214).
- Most such projects failed despite lavish subsidies; the level of abstraction was fatal (p. 214).
- Colonial specialists dismissed West African polycropping on sight, as failing the visual test (p. 215), and independent successors continued the campaigns (p. 215).
- Polyculture fits tropical conditions: greater natural diversity, thin soils, rain-governed seasons, and the uneconomic labor cost of uniform environments at low population densities (p. 215).
- Polyculture is the norm on 80 percent of West Africa's farmland (p. 217) and works through crowding, weed suppression, complementary rooting and nutrition, and pest limitation (pp. 217-218), plus soil protection from its multistory structure (p. 218).
- Its greatest advantage for farmers is flexibility, risk spreading, food security and embeddedness in social relations (pp. 218-219).
- Critique of hegemonic ideas comes from the margins, from rogue figures such as Albert Howard and Rachel Carson (pp. 219-220).
- Scott does not claim polyculture is superior; he claims it merited empirical examination and was summarily dismissed (p. 220).
- Polyculture is more stable, sustainable, resilient and likely to produce Hicksian income (p. 220); the same diversity argument holds for forests and for Jacobs's neighborhoods (pp. 220-221).
- Shifting cultivation is rational, efficient and sustainable under its conditions (p. 221), but illegible to the state (p. 221), and offended modernizers by looking like unimproved nature (p. 222). Shallow cultivation and burning were later found beneficial (p. 222).
- African farmers used shifting cultivation discriminatingly alongside permanent bottomland farming (pp. 222-223).
- Fertilizer recommendations are gross simplifications that ignore microvariation (p. 223), and Liebig's NPK became imperial knowledge that neglects soil structure, humus and the fungus bridge (p. 223).
- Indigenous practice was dynamic, not static, readily adopting New World crops (pp. 223-224); every African farm was something of an experimental station (p. 224). Local knowledge also had limits (p. 224).
- Disdain for local competence was partly institutional privilege: recognizing farmers as competent equals would undermine officials' status (pp. 224-225).
- Centralization brought radical de-skilling (p. 225), a prospect even liberal reformers such as Liberty Hyde Bailey welcomed (p. 225).
- Hybrid corn created seed-company property because farmers cannot save true-breeding seed (p. 225); high-yielding varieties put cultivators in specialists' hands through an arms race of seeds and pesticides (pp. 225-226).
- Many failed schemes had been preceded by sound technical studies and trials (p. 226), so part of the problem lies in the necessary limits of scientific work aimed at diverse practitioners (p. 226).
- Isolating a few variables is necessary for unambiguous, universal results (p. 227), and monoculture and quantitative yields fit this paradigm (p. 227).
- Science dealing with many interacting variables loses what makes it modern science, and many narrow studies do not add up to one complex study (p. 228).
- Agronomists had scientific, not only aesthetic and institutional, grounds for opposing polyculture: it is too multivariate for clear causal proof (p. 228).
- Agricultural research has an elective affinity with techniques its methods handle, and agencies simplified environments to fit their knowledge (p. 229).
- DDT shows effects outside the experimental model taking revenge (pp. 229-230); awareness came from outside the paradigm, through the decline of songbirds (p. 230).
- Agroeconomic analysis likewise restricts its focus, but large firms can use political power to externalize costs (p. 230).
- Short trial horizons are institutional, not methodological, whereas isolation of variables and bracketing of interactions are inscribed in method (p. 231).
- Treating all grain as equal suits commodities and statistics but ignores cooking, ritual and other uses (pp. 231-233).
- An average year or standard soil is a statistical fiction (p. 233); farmers attend to microfeatures, as with Mende rice chosen for long awns against birds (pp. 234-235).
- Howard's critique of plots and quantification misses the most important abstraction: the Everyman cultivator (pp. 235-236).
- Real farmers pursue many goals tied to labor endowment, family cycle, subsistence, aesthetics, ritual, and household politics (pp. 236-237).
- Scientific abstractions, like freehold tenure, are misrepresentations that circle back to change reality through research focus and public policy (p. 237).
- Andean craft cultivation starts from the plot; scientific potato breeding starts from an ideal genotype and homogenizes the field to suit it (pp. 237-238). The new genotypes usually fail within three or four years (p. 240).
- Explicit rules work in cut-and-dried situations, so simplifying the environment expands rule-makers' power: the inner logic of de-skilling (p. 239).
- Farmers blend scientific and local strategies, but their infield experiments are illegible to researchers (p. 240).
- The failures came from uncritical, hence unscientific, faith (p. 240). Three reasons for scorn: professional interest, high modernism's contempt for the past, and the uncongenial form of practical knowledge (pp. 241-242).
- Farmers have discovered practices that work without knowing why; some may never be fully understood by science (p. 242).

## Arguments

**A. The failures were systemic, not administrative (pp. 206-208)**

- **Premises**: (p. 206) Bureaucratic abstractions cannot represent complexity, and high-modernist agriculture uses the same kind. (p. 207) The model has a poor track record in Africa, per an experienced agronomist (Richards). (p. 207) Four elements operate at a level deeper than bureaucratic pathology or predation.
- **Unstated premises**: that the counterfactual (failure "under the best assumptions about administrative efficiency and probity" (p. 207)) can be inferred from the cases, though no case is offered where administration was good and the scheme still failed for the stated reasons. That all four elements are "deeper" than administration: the second (schemes bent to serve officials' power, p. 207) is itself an institutional pathology, close to ch07's goal displacement. Endnote 4 grants that Bates's political economy explains much of agricultural policy and claims only to address sources "outside Bates’s political-economy field of vision" (p. 348) in an endnote, so the systemic thesis is a supplement, not a rival. The framing also grants that the model was "apparently" successful in the West (p. 207), which implies the method works where environments can be made uniform; the argument is therefore about fit, not about the science as such.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 207) "these were, in other words, systemic failures and would have occurred under the best assumptions about administrative efficiency and probity."

**B. Simplification breeds vulnerability, and science depends on what it displaces (pp. 208-213)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 208-209) Landraces are diverse insurance and the basis of all modern crops. (pp. 209-210) Market pressures and mechanization drove uniformity and phytoengineering. (p. 211) Uniform fields invite epidemics. (p. 212) Pesticides are the response and breed resistance. (p. 212) The 1970 blight hit uniform Texas-cytoplasm hybrids; a Mexican landrace supplied the remedy.
- **Unstated premises**: that the drivers of simplification here are high-modernist rather than commercial. Scott's own causes in this section are profit, mechanization driven by Western factor prices, and supermarket chains (pp. 209-210); the state barely appears. This is high modernism without a state, or market simplification of the kind a ch07 endnote attributed to Polanyi (ch07, endnote, p. 346). That the 1970 blight is representative of systemic fragility; it also shows the system recovering within the breeding paradigm, once landrace germ plasm was available. The "dark twin" analogy (pp. 212-213) stretches the ch07 term: in Brasilia the twin is concurrent informal practice, while here it is a genetic stock built up in the past and drawn on as a resource.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 213) standardized agriculture "depends for its existence on a “dark twin” of informal practices and experience on which it is, ultimately, parasitic."

**C. Polyculture and shifting cultivation were dismissed on sight (pp. 214-223)**

- **Premises**: (p. 215) Colonial specialists judged polyculture backward by its look. (p. 215) Tropical ecology explains polyculture's functions. (pp. 216-217) Howard Jones and Edgar Anderson found order behind visual disorder. (pp. 217-218) Research later confirmed yield, soil and pest advantages. (pp. 218-219) Farmers gain flexibility and food security. (pp. 221-222) Shifting cultivation is ecologically cautious and its burning and shallow tillage were later vindicated.
- **Unstated premises**: that the visual aesthetic, rather than ordinary error under limited evidence, explains the dismissal. The claim that specialists "knew, without further empirical investigation" (p. 215) is asserted, sourced to Richards, and the speculation that temperate nature shapes Western visual culture is hedged as "may play a role" (p. 215). The evidence base is heavily one author: Scott says "in this chapter I rely heavily on this brilliant book" (p. 348) in an endnote, Richards's Indigenous Agricultural Revolution. The rogue dissenters he cites (Howard Jones, Sampson and Crowther, Stamp, Howard) are those later vindicated, so the sample selects for prescience. Endnote 59 admits that Richards's test accepts "the premise that the only test is market efficiency, providing that it is sustainable" (p. 354) in an endnote, which is narrower than Scott's own broader case for farmers' many goals. Endnote 37 concedes the Guatemalan garden's diversity partly reflects subsistence needs (endnote, p. 351), and endnote 45 that many risk-spreading benefits could come from tiny single-crop parcels (endnote, p. 352). Scott disclaims proving polyculture superior (p. 220), so his conclusion is procedural: it deserved testing.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 220) its summary dismissal "is a tribute to the power both of imperialist ideology and of the visual aesthetic of agricultural high modernism."

**D. Institutional interest sustains the disdain (pp. 224-226)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 224-225) Recognizing farmers' competence would undermine officials' status. (p. 225) Collectivization, ujamaa, large irrigation and plantations centralize. (p. 225) Bailey's vision for the American countryside was a managerial elite of experts. (pp. 225-226) Hybrids and HYVs create dependence on seed companies and specialists.
- **Unstated premises**: that institutional interest explains belief, a functionalist step that endnote 68 supports with Ferguson's Lesotho study (endnote, pp. 354-355) but which is not shown in the agricultural cases. Endnote 69 undercuts the claim that large irrigation needs centralization: Lansing's Bali and Ostrom show large systems run "without centralized political authorities exercising coercive powers" (p. 355) in an endnote, which supports Scott but shows centralization is a choice, not a technical necessity. Endnote 67 concedes that the agricultural-revolution-from-below generalization "does not hold true" for large irrigation or HYV breeding (endnote, p. 354). The HYV yields are granted as "enormous" (p. 225) but not weighed against the dependence they create. Endnote 73 notes that wheat and rice are self-pollinating, so the seed-company monopoly logic applies less to them (endnote, p. 355), which narrows the hybrid argument mainly to maize.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 225) the specialists' claim to be neutral technicians "can hardly be accepted at face value."

**E. The method itself has blind spots (pp. 226-231)**

- **Premises**: (p. 226) Many failed schemes were preceded by careful trials. (p. 227) Isolating a few variables is necessary for universal results. (p. 227) Monoculture and quantitative yields fit this paradigm best. (p. 228) Moving to farm trials loses control, as West African researchers lamented. (p. 228) Polyculture's interactions resist causal proof. (pp. 228-229) The n-body problem shows how interactions multiply. (pp. 229-230) DDT's side effects lay outside the model. (p. 231) Trials are short.
- **Unstated premises**: that experimental science cannot study interactions. Outside knowledge, not in the book: factorial designs and analysis of variance were developed in agricultural field trials (Fisher at Rothamsted, 1920s-30s) precisely to estimate interaction effects, and long-term trials such as Rothamsted's Broadbalk run back to the 1840s. Scott's claim that multivariable comparison becomes "intractable to scientific method" (p. 228) is therefore too strong as a statement about method; it holds better as a statement about the practice and incentives of most applied research, which is the distinction Scott himself draws for time horizons (p. 231). Endnote 78 concedes there is "no reason, in principle" why the dependent variable cannot be taste or hardiness, only that research is more manageable otherwise (endnote, p. 356); endnote 82 shows the real obstacle in polyculture is choosing a common measure across crops (endnote, p. 356), which is a valuation problem, not a limit of experiment. Endnote 84 records that the USDA did make organic-farming comparisons by 1980 (endnote, p. 356), showing the gap was institutional. On DDT, endnote 86 grants that a cost-benefit verdict could still favour its use (endnote, p. 356); Scott's point is only that the costs lay outside the model. The Newton analogy (pp. 228-229) is illustrative, not evidence.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 230) "the power of the scientific paradigm was achieved partly by its exclusion of extra-experimental variables that have often circled back, as it were, to take their revenge."

**F. The fictional farmer and the grid that remakes reality (pp. 231-237)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 231-232) Yield statistics treat all grain as equal. (p. 232) Mende women judge rice by cooking, storage and labor (Richards). (pp. 232-233) Plants yield fodder, thatch, trellises and more. (p. 233) Average soils and years are fictions (Berry). (pp. 234-235) Farmers see microfeatures. (p. 236) Farmers pursue many goals. (p. 237) Policy incentives nudge reality toward the research schema.
- **Unstated premises**: that the abstraction is specific to agricultural science rather than to any economics of the farm as firm. Scott concedes that most of the complexity "could, at least in principle, be accommodated within a drastically modified, neoclassical notion of economic maximization" (p. 236); only aesthetics, ritual and social goals break it. The claim that we will not doubt the farmer's knowledge of his own interests (p. 237) is softened in endnote 99 to "whether he is entirely sure of them or not" (p. 357) in an endnote. The circle-back mechanism (p. 237) parallels ch01's freehold tenure and is the chapter's most important link to legibility: the grid changes the landscape through carrots, not sticks, which is a liberal-state variant of high modernism.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 237) "But over the long haul such a powerful grid can, and does, change the landscape."

**G. Two logics: craft versus genotype (pp. 237-240)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 237-238) Andean farmers adapt varieties to unique plots and test new crosses over years. (p. 238) Scientific breeding defines an ideal genotype first and then remakes fields to suit it. (pp. 238-239) Extension becomes remaking fields and farmers into standard ones. (p. 240) The new genotypes fail within three or four years. (p. 239) Simplified environments let rules explain more, which empowers rule-makers.
- **Unstated premises**: that one study (van der Ploeg) generalizes to scientific agriculture as a whole. Endnote 107 admits "The author does not specify the precise reasons for the decline" (p. 358) in an endnote, and Scott supplies possible reasons himself. Endnote 105 concedes well-watered bottomlands tolerate abstract treatment (endnote, p. 358), so the argument is strongest for fragile, heterogeneous environments like the Andes and weakest for the temperate plains where the model succeeded. The de-skilling inference (p. 239) treats the expansion of rule-makers' power as the purpose ("One of the major purposes", p. 239), which is a claim about intent offered as "what I take to be" (p. 239) rather than demonstrated.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 239) "This, at any rate, is what I take to be the inner logic of social, economic, and productive de-skilling."

**H. Unscientific faith, and why practical knowledge was scorned (pp. 240-242)**

- **Premises**: (p. 240) High-modernist agriculture carried the prestige of Western productivity and science. (p. 240) A scientific spirit would have examined farmers' practices. (pp. 240-241) Farmers are lifelong experimenters with a shared folk research library, and their lives depend on the results (p. 241). (pp. 241-242) Three reasons for scorn: professional interest, contempt for the past, uncongenial form.
- **Unstated premises**: that surviving practices are evidence of efficacy, a selectionist premise close to Hayek and Burke. Scott himself lists the limits: no controls and false inferences, no access to microprocesses, and uncertain regional aggregation (p. 241), which leaves open how to tell a working practice from a surviving one. The charge of "unscientific" faith (p. 240) uses science's own norms against the scientists, so it is a critique of scientism, consistent with the disclaimer on p. 208. Note the shift: on p. 228 agronomists had real scientific grounds to avoid polyculture; by p. 240 the dismissal is wholly faith. The two claims are compatible only if the scientific grounds explain why it was not studied and the faith explains why it was condemned.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 242) "The imperial pretense of scientific modernism admits knowledge only if it arrives through the aperture that the experimental method has constructed for its admission."

## Evidence offered

- **Main source**: Paul Richards, Indigenous Agricultural Revolution (1985), on West African polyculture, shifting cultivation, fertilizer, Mende rice, irrigation types and on-farm trials (pp. 207, 218, 222-223, 228, 232, 234-235; endnotes, pp. 348, 351-354, 356-358). - **Institutions and science**: Ferguson (endnote, pp. 354-355), Lansing and Ostrom (endnote, p. 355), Liberty Hyde Bailey via Marglin (p. 225; endnote, p. 355), Marglin's unpublished "Farmers, Seedsmen, and Scientists" (pp. 225, 227, 231; endnotes, pp. 355, 357), Feigenbaum via Gleick (p. 227; endnote, p. 355), Porter and Hacking on laboratory self-vindication (endnote, p. 355), Berry's The Unsettling of America (pp. 226, 233-234; endnotes, pp. 356-357), Raup's 1972 Senate testimony (p. 230; endnote, p. 357), Chayanov on the family cycle (endnotes, p. 357).
- **Andes**: van der Ploeg, "Potatoes and Knowledge" (pp. 237-240; endnotes, pp. 357-358), a single anthropological chapter; Fridolin via Yaney (p. 239; endnote, p. 358).

- **Perspective of the sources**: a coalition of critics of industrial agriculture (Berry, Carson, Howard, Jackson, Kloppenberg, Hightower, Marglin) and sympathetic agricultural anthropologists (Richards, van der Ploeg, Conklin, Chambers). Colonial specialists appear mainly as quoted by Richards. Mainstream agronomists and breeders appear through the NRC report and the phytoengineering proponents, quoted for their own admissions. Farmers' voices are mediated through ethnographers. Much of the scientific-ecology evidence comes from secondary or journalistic sources (New York Times reports on prairie plots and malathion).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Back to ch01.** The chapter explicitly returns to scientific forestry: same logic, with agriculture as the original model (p. 206); restoration ecology in German and Japanese forests (pp. 220-221); paper profits of monocropped forests (p. 231). Customary tenure versus freehold (ch01) is the template for the grid that changes reality (p. 237), and experimental plots are likened to cadastral maps (p. 233).
- **Back to ch07.** ch07's prediction that peasants were the empiricists and planners the true believers (ch07, p. 199) is generalized here into "unscientific" faith (p. 240). The "visual codification" (p. 215), "taxidermy" (p. 240) and "dark twin" (p. 213) all carry over from ch07. Tanzania is invoked for the state's war on shifting cultivation (p. 221) and Nyerere's democratic policies being "selected against" (p. 226). ch07's monocropping-as-inspection argument (ch07, p. 191) reappears as the elective affinity of method and monoculture (p. 229).
- **Back to ch04 and ch06.** Jacobs's visual versus working order (p. 215) and neighborhood diversity (p. 221) are applied to fields. The Chicago hotel-room plan (p. 213) recalls ch06. The polycropping of crops answers ch06's promise that the wheat and raspberry distinction would matter for the agricultural cases, though the raspberry terms are again not used.
- **Where is the state?** The title promises an agriculture of legibility, but the word "legible" appears only in the title; "illegible" appears three times (pp. 221, 240). Much of the chapter's simplification is driven by markets, mechanization, supermarkets, seed companies and research method (pp. 209-210, 225, 226-231), and the American case works through tax incentives and loans, "sticks" versus "carrots" (p. 237). This widens high modernism from a state ideology to a property of science and capital, which matters for cross-book work: Scott's critique here applies to market agriculture as much as to planning, extending his qualification of Hayek (ch07, endnote, p. 346).
- **Science versus scientism.** Scott protects agronomic science (p. 208; p. 228) and attacks its imperial pretension (p. 208; p. 242). But Argument E sometimes makes stronger claims about method itself (pp. 227-228) than the evidence supports, while p. 231 correctly separates method from institutional practice. The chapter's own endnotes (78, 82, 84) point toward the institutional reading.
- **Shift in the explanation of dismissal.** The polyculture section attributes dismissal to aesthetics and imperial ideology (p. 220); the institutional section to privilege (p. 224); the method section to genuine scientific difficulty (p. 228); the conclusion to faith, professional interest, contempt for the past, and uncongenial form (pp. 240-242). Scott offers these as cumulative, without weighing them.
- **Bridge to ch09.** The word "metis" appears in the ch08 portion only in endnotes (endnote, p. 355; endnote, p. 357). "Craft" (p. 237), the farmer as experimenter (pp. 224, 240-241), and practice preceding theory (p. 242) are the material ch09 will theorize. Endnote 70 places Scott's analysis of metis in "chapter 8" (p. 355) in an endnote, but it is in chapter 9, which suggests the notes were written before a chapter renumbering.
- **Agricultural revolutions from below.** p. 226 promises that ch09 will ask why successful agricultural changes came from cultivators. Endnote 67 already limits this: Howard's generalization fails for large irrigation and HYVs (endnote, p. 354). The Green Revolution's yields (p. 225) are the strongest counterexample to the chapter and are not weighed.

(e) "HYVS" (pp. 225-226) for HYVs. (f) Endnote 34: "a second cultigen in planted" (p. 351) in an endnote for "is planted". ## Open questions

- Does Argument E hold against factorial and long-term field experimentation, or is the real problem the incentives and time horizons of applied research, as p. 231 and endnotes 78, 82 and 84 suggest?
- How should the Green Revolution be scored against the chapter? Scott grants "enormous" yield effects (p. 225) and concedes HYVs were state-driven (endnote, p. 354), but does not weigh them against dependence and vulnerability.
- Can the aesthetic explanation of dismissal be separated from the method and institutional explanations with any evidence beyond Richards's accounts of colonial attitudes?
- How representative is van der Ploeg's Andean study, and why exactly did the new genotypes decline (endnote, p. 358)?
- If surviving farmer practices are evidence of efficacy (pp. 241-242), how does one distinguish practices that work from practices that merely persist, given the limits Scott lists (p. 241)?
- Is the "dark twin" here (landrace germ plasm, pp. 212-213) the same concept as ch07's informal practice, or a different dependence of the formal on the informal?
- Does the circle-back mechanism of research and policy (p. 237) amount to high modernism in liberal states, and how does it fit ch03's claim that the dangerous combination needs authoritarian power?
