# ch05: The Revolutionary Party: A Plan and a Diagnosis (pp. 115–143)

## Question this chapter answers

Does high modernism extend from the design of cities to the design of revolution and socialism, and what does the critique of it look like there? Scott answers by reading Lenin as a high modernist across three texts and setting him against Rosa Luxemburg and Aleksandra Kollontay, who play the part Jane Jacobs played against Le Corbusier in ch04 (p. 115).

The chapter has five parts: Lenin's What Is to Be Done? as a high-modernist plan (pp. 116-124); the 1917 revolutions as a test of that plan, plus the official history that rewrote them (pp. 125-128); State and Revolution and The Agrarian Question as proof that the outlook persisted (pp. 128-134); Luxemburg as critic (pp. 134-139); Kollontay and the Workers' Opposition, ending in the first use of metis (pp. 139-143).

Only the ch05 part of p. 115 (after the end of ch04) and the ch05 part of p. 143 (before the Part 3 introduction) are covered here.

## Leading propositions

- Lenin's plan for revolution parallels Le Corbusier's plan for the city: a complex task entrusted to a trained cadre with full power, and in both cases a critic (Jacobs; Luxemburg and Kollontay) doubted both its possibility and its desirability (p. 115).
- Judged by his major writings Lenin was a consistent high modernist; the Lenin of practice, a tactician with a feel for popular mood, is set aside (p. 116).
- What Is to Be Done? draws its idea of a "new man" who rules to build utopia from Chernyshevsky's novel of the same name, Lenin's favourite book (p. 116).
- The text's metaphors (classroom, barracks, brain and body, general staff, factory, building site) are not decoration; they set the limits of what the work can say (pp. 116-119).
- Lenin's case against spontaneity rests on two premises: only two ideologies exist, and the working class alone reaches only trade-union consciousness (p. 119).
- The great paradox of the book is that it turns a subject full of anger, violence and new political ends into a discourse on technical specialization and efficient means, so that politics disappears inside the movement (p. 120).
- The party's secrecy was meant to guard against contamination from below as much as against the police (p. 122).
- The masses' role in Lenin's scheme is hard to see beyond being "mustered up" (p. 123); his party relates to workers as an entrepreneur does to factory hands (p. 123).
- Lenin and Le Corbusier share a belief in a master science that authorizes a small elite, a conviction that there is one correct answer, disregard of their beneficiaries' practices, and machine and military metaphors; they were extreme but representative (p. 124).
- The Russian Revolution was not, to any significant degree, made by the Bolsheviks; they captured it once it had happened (p. 125).
- Workers' soviets and peasant land seizures were autonomous, as in 1905; the new factor in 1917 was the military collapse in World War I (pp. 125-126).
- 1917-1921 is best described as the Bolshevik state's reconquest of Russia, against the Whites and against the autonomous soviets and peasant communes (p. 126).
- What Is to Be Done? was a pipe dream as a description of revolution but accurate as a description of the post-revolutionary state (p. 126).
- The official account of a revolution as the leaders' planned work is the ultimate state simplification; it legitimates the victors, implies that the workers cannot act alone, and is reinforced by the way hindsight erases contingency (pp. 126-127).
- State and Revolution, despite its egalitarian tone, still rests on state coercion, the party as brain, and a static picture of administration as simple operations anyone literate can perform (pp. 128-129).
- Lenin embraced Taylorist and Fordist methods as the key to rational state planning, believing in a single objectively correct design for production (p. 129).
- High modernism is politically polymorphous (p. 130).
- The Agrarian Question shows the same outlook in agriculture: small family farming is doomed as hand looms were, and evidence of its efficiency is explained away as self-exploitation (pp. 130-132).
- Lenin's enthusiasm for electricity expressed his model of state power: central, precise, one-way, and calculable (pp. 132-133).
- Despite tactical retreats (land seizures, the shelving of collectivization), Lenin never abandoned the core of his high modernism; the retreat after Kronstadt changes the timetable, not the destination (pp. 133-134).
- Lenin and Luxemburg shared Marxist premises, hostility to gradualism, belief in a vanguard, and the lack of a sociology of the party (p. 134).
- The difference is one of degree: for Lenin the party monopolized knowledge of the whole; for Luxemburg it would constantly be surprised and taught by those it led (p. 135).
- For Luxemburg a mass strike cannot be ordered or forbidden; it is a complex event with many agents (p. 135).
- How a revolution is made shapes the proletariat that results, so process matters as much as outcome (pp. 135-136).
- Luxemburg's account better describes how the Bolsheviks actually came to power than What Is to Be Done? does (p. 137).
- Luxemburg's 1918 warnings about the Bolshevik dictatorship were prophetic and accurate (pp. 138-139).
- At bottom the dispute was about whether the road to socialism was already mapped (Lenin) or had to be discovered with the workers (Luxemburg) (p. 139).
- Kollontay brought a Luxemburgian critique inside the Bolshevik party; her experience as an advocate for working women parallels Jacobs's view as housewife and mother (pp. 139-141).
- Kollontay wanted experts to serve producers, on a model resembling an agricultural extension service (p. 141).
- Revolution and building new forms of production are uncharted voyages that can only be approached by trial and error, and the knowledge they need is mētis, not deduction from first principles (p. 142).
- For Luxemburg and Kollontay means and ends cannot be separated: the party is also producing a kind of working class, so it cannot be judged by output alone (p. 143).

## Arguments

**A. Lenin's vanguard as high-modernist planning (pp. 116-124)**

- **Premises**: (p. 116) Lenin's writings focus on a unitary scientific answer known to a trained intelligentsia. (pp. 116-119) His metaphors for the party (teacher, officer, brain, engineer, architect) all put knowledge and direction at the top. (p. 119) Socialist consciousness must come from outside the working class. (p. 120) Specialization requires centralization. (p. 124) Le Corbusier's doctrine rests on the same features.
- **Unstated premises**: that metaphors in a polemic reveal how the writer actually thinks, rather than what persuades his audience; Scott asserts this for the military figures (p. 117) but does not show it. That What Is to Be Done? is the representative Lenin; Scott explicitly sets aside the practitioner (p. 116), and then judges the theory by the practice in the next section. That an argument shaped by conditions under the czarist police can be separated cleanly into a "contingent" part and a principled part (p. 122); Scott does this by reading one passage, saying "There is no other way to interpret" it (p. 122).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 124) Lenin and Le Corbusier "shared some basic elements of the high-modernist outlook" and were representative of it.

**B. The plan failed as a description of 1917 and succeeded as a description of the state (pp. 125-126)**

- **Premises**: (p. 125) The Bolsheviks contributed little to the overthrow of czarism (Carr, Arendt). (p. 125) Workers' soviets and peasant seizures were spontaneous and ignored central instructions. (pp. 125-126) Command structures fail in battle anyway, and Lenin's had never existed. (p. 126) After October the new state destroyed the soviets' independence and imposed control on the peasantry.
- **Unstated premises**: that a disciplined cadre was irrelevant to the outcome; Scott himself concedes that the October uprising "proved a crucial stroke" (p. 126), which is the one thing a small organized party is for. That the post-revolutionary state was shaped by Lenin's doctrine rather than by civil war, as the Workers' Opposition section half-admits for martial-law management (p. 140).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 126) As a description of revolution the model is "a pipe dream"; it is accurate only for the exercise of state power after the seizure.

**C. The official story as state simplification (pp. 126-128)**

- **Premises**: (p. 127) Victors write the history and have an interest in appearing as the prime movers. (p. 127) Leaders sincerely overrate their influence, and subordinates rarely contradict them. (p. 127) A schematic account legitimates, implies the workers cannot act alone, and names enemies. (p. 127) Hindsight naturalizes outcomes and hides contingency.
- **Unstated premises**: that the simplification of a narrative is the same kind of thing as the administrative simplifications of ch01-ch02 (maps, cadastres, surnames). Here the term shifts from a tool for seeing and acting on a population to a story told to it. Scott concedes in an endnote that the official narrative also needed a genuine mass movement, which it did not have to invent (endnote, p. 321).
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 127-128) The official history of the Bolshevik Revolution was made to conform to What Is to Be Done?

**D. Lenin's high modernism was consistent across fields (pp. 128-134)**

- **Premises**: (p. 128) State and Revolution still relies on state coercion and the party as teacher. (pp. 128-129) Its picture of administration is a static division of labour of simple operations. (p. 129) Lenin embraced Taylorism and one-man management. (pp. 130-132) The Agrarian Question condemns small farming as doomed. (pp. 132-133) Electrification is his image of central power. (pp. 133-134) Even his retreat after Kronstadt keeps the goal of remaking the peasant by machinery.
- **Unstated premises**: that sources from 1901 to 1921 express one unchanging outlook; the one-man-management passage on p. 129 is from a 1918 speech, not State and Revolution (endnote, p. 321). That Lenin's counter-evidence on small farms (self-exploitation) was weaker than the economists' data; Scott says it was "not completely convincing" (p. 131) without weighing it. The 1921 passage is read both ways: as "new modesty" and as unchanged vision (pp. 133-134); either reading fits the thesis, so it cannot test it.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 133) There is "little reason" to think Lenin ever abandoned the core of his high-modernist convictions.

**E. Luxemburg's critique: revolution as living process (pp. 134-139)**

- **Premises**: (p. 135) A mass strike involves the wills and knowledge of many agents, of which the party is only one. (pp. 135-136) Each strike changes both capital and the workforce, so process shapes the actor. (p. 136) Discipline, even if possible, would deprive the party of the proletariat's creativity. (p. 138) Errors made by a real movement are more valuable than an infallible committee. (p. 139) Without political freedom, life dies out in every public institution.
- **Unstated premises**: that the creativity lost through central control outweighs the coordination gained; the chapter never states the cost of disorder. That Luxemburg's view would survive power; Scott flags this himself (endnote, p. 323). That the workers' independent judgement points toward socialism; Scott notes in an endnote that she underrated bread-and-butter interests, nationalism and timidity (endnotes, pp. 324-325).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 139) Socialism must be discovered in collaboration between workers and the revolutionary state; (p. 137) her account of revolution fits the facts better than Lenin's.

**F. Uncharted tasks need mētis, and means shape ends (pp. 142-143)**

- **Premises**: (p. 142) Making revolutions and new forms of production are voyages with many unknowns, so blueprints are impossible. (p. 142) Such goals can be approached only stochastically, by trial and error. (p. 142) The knowledge they need comes from long practice and adaptation. (p. 142) Kollontay: feudal estate managers could not have invented capitalism, so specialists trained under capitalism cannot invent socialism. (p. 143) The party also produces a kind of working class.
- **Unstated premises**: that the workers' practical knowledge, formed under capitalism too, is not subject to the same objection Kollontay makes against specialists. That novelty of the goal is what defeats planning; Scott's later cases (forestry, agriculture) are not novel goals, so the argument for mētis will have to rest on something else. That "a plurality of experiments" (p. 143) can coexist with a revolutionary state; Luxemburg and Kollontay are both shown losing that argument.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 143) "the way the trip is made matters at least as much as the destination", and a vanguard can achieve results "in ways that defeat its central purpose".

## Evidence offered

- **Lenin's own texts**, quoted extensively: What Is to Be Done? (1929 International Publishers edition) for pp. 116-123 (endnotes, pp. 318-319); State and Revolution (1931 edition) for pp. 128-130 (endnotes, p. 321); The Agrarian Question and the Critics of Marx and The Agrarian Programme of Social Democracy for pp. 130-132 (endnote, p. 322); the GOELRO report via Tucker's Lenin Anthology (endnote, p. 323). Several Lenin quotations are second-hand: the one-man-management passage via Claudin-Urondo (endnote, p. 321), the 1917 "Socialism is not created by orders" and 1921 peasant passages via Avrich's Kronstadt (endnotes, pp. 321, 323), the Chernyshevsky remark via Conquest (endnote, p. 318).
- **Narrative of 1917**: E. H. Carr, Arendt (one line), and a list of social histories (Fitzpatrick, Ferro) for the confusion of October (pp. 125-126; endnotes, pp. 319-320); Figes for the Volga countryside (endnote, p. 320); Guérin for Lenin's 1918 remark that anarchist ideas had taken living form (endnote, p. 320). The reconquest thesis (p. 126) is stated briefly with Kronstadt, Tambov and the Makhnovshchina as its illustrations.
- **The official story**: Djilas's New Class (p. 127; endnote, p. 320); an acknowledgment to Peter Perdue (endnote, p. 320); the "for want of a nail" ditty and ordinary analogies about newspaper accounts and suicides (endnotes, pp. 320-321). This section is argued mostly from general reasoning, not from Soviet historiography.
- **Agrarian debate**: the German and Austrian household-survey schools, Chayanov and Maslov, named only in an endnote (endnote, p. 322). The claim that "we have since learned" how efficient small production can be (p. 132) has no citation here. Electricity: Coppersmith, Bailes, H. G. Wells's "Utopia of the electricians" (endnote, p. 322); the tractor point against Lenin is Scott's own (endnote, p. 323).
- **Mass exercises**: the 1891 Sokol congress via Garver (p. 124; endnote, p. 319); Ceauşescu's palace clocks (endnote, p. 319). Peter Rutland's caution that such displays were shared by democratic and bourgeois movements is in the endnote (endnote, p. 319), which weakens their use as a sign of authoritarian ideology.
- **Luxemburg**: three essays, "Organizational Questions" (1904), "Mass-Strike, Party, and Trade Unions" (1906) and "The Russian Revolution" (1918), from Howard's and Waters's English editions (p. 134; endnote, p. 323). Biographical colour from Ettinger and Nettl (endnotes, pp. 323-324), including the newspaper "like a wild orchard" (endnote, p. 324).
- **Kollontay**: a single text, "The Workers' Opposition", in Holt's translation, which reprints a 1921 translation because the Russian original could not be found (endnote, p. 325). Farnsworth's biography for her later fate (endnote, p. 325).
- **Perspective of the sources**: Lenin is read through his own polemics and through Western liberal or anti-Bolshevik historians (Carr, Conquest, Volkogonov via Service, Kolakowski, Keep). The critics are read through their own writings, sympathetically. No Bolshevik or Leninist defence of the vanguard model is heard, and the case for discipline is presented only in Lenin's words.
- **Qualifications placed in endnotes**: Lenin opposed any cult of personality (endnote, p. 319); neither Lenin nor Le Corbusier had a bureaucratic temperament (endnote, p. 319); Gramsci shared Lenin's view of the need for leaders (endnote, p. 318); Luxemburg's views overlap with Bakunin's (endnote, p. 324); her aesthetic of a "wild orchard" (endnote, p. 324); she was untested by power (endnote, p. 323); she underrated material interests (endnotes, pp. 324-325); Lenin's 1922 call to shoot Shuya believers as evidence that he kept his belief in violence (endnote, p. 323).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **The three-element model, now with the state.** ch04's large case, Brasilia, was built under a competitive political system (ch04, p. 101). This chapter supplies the missing element: Lenin's doctrine fails as a theory of revolution but describes the state the Bolsheviks built (p. 126), which matches ch03's claim that harm comes when high-modernist ideology meets unrestrained state power (ch03, p. 71). The chapter itself does not show the harms; it points forward to collectivization (p. 133) and ch06.
- **Which Lenin?** Scott sets aside the practical Lenin (p. 116), then uses practice to refute the theory (pp. 125-126), then counts the practical retreats as tactics that leave the theory intact (pp. 133-134). Theory is thus insulated from practice when practice is flexible and confronted with it when practice is spontaneous. The thesis is about Lenin's texts, and the evidence on texts is strong; the claims about consistency over 1901-1921 are weaker.
- **Asymmetric comparisons.** Luxemburg out of power is compared with Lenin in power; Scott answers that he compares their views out of power (endnote, p. 323), but the body's verdict on Luxemburg's "prophetic" warnings (p. 138) is a verdict on Lenin's state. The Jacobs and Luxemburg pairing is also uneven by Scott's own account: Le Corbusier had never heard of Jacobs, while Lenin and Luxemburg debated directly (p. 134).
- **Simplification changes meaning.** In ch01-ch02 a state simplification is an administrative instrument for seeing and acting. Here the official history of a revolution is "the ultimate state simplification" (p. 127), a narrative directed at citizens. The term widens from a means of governing to a legitimating story.
- **High modernism keeps widening.** ch03 defined it as a strong version of nineteenth-century beliefs in scientific and technical progress, applied usually through the state, and already allowed a right as well as a left version (ch03, pp. 71-72). Here it is "politically polymorphous" and can even be anarchist (p. 130), and Lenin's views get a lineage running back to Peter the Great and Arakcheev's military colonies (p. 133), echoing the stretch to Saint Petersburg at the end of ch04 (ch04, p. 115). Marx, Saint-Simon and Bellamy are included too (p. 130).
- **First appearance of mētis.** The concept the book is built toward appears here for the first time, defined briefly and deferred (p. 142), with ch09 as its full treatment. Its first job is to name what Luxemburg and Kollontay appeal to. This links it from the start to political participation and morale, not only to craft and local ecological knowledge as later chapters may stress.
- **Process and producing people.** The argument that a party produces a kind of working class, not just an outcome (pp. 135-136, 143), is new relative to ch04; Jacobs's case was about whether plans work. Here the claim is also that central control degrades the capacities of the people controlled, foreshadowed in Kollontay's dispirited workforce and "bad-faith" day (p. 142). This is an argument about character formation and is distinct from the knowledge argument.
- **Gender as vantage.** Kollontay's work for women is paralleled with Jacobs's roles as housewife and mother (p. 141), repeating ch04's "woman's eye" (ch04, pp. 108-109). Scott says "There is some evidence" (p. 141); the evidence is one passage of her pamphlet.
- **Agriculture and extension.** Lenin's agrarian views anticipate machine-tractor stations, state farms and collectivization (p. 133), setting up ch06. Kollontay's model of technical centres serving producers like an agricultural extension service (p. 141) is the positive counter-model that ch08's discussion of scientific agriculture will presumably test.
- **Haussmann and the forest.** The electrical grid is compared with the radial plan of Paris (p. 132); the text says "see chapter 1", but the centralized traffic hub material is in ch02 in this workspace; the forestry analogy of ch01 and ch04 is absent here, replaced by Luxemburg's organic metaphors (pp. 136-138).
- **Hayek again.** ch04's endnote distanced Scott from Hayek's naturalizing of the market (ch04, endnote, p. 318). Luxemburg's "unobstructed, effervescing life" (p. 139) naturalizes the revolutionary process in the same way, and Scott does not apply the same reservation to her.
- **Details that look wrong or inconsistent in the text.** (a) What Is to Be Done? is dated 1903 (p. 116); outside knowledge: it was published in 1902. (b) The Development of Capitalism in Russia is dated 1889 (endnote, p. 322); outside knowledge: 1899. (c) The Kronstadt historian is spelled "Averich" throughout the notes (endnotes, pp. 319-323); outside knowledge: Paul Avrich. (d) The autoworkers' plant is placed in "Lordsville, Ohio" (endnote, p. 322); outside knowledge: presumably the GM plant at Lordstown. (e) The Luxemburg quotation on p. 135 reads "clasped in his packet" and has an unbalanced inner quotation around "forbidden", probably a transcription slip for "pocket". (f) The 1904 to 1918 interval is called "Nearly fifteen years" (p. 138), which is fair. (g) "midwife" appears in the section heading (p. 134) but nowhere in the body. None of these affects the argument.

## Open questions

- If Lenin's model only became true after the seizure of power (p. 126), is the high-modernist plan the cause of the Soviet state's form, or did civil war and the reconquest (p. 126) produce it, with the doctrine as justification? The chapter asserts continuity but does not separate these.
- The October uprising was "a crucial stroke" (p. 126). Does that concede the one function a disciplined vanguard was designed for, and if so, what is left of the claim that the plan was irrelevant to the revolution?
- What would Luxemburg or Kollontay have done about the coordination problems of civil war and famine? Scott notes that martial-law methods were "perhaps understandable" during the war (p. 140) but never states the cost of their alternative.
- Is "the ultimate state simplification" (p. 127) the same concept as the cadastral map, or a metaphorical extension? If the latter, what work does the word "state" do when any victor's history would fit?
- The claim that small-scale farming is efficient and tenacious (p. 132) is asserted without evidence here. Does ch08 supply it, and does it hold for the German and Austrian data Lenin contested (p. 131)?
- Mētis is introduced to explain why revolution cannot be planned (p. 142). Is mētis the right concept for political creativity, which is novel by definition, when it is defined as knowledge from long practice at similar tasks?
- Does the means-shapes-ends argument (p. 143) apply to the liberal state as well? Scott's earlier chapters suggest representative institutions resist high modernism (ch03, pp. 82-83), but they also run by specialists and transmission belts of their own.
