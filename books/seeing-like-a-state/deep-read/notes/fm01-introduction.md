# fm01: Front Matter, Acknowledgments and Introduction (pp. 1–12)

## Question this chapter answers

Why have so many well-intended, state-led schemes to improve the human condition ended in disaster, and what does the book claim, and decline to claim, about the state, planning and local knowledge?

The Introduction (pp. 6-12) is the only argumentative part of this unit. The title page, contents and acknowledgments (pp. 1-6) are covered only for the intellectual debts that bear on the argument.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legibility | p. 7 | A state's arranging of population and terrain so that they can be read from the centre, which simplifies taxation, conscription and the prevention of rebellion. Called "a central problem in statecraft" (p. 7). |
| State simplifications | p. 7, p. 8 | Standard grids (names, measures, cadastres, registers, tenure, language, city layout) laid over complex local practice. Compared to abridged maps that show only the slice that interests the official observer. |
| Sedentarization | p. 7 | Efforts to settle mobile peoples permanently; the project from which the book's question first arose. |
| High-modernist ideology | p. 9 | A "muscle-bound" confidence in scientific and technical progress, mastery of nature and the rational design of social order. A faith that borrows the legitimacy of science, not science itself. |
| Miniaturization | p. 9 | The retreat, once large plans miscarry, to a controllable micro-order: model cities, villages and farms. |
| Authoritarian state | p. 10 | A state willing and able to use its full coercive power to bring high-modernist designs into being. |
| Prostrate civil society | p. 10 | A society lacking the capacity to resist the plans, usually after war, revolution or economic collapse. |
| Imperialism (of planned order) | p. 11 | The hegemonic planning mentality that excludes local knowledge. The target of the book, as opposed to planning as such. |
| Metis | p. 11 | Knowledge that can come only from practical experience; a portmanteau word for practical knowledge, informal process and improvisation. |
| Mutuality | p. 11 | The anarchists' term for non-hierarchical coordination; Scott says it covers some but not all of what he means by metis. |

## Leading propositions

- The book began as a study of why states are hostile to people who move around, and turned into a study of legibility (pp. 6-7).
- The premodern state was "partially blind" (p. 7): it lacked a detailed map of its terrain and people and a common metric.
- Permanent surnames, standard weights and measures, cadastral surveys, population registers, freehold tenure, standard language, city design and transport organisation are all attempts at legibility and simplification (p. 7).
- The same holds for the natural world: scientific forestry, plantations, collective farms, ujamaa villages and strategic hamlets make terrain, product and workforce manipulable from above (p. 7).
- State simplifications are maps that, allied with state power, remake the reality they depict; a cadastral map creates a tenure system by giving its categories the force of law (p. 8).
- The most tragic episodes of state social engineering arise from a combination of four elements, all of which are necessary: administrative ordering of nature and society, high-modernist ideology, an authoritarian state, and a prostrate civil society (pp. 9-10).
- The first element alone is an unremarkable tool, as vital to welfare and freedom as to despotism (p. 9).
- High modernism is found across the political spectrum, and in liberal parliamentary societies, where planners must negotiate with organised citizens, it could spur reform (p. 10).
- The schemes failed because designed order is necessarily schematic and depends on informal processes it cannot create or maintain (p. 10).
- The book is not a blanket case against bureaucratic planning or high-modernist ideology, not an uncritical celebration of the local and traditional, and not an anarchist case against the state (pp. 11-12).
- Large-scale capitalism is as much an agency of homogenisation and simplification as the state; the argument is not a case for unfettered market coordination (p. 12).

## Arguments

**A. The four-element account of disaster (pp. 9-10)**

- **Premises**: (p. 9) Legibility supplies the capacity for large-scale social engineering. (p. 9) High-modernist ideology supplies the desire. (p. 10) An authoritarian state supplies the determination to act. (p. 10) An incapacitated civil society supplies the levelled terrain.
- **Unstated premises**: that the four can be identified independently of the outcome they explain; that "full-fledged disaster" is a category with clear membership. The Introduction gives no case in which all four were present and disaster did not follow, or in which one was absent and it did.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 9) "All four are necessary for a full-fledged disaster."

**B. Why the plans failed (pp. 10-11)**

- **Premises**: (p. 10) Any production process depends on informal practices and improvisations that could never be codified; the work-to-rule strike shows this. (p. 10) The simplified rules behind a planned city, village or collective farm are likewise inadequate as instructions for a functioning order. (p. 10) The formal scheme is parasitic on informal processes.
- **Unstated premises**: that the work-to-rule case generalises from the factory to whole settlements and economies; that suppressing informal process, and not coercion, bad incentives or bad science, is the main cause of failure.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 10) To the degree the scheme ignores or suppresses those processes it fails its beneficiaries and finally its designers.

**C. The analogy between social and natural simplification (p. 11)**

- **Premises**: (p. 11) Monocrop forests and mechanised monocropping fail in ways that mimic collective farms and planned cities.
- **Unstated premise**: that the resemblance reflects a common mechanism and is not only a likeness of appearance.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 11) A case for the resilience of social and natural diversity, and for limits in principle on what can be known about complex functioning order.

## Evidence offered

- The modern beehive as an analogy for legibility: frames, the queen excluder and "bee space" arrange the colony for the beekeeper's inspection and extraction (pp. 7-8). An analogy, not evidence; Scott says he will not push it far (p. 8).
- Named disasters: the Great Leap Forward, Soviet collectivization, villagization in Tanzania, Mozambique and Ethiopia; Brasilia and Chandigarh as failed cities (p. 8). Of these, the Great Leap Forward and Mozambique get no chapter.
- The work-to-rule strike (p. 10).
- A cut case study of the Tennessee Valley Authority is mentioned twice (p. 6, p. 11); the American high-modernist case is therefore missing from the book by the author's own account.
- Debts that shape the evidence base: Holston on Brasilia, Fitzpatrick and Fitzgerald on Soviet and American agriculture, Detienne and Vernant on metis, Marglin on episteme and techne, Paul Richards and van der Ploeg on agriculture, Joel Gao Hiza on Tanzania (p. 4). Scott calls himself "an amateur as an Africanist" (p. 4).
- Endnotes: the endnote section (pp. 284-369) opens directly with chapter 1. I found no separate block of notes for the Introduction.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The four-element model is stated here and is the standard against which ch06 and ch07 should be tested. The legibility chapters (ch01, ch02) concern element one only.
- The disclaimer about markets (p. 12) promises that the conclusions apply to market-driven standardisation. Check ch08 and ch10 for whether that promise is kept in more than passing.
- The claim that authoritarian schemes "inevitably fail" (p. 11) when they exclude local knowledge is stronger than the "to the degree" formulation on p. 10.
- Scott admits the risk of looking at everything through one lens (p. 11).

## Open questions

- Is the four-element claim a testable causal claim or a description of the cases selected?
- If legibility is also the ground of welfare and citizenship (p. 9), what distinguishes a benign simplification from a harmful one before the fact?
- The Introduction says the state "may in some instances be the defender of local difference" (p. 12). Does the book ever show one?
