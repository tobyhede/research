# ch07: Compulsory Villagization in Tanzania: Aesthetics and Miniaturization (pp. 174–206)

## Question this chapter answers

What happens when authoritarian high modernism is carried out by a relatively benign, weak, development-minded state rather than by Stalin, and what does that case reveal that the Soviet case could not? Scott's answer: the "family resemblances" persist (p. 175). Villagization still aimed at legibility and control, still continued colonial agrarian policy, still failed economically and ecologically, and it shows more clearly a third motive that ch06 only touched: an aesthetic, quasi-religious faith in how a modern village and farm should look, which travels free of its original context and tends to retreat into miniatures (model villages, demonstration farms, new capitals) when the larger transformation fails (pp. 175, 198-205). The chapter then generalizes, through the Ethiopian case and a closing theoretical section, to the "thinness" of all planned communities and the "dark twin" of informal practice that every such plan needs (pp. 200-206).

The unit runs: introduction and the four resemblances (pp. 174-176); colonial high-modernist agriculture, the lower Shire Valley and the groundnut scheme (pp. 176-179); villages and improved farming before 1973, including Dodoma 1970 and the Ruvuma Development Association (pp. 179-183); the 1973 order and Operation Planned Villages (pp. 183-186); the aesthetics of the planned village (pp. 186-187); communal farming and intensive production (pp. 187-191); bureaucratic convenience and interests (pp. 191-193); the "national plantation" and the impossibility argument (pp. 193-194); the Ethiopian variation (pp. 194-198); conclusion: optics of power, the failure of grids, thin and thick institutions, miniaturization of perfection and control, the dark twin (pp. 198-206).

Only the ch07 portion of p. 174 (after ch06's raspberry passage ends) and of p. 206 (before ch08 begins) is covered. Endnotes are on pp. 336-347 (ch07 notes 1-129 run from p. 336 to p. 347).

## Leading propositions

- The ujamaa campaign was the largest forced resettlement in independent Africa to that date, at least 5 million people (p. 174). Endnote: Nyerere claimed over 9 million, but many villages were administrative fictions (endnote, p. 336).
- Tanzania is a useful case because documentation is abundant and because the campaign was a development and welfare project by a relatively benign, weak state, not punitive appropriation or ethnic cleansing (p. 174).
- Nyerere's resettlement thinking was continuous with colonial policy and with international development discourse shaped by the TVA, American capital-intensive farming and wartime mobilization (pp. 174-175).
- The campaign was not a war of appropriation, but it was coercive, occasionally violent, and an ecological and economic failure (p. 175).
- Four family resemblances to the Soviet case: improvement and legibility, political control, an aesthetic dimension detached from efficiency, and failure through neglect of local knowledge (pp. 175-176).
- Social engineering's efficiency depends on the cooperation of its subjects, who can make an efficient-in-principle arrangement inefficient (p. 176).
- Colonial policy in East Africa rested on faith in scientific agriculture and skepticism toward African practice, a skepticism that also justified the experts' authority (p. 176).
- Resettlement appealed to colonial planners because it allowed a start from scratch on a prepared, legible site; the more details planners filled in, the more control (p. 177).
- The lower Shire Valley plan was driven by a pictorial technical imagination, including a three-dimensional model of the whole scheme (p. 177), and "failed almost completely" (p. 177).
- Peasants' resistance to ridging was later shown to be economically and ecologically sound (p. 178).
- The Shire plan failed because it standardized both the environment and the cultivator; peasants had flexible, plot- and season-specific repertoires (p. 178).
- Unassisted, illegible pioneer settlement outside state purview was more productive but was castigated as squatting (p. 178).
- The groundnut scheme (3 million acres planned, under 10 percent cleared by 1950) failed through abstract agronomic figures and blind faith in machinery (pp. 178-179); capitalist and socialist high modernism were equally inappropriate to Tanzania (p. 179).
- At independence about 11 of 12 million rural people lived scattered and largely beyond state legibility and appropriation (p. 179).
- TANU's rural popularity rested partly on endorsing resistance to colonial agricultural regulations (p. 180).
- For Nyerere, villages, services, communal farming and mechanization were one package; tractors and electrification were emblems shared with Lenin (pp. 180-181).
- Nyerere's voluntarism was qualified from the start by the conviction that peasants did not know their own good, echoing the 1961 World Bank report (p. 181).
- Pilot schemes in the 1960s became expensive showpieces irrelevant to Tanzanian conditions (p. 182).
- Single-party rule exaggerated bureaucratic pathologies: sites chosen from blank spots on maps, unrealistic regional plans (p. 182).
- Dodoma 1970 previewed compulsion: famine relief tied to residence in ujamaa villages, forced amalgamation, punishment of dissenting officials (pp. 182-183).
- The banning of the Ruvuma Development Association in 1968 showed that villagization meant central control, not just village formation or communal farming (p. 183).
- The December 1973 order put the whole state behind compulsory, universal villagization (p. 183).
- Speed was intended: shock settlement would uproot peasants and make them remakeable as modern producers (p. 184).
- Sites were chosen for administrative convenience, often far from water and fuelwood and beyond carrying capacity (p. 184).
- Violence was inevitable given resistance and a crash program: threats, militia, house burning (p. 185).
- Wealthy cash-crop areas overrepresented in the bureaucratic elite were largely spared (p. 185).
- Nyerere deplored abuses but would not call off the campaign; meetings were one-way, and the outcome was an alienated, uncooperative peasantry (pp. 185-186).
- Roadside linear villages show control trumping production (p. 186).
- The planned village was a point-by-point negation of existing practice: shifting cultivation, polycropping, dispersal, lineage authority, opacity (p. 187).
- Villagization itself so disrupted production that huge food imports were needed from 1973 to 1975; about 60 percent of new villages were on semiarid land (p. 188).
- Forced cultivation needed a concentrated, monitorable peasantry, as colonizers knew (p. 188).
- Communal fields were experienced as corvee; the nested section and cell system was designed for transparency but broke down; plots were divided per household and neglected in favour of private plots (pp. 188-189).
- The 1978 plan stated the aim openly: identify able-bodied workers and acreage, set work targets (p. 189).
- Nyerere's premise was the colonial premise: African practice is backward, and experts are the solution (pp. 189-190).
- Monocropping and row planting greatly simplify inspection, trials, extension and harvest control, as the stripped-down forest did for foresters (p. 191).
- Bureaucratic pathologies are inherent in high-modernist campaigns (p. 191): goal displacement toward quantitative criteria (pp. 191-192) and reinterpretation in line with officials' corporate interests (pp. 192-193).
- The scheme approached a vast noncontiguous state plantation, unquestioned because it sailed under the banner of development (p. 193).
- Even with more time, skill and a better manner, the state could not have gathered the information needed for a schematic plan to succeed (p. 193).
- Specific ecological failures: loss of flood lands, unfamiliar soils, distance from fields, disease, and disaster for Maasai range conservation (p. 194).
- Tanzanian leaders were consumers rather than producers of high-modernist faith, shared by the World Bank and USAID (p. 194).
- Peasant tactics and state weakness made practice far less destructive than theory (p. 194).
- Ethiopia combined Russian coerciveness with Tanzanian rationale and a century-old imperial project of subjugation (pp. 194-195).
- Ethiopian villages followed a strict central template (1,000 inhabitants, 1,000-square-metre compounds) requiring no local knowledge (pp. 195-196); refugees understood the purpose as control (p. 196).
- Resettlement destroyed local knowledge and 30,000-40,000 functioning communities; much of the famine was its product (pp. 196-197).
- Villagization plans were not scientific in any meaningful sense; they were an aesthetic conviction plus a teleology (pp. 198-199).
- Peasants were the empiricists, planners the true believers (p. 199).
- The visual codification served the status, mission and centralizing interests of its bearers (p. 199).
- Formal structures are easier to change than practices; organizations run on tacit understandings, as work-to-rule shows (pp. 200-201).
- Planned communities are thin, like Esperanto (pp. 201-202).
- Frustration drives a retreat into miniatures, which offer control, visual completeness and political display (pp. 202-203).
- New capitals, from New Delhi to Dodoma, function as theme parks of high-modernist development (pp. 203-204).
- Every thin planned institution generates a dark twin; the more rigid the official micro-order, the larger the nonconforming practice needed to sustain it (p. 205).

## Arguments

**A. Benign intentions do not escape the family resemblances (pp. 174-176, 193-194)**

- **Premises**: (p. 174) Tanzania was a development project by a relatively benign, weak state. (p. 175) Nyerere warned against coercion. (p. 175) Yet the campaign shared the logic of improvement, the aim of control, an aesthetic dimension and failure. (p. 175) Parallels with colonial policy suggest something generic about the developmentalist state. (p. 194) The same vision was shared by the World Bank and USAID.
- **Unstated premises**: that the shared features flow from high modernism rather than from the general situation of any poor state trying to deliver services to a dispersed rural population; Nyerere's 1962 case (pp. 180-181) is a plain service-delivery argument that needs no aesthetic. That "benign" can be granted while the campaign was still coercive; endnote 28 records preventive detention of about five hundred opponents and routine suborning of the legal system (endnote, p. 338), so the benign-state framing is relative to neighbours. The appropriation motive Scott sets aside (p. 174) re-enters through endnote 65: the state faced a fiscal crisis, and regimenting the peasant economy to raise revenue was "virtually the only alternative available" (p. 342), in an endnote, and through the official class's gains (endnote, p. 343).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 175) "The parallels suggest that we have stumbled across something generic about the projects of the modern developmentalist state."

**B. Colonial schemes rehearsed the failure (pp. 176-179)**

- **Premises**: (p. 176) Colonial officials trusted scientific agriculture and distrusted African practice. (p. 177) Resettlement allowed a fresh, legible site. (p. 177) Shire planners worked from a pictorial imagination and an architect's model. (p. 178) Ridging failed on sandy soil, as later research confirmed. (p. 178) The plan standardized environment and cultivator alike. (p. 179) The groundnut scheme applied general figures without soil maps or trials.
- **Unstated premises**: that Beinert's single Malawi study is representative of British East Africa; Scott asserts that policy varied little (p. 176). That the cause of failure is the standardization itself, rather than the specific wrong technical beliefs (ridging, dryland rotation) that could in principle have been corrected; Scott's own concession that "later research" vindicated peasants (p. 178) implies that better science could have helped. The groundnut scheme mixes capitalist firm and colonial state (p. 178), which Scott uses to show that high modernism is not socialist property; the argument assumes that failure reflects the plan's abstraction rather than ordinary commercial misjudgement.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 178) the Shire plan "was the kind of environmental and social taxidermy that doomed it almost from the start."

**C. Villagization was about control, not merely villages (pp. 179-189)**

- **Premises**: (p. 179) Most rural people were outside state legibility. (p. 182) Dodoma used famine relief as leverage. (p. 183) The RDA, which embodied Nyerere's ideals, was banned for its independence. (p. 186) Roadside siting served monitoring over production. (pp. 188-189) Sections and cells mirrored residential and labour hierarchies for transparency. (p. 189) The 1978 plan named identification of workers and acreage as the gain.
- **Unstated premises**: that the RDA case shows the system's logic rather than a contingent conflict over one crop (fire-cured tobacco, p. 183). Scott admits it is "hard to tell how economically sound" the RDA's enterprises were (p. 183). That roadside siting is about control rather than about the services Nyerere promised, which also need roads; Bernstein is cited for the roadside point (endnote, p. 340).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 183) the RDA's "refusal to fit into the centralized scheme of the party was fatal."

**D. The aesthetic is an independent cause (pp. 175, 181, 186-187, 190-191, 198-199)**

- **Premises**: (p. 175) Plans travel as visual images held as faith. (p. 181) Nyerere spoke of "proper" villages. (p. 187) Bureaucrats preferred houses and fields in straight lines. (p. 191) Extension officers judged success by straight, pure-stand rows and promoted monocropping as faith despite mounting evidence for intercropping. (p. 192) Houses were moved fifty feet to the surveyor's line. (pp. 198-199) The beliefs were aesthetic and teleological, not empirical.
- **Unstated premises**: that aesthetic preference can be separated from administrative convenience; Scott's own analysis of monocropping lists only administrative advantages (p. 191), and the house-moving example is "presumably linked to easy surveying and the desire to please the inspecting officials" (p. 192). Much of the evidence for an aesthetic motive is Scott's inference, flagged "I believe" (p. 175; p. 181) and "One imagines" (endnote, p. 337). The origin of the image is left open: Russia, China or the West (endnote, p. 338; p. 187). Endnote 10's Bohemian maize fields show that Scott's own visual expectations are historically contingent (endnote, pp. 336-337), which supports the general claim but also concedes that every observer has such an aesthetic, including critics.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 198) "such plans were not scientific or rational in any meaningful sense of those terms."

**E. Bureaucratic pathologies are inherent, not incidental (pp. 191-193)**

- **Premises**: (p. 191) Authoritarian social engineering needs force and treats subjects as functions of administrative routines. (pp. 191-192) Officials shifted the goal to countable outputs. (p. 192) Regional competition drove rushed implementation. (p. 192) Nyerere saw the campaign through statistics. (p. 193) Officials evaded participatory policies that would have reduced their power, following Coulson.
- **Unstated premises**: that these behaviours are specific to high-modernist campaigns rather than general features of large bureaucracies under pressure for results; Scott's "inherent" (p. 191) is stronger than the evidence, which shows they occurred. Ferguson's Lesotho study (endnote, p. 343) is offered as a parallel and supports the corporate-interest argument from a non-socialist case.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 191) "Far from being regrettable anomalies, these behavioral by-products are inherent in high-modernist campaigns of this kind."

**F. No plan could have succeeded: the information argument (pp. 193-194)**

- **Premises**: (p. 193) Peasant economic activity and movement are a complex, delicate, pliable set of adaptations. (p. 193) Like customary tenure in ch01, they defy codification. (p. 194) Specific ecological failures follow from ignoring them.
- **Unstated premises**: that the plan had to be "fundamentally schematic" (p. 193); the argument shows that a uniform plan imposed from above cannot work, not that state intervention per se cannot. Scott grants experts "did have something to contribute" (p. 194), which leaves room for non-schematic, consultative intervention he does not describe here. The argument runs parallel to Hayek, whom Scott cites later but qualifies (endnote, p. 346).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 193) "To concentrate on such administrative and political shortcomings, however, is to miss the point."

**G. Thin institutions and their dark twins (pp. 200-206)**

- **Premises**: (p. 200) Formal structure is easier to change than practice. (pp. 200-201) Organizations depend on tacit coordination, which work-to-rule exploits. (p. 201) Villages, cities and languages are products of many hands. (p. 201) Hayek makes the point for markets; Scott extends it to social life. (pp. 201-202) Esperanto shows how thin a designed language is. (p. 205) Brasilia generated an unplanned Brasilia.
- **Unstated premises**: that thickness is good and thinness bad in general; Scott concedes in an endnote that the natural/artificial binary is "ultimately untenable" and narrows "artificial" to planning at a single stroke (endnote, p. 346), so the argument is about speed and centralization, not design as such. The Esperanto analogy cuts both ways: national languages that did prevail were often state-imposed (Scott quotes the linguists' saying that a national language is "a dialect with an army" (p. 202)), so state power can make a thin form thick over time. The "dark twin" hypothesis is explicitly "speculative" (p. 205).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 205) "the greater the pretense of and insistence on an officially decreed micro-order, the greater the volume of nonconforming practices necessary to sustain that fiction."

**H. Miniaturization as retreat (pp. 182, 202-205)**

- **Premises**: (p. 202) Large-scale plans meet intractable resistance. (p. 202) A response is retreat into model cities and Potemkin villages. (p. 202) Miniatures minimize rogue variables, concentrate resources, and please superiors. (p. 203) Tuan's toys and gardens suggest a general human wish to domesticate the uncontrollable. (pp. 203-204) Capitals from New Delhi to Dodoma are such miniatures. (pp. 204-205) Colonial settlement schemes and the focal-point approach were rural miniatures.
- **Unstated premises**: that the motive is psychological or symbolic rather than ordinary political economy; the pilot-scheme logic is rational if it scales (p. 202), and Scott concedes this. The Tuan analogy is suggestive, offered as "tempting to wonder" (p. 203). The Dodoma claim that the hilltop refuge was "not by accident" (p. 204) is Scott's reading. Endnote 120 offers a non-aesthetic reason for new capitals (neutral ground among communities) (endnote, p. 347), which competes with the miniaturization reading.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 202) "This miniaturization of perfection, I think, has a logic all its own"

## Evidence offered

- **Scale and statistics**: at least 5 million moved (p. 174), Nyerere's figures of 2,028,164, 9,100,000 and 13,065,000 people in villages, 70 percent moved in about three years (p. 192), with Hyden's skepticism of official figures (endnote, p. 336). 11 of 12 million rural people scattered at independence (p. 179). 1.2 billion shillings of food imports (p. 188); a fivefold volume and thirtyfold value increase in imports, 1973-75 (endnote, p. 340). About 60 percent of new villages on semiarid land (p. 188), uncited in the body.
- **Colonial schemes**: Beinert on the lower Shire Valley (pp. 176-178; endnotes, pp. 336-337), the main source for welfare colonialism; nearly thirty thousand laborers conscripted in the war (p. 176); the groundnut scheme figures and Samuel's motto (pp. 178-179), via Coulson and Johnson and Ruttan (endnote, p. 337), with a workforce of thirty-two thousand Africans (endnote, p. 337).
- **Nyerere's own words**: 1962 inaugural (p. 180), "Socialism and Rural Development" 1967 (p. 180), the tractors passage (p. 181), voluntarism and its qualification (p. 181), the 1961 traitor broadcast (p. 190), the 1973 radio order paraphrased by Hyden (endnote, pp. 338-339), the 1976 report of figures (p. 192), admissions of abuse (pp. 185-186). Nyerere is quoted directly and at length; he is the best-represented voice in the chapter.
- **Official and expert documents**: 1961 World Bank report (p. 181; endnote, p. 338; endnote, p. 339), first five-year plan (p. 187), 1964 plan (p. 190), third five-year plan of 1978 via Bernstein (p. 189; endnote, p. 341), a 1956 Department of Agriculture document on the focal-point approach (p. 205; endnote, p. 347), Mwapachu, an official in charge of forced settlement in Shinyanga (p. 183; p. 186; p. 192; endnote, p. 339).
- **Secondary scholarship on Tanzania**: Coulson (the most cited, both his book and his review article, which Scott calls splendid, endnote, p. 338), Boesen, Madsen and Moody's Ujamaa: Socialism from Above (pp. 182, 187-188, 190; endnotes, pp. 338, 340-342), Hyden (pp. 180, 185; endnotes, pp. 336-337, 339-340), McHenry (endnotes, pp. 339-340), Bernstein (endnotes, pp. 340-342), Cliffe and Cunningham (endnotes, pp. 336, 338), Raikes (endnotes, pp. 340, 342-343), De Vries and Fortmann (endnote, p. 342), Thiele (endnote, p. 341), Moore (p. 186). Scott disagrees with Hyden's "economy of affection" explanation of peasant resistance (endnote, p. 340).
- **Peasant voices**: a University of Dar es Salaam student report on Kigoma (p. 185; endnote, p. 340); a cultivator who shot a regional commissioner (endnote, p. 340); villagers' demands for written contracts (p. 185). These are mediated through students and scholars; no direct interview material for Tanzania.
- **Student and unpublished work**: Joel Gao Hiza's senior essay (endnote, p. 337), Bruce McKim's term paper (endnotes, pp. 340-341), Peters' and Donham's seminar papers (endnotes, pp. 345, 347), Müller's unpublished paper (endnote, p. 347), Aminzade's personal communication (endnote, p. 343).
- **Ethiopia**: Cultural Survival's Spoils of Famine (Clay, Steingraber and Niggli) for policy, refugee interviews in Somalia, Tigray cropping and Gambella (pp. 195-197; endnotes, pp. 344-345); Cohen and Isaksson on Arsi (p. 195; endnote, p. 344); Kebbede for Mengistu's statements (p. 195; endnote, p. 344). Scott notes the two sets of sources diverge in how far they trust regime claims, and finds the 4.6 million figure "a bit fishy" (endnote, p. 344). Figures 32-33 contrast a plan and an aerial photograph (pp. 195-196).
- **Comparative cases in endnotes**: Mozambique cotton concentrations (endnote, p. 341), Pol Pot's hectare squares (endnote, p. 344), Ceausescu's systematization (endnote, p. 345), the Javanese transmigrants flown to disorient them, from a World Bank official's remark (endnote, p. 339), Mao's staged tableaux (endnote, p. 346), Soviet and Indonesian theme parks (endnote, p. 346).
- **Theory**: Jacobs (p. 175), Berger via Jay on perspective (p. 199; endnote, p. 345), Scott's own Domination and the Arts of Resistance on mass display (endnote, p. 345), Bauman's gardening metaphor (endnote, p. 345), Lindblom's muddling through (endnote, p. 346), Hayek and Polanyi (p. 201; endnote, p. 346), Tuan (p. 203), Stewart on miniatures (endnote, p. 346), Vale on capitals (pp. 203-204; endnotes, pp. 346-347), Holston on Brasilia (p. 205), Ferguson's Anti-Politics Machine (endnotes, pp. 343).
- **Scott's own observation**: only the Bohemian maize fields (endnote, pp. 336-337) and the Paris taxi example (p. 201). No fieldwork in Tanzania or Ethiopia.
- **Perspective of the sources**: mostly Western and Scandinavian critical scholars of ujamaa (Coulson, Boesen, Hyden, Bernstein, Raikes), many sympathetic to its aims, plus official Tanzanian sources read against the grain. Ethiopian material comes largely from a human-rights NGO interviewing refugees. Peasant voices are thin and mediated. Planners and officials are quoted mainly to expose them.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Back to ch06.** ch06 ended by promising that the wheat/raspberry distinction would "prove germane to the Tanzanian example" (p. 174). ch07 delivers through polycropping against monocropping (p. 191) and the Tigray farmer's fifteen crops (p. 197), but never returns to the raspberry terms explicitly. The chapter repeatedly cross-references ch06: the Chicago hotel-room plan for Verblud (p. 179; p. 182), Stalin's captured peasantry (p. 186), Nyerere and Lenin on tractors and electrification (p. 181), no "Dizzy with Success" speech (endnote, p. 342), the abolition of private trading spaces "As in Russia" (p. 196), echoing ch06's state spaces. The "miniaturization of order" introduced in ch06 (ch06, p. 153) becomes "miniaturization of perfection and control" here (p. 202), now with an explicit rational core (pilot schemes) and a psychological one (Tuan).
- **Appropriation again.** pt03 and ch06 asked what high modernism adds to appropriation. ch07 picks a case precisely to remove appropriation (p. 174), and so is the book's best test of whether high modernism alone produces the pathologies. But the test is not clean: control is a "thinly veiled subtext" (p. 175), the state faced a fiscal crisis that made regimenting peasants for revenue nearly the only option (endnote, p. 342), the official class gained trade and budget (endnote, p. 343), and Ethiopia's case is full appropriation and pacification (p. 196). The cleanest evidence for a non-appropriative high-modernist motive is the aesthetic material, which is also the most inferential (Argument D).
- **Aesthetic vs administrative logic.** Scott distinguishes "a bureaucratic logic and an aesthetic logic" (p. 186), but many of his aesthetic examples are explained in administrative terms in the same breath (pp. 191-192). The separation is asserted more than demonstrated. This matters for later syntheses: the book's distinctive claim (high modernism as visual faith) rests heavily on this chapter.
- **"Benign and weak" state against ch03's model.** ch03's dangerous combination needs authoritarian power and a prostrate civil society (ch03, p. 71). Tanzania had a weak state and a peasantry able to flee, smuggle and drag its feet (p. 194; endnote, p. 344), and the result was far less destructive than theory or than Ethiopia (p. 194; p. 196). This supports ch03's model: weakness of the state and residual civil society limited damage. It also suggests that the failure here is economic and ecological rather than catastrophic, which is a different kind of harm from ch06.
- **Peasants as empiricists.** The claim that peasants had readjusted with notable success in the two prior decades (p. 199) is uncited in the body, and endnote 47 grounds peasant caution in rational memory of failed schemes rather than tradition (endnote, p. 340). This anticipates ch08-ch09's metis argument. The word "metis" does not yet appear; "local knowledge" does (p. 175; p. 197).
- **Links to ch01 and ch02.** Customary tenure defying codification (ch01) is invoked explicitly for the impossibility argument (p. 193); the stripped-down forest (p. 191) and improvement (p. 175) recall scientific forestry. The serial-number epigraph from Anderson (p. 176) recalls ch02's legibility of persons.
- **Links to ch04.** Jacobs (p. 175), Brasilia's thinness and Holston's unplanned Brasilia (pp. 201-202, 205), and new capitals (pp. 203-204) tie the village to ch04's city. The dark twin generalizes ch04's informal Brasilia into a law-like hypothesis (p. 205).
- **Hayek and markets.** Scott borrows Hayek's knowledge argument (p. 201) and immediately qualifies it: markets require their own simplifications of land and labour, with Polanyi as the better case (endnote, p. 346). The groundnut scheme (p. 179) and the World Bank's role (p. 181; p. 194) show capitalist high modernism too. This is an important marker for any cross-book synthesis with The Road to Serfdom: Scott's knowledge argument cuts against both plan and market simplification.
- **Gains not weighed.** Endnote 87 reports a claim that resettlement eroded age and gender hierarchies (endnote, p. 343), and the stated goals (schools, clinics, water) are granted as distinctive (p. 194). The chapter does not weigh whether any services were delivered.

## Open questions

- Can the aesthetic motive be separated from administrative convenience with any evidence other than Scott's inference? What would a test look like: a case where the "proper" layout was chosen at clear administrative cost?
- Does the case really remove appropriation, given the fiscal crisis (endnote, p. 342) and the gains of the official class (endnote, p. 343)?
- How much of the production collapse was villagization and how much was the 1974 drought, oil prices and confiscatory producer prices (endnote, pp. 340-341; endnote, pp. 343-344)? Scott concedes this is difficult to specify (endnote, p. 343).
- Did villagization deliver the schools, clinics and water it promised, and did these persist? The chapter treats service delivery as rationale, not as outcome.
- Is the impossibility argument (p. 193) an argument against any state planning of settlement, or only against fast, uniform, coercive planning? What would a non-schematic intervention, using the experts' "something to contribute" (p. 194), look like? Lindblom's muddling through (endnote, p. 346) hints at Scott's answer.
- Is the dark-twin hypothesis (p. 205) testable, and does it hold for planned institutions in liberal states as well as authoritarian ones?
- How reliable is the Ethiopian famine attribution (p. 197), resting mainly on an NGO report based on refugee interviews?
