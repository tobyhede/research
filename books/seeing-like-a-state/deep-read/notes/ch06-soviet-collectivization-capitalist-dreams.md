# ch06: Soviet Collectivization, Capitalist Dreams (pp. 151–174)

## Question this chapter answers

Why did the Soviet state collectivize agriculture, why did collectivization fail as a scheme of production while succeeding as a scheme of appropriation and control, and what does the case reveal about the institutional preferences and the limits of authoritarian high modernism? Scott answers in three moves: he shows that faith in huge mechanized farms was shared by American and Soviet modernizers, so it was a high-modernist creed rather than a Marxist one; he narrates collectivization as the meeting of that creed with a crisis of grain procurement; and he generalizes the case into a table of legible and illegible institutions and a distinction between "proletarian" and "petit-bourgeois" crops.

The unit runs: Soviet high modernism, its czarist precursors and its cultural and aesthetic side (pp. 151-153); miniaturization of order (p. 153); the American industrial farming creed and the Soviet-American exchange, ending in the Verblud sovkhoz (pp. 153-158); collectivization as a war on the peasantry and its qualified success (pp. 158-160); Round One, 1917-21, the opacity of the revolutionary village and War Communism (pp. 160-164); Round Two, high modernism and procurement, 1928-30 (pp. 164-165); the kolkhoz as high-modernist plan and as second serfdom (pp. 165-170); state landscapes of control and the legibility table (pp. 170-172); the limits of authoritarian high modernism, wheat against raspberries (pp. 172-174).

Only the ch06 portion of p. 151 (after the Part 3 introduction ends) and of p. 174 (before ch07 begins) is covered. Endnotes are on pp. 328-336 (ch06 notes 1-83 run from p. 328 to the top of p. 336).

| Administrative utopianism | p. 151 | Stites's term for czarist schemes to organize serfs, soldiers and workers into hierarchical, disciplined, geometrical, welfarist institutions; Saint Petersburg and Arakcheev's military colonies are the examples (pp. 151-152). It makes Soviet high modernism a continuation of absolutism rather than a break (p. 151). |
| Gigantomania | p. 152 | Fitzpatrick's word for the Bolshevik passion for sheer size: the Palace of Soviets, the White Sea Canal, Magnitogorsk, collectivization (p. 152; endnote, p. 329). |
| Kultura | p. 152 | The ethic of the Bolshevik "new man": punctuality, cleanliness, directness, modest manners, clock-driven routine (p. 152). |
| Miniaturization of order | p. 153 | A facade or a small, managed zone of order that comes to substitute for the real thing; the representation displaces what it represents, as when a general mistakes the parade ground for the battlefield (p. 153). Introduced through Lunacharsky's staged revolution and Kaganovich's processions (p. 153). Becomes part of ch07's title. |
| Industrial farm / food and fiber factory | p. 154 | The American agricultural engineers' reconception of the farm on the model of the Taylorist factory: large scale, standard commodities, mechanization, lower unit costs (p. 154). Held "as a creed rather than a scientific hypothesis" (p. 154). |
| Context (as cause of failure) | p. 157 | Scott's single rubric for why plans like Verblud fail: the actual farm is particular, with its own soils, social structure, administrative culture, weather, machinery, roads and work habits, while the plan is generic (pp. 157-158). |
| Traditional statecraft (twin goals) | p. 159 | Appropriation and political control, the goals collectivization did serve, in contrast to its failed high-modernist goals of productivity and new men (p. 159; p. 170). |

| Potemkin collectives | p. 163 | Early state or collective farms set up to give legitimacy to existing practices (p. 163). |
| Total (sploshnaia) collectivization | p. 165 | The decision of late 1929, made in the course of the war over grain rather than as a planned initiative (p. 165). |
| Second serfdom | p. 168 | Peasant reading of VKP as vtoroe krepostnoe pravo; Scott argues the parallel was literal: forced labour on state land, corvée dues, delivery quotas from private plots, arbitrary officials, legal immobility through internal passports (p. 168). |
| State spaces / nonstate public spaces | p. 169 | Carried over from pt03 (pt03, p. 146) and applied inside the village: the tavern, fairs, church and mill give way to the kolkhoz office, meeting room and school (p. 169). |
| Elective affinity | p. 171 | Weber's term, used for the fit between authoritarian high modernism and certain institutions, explicitly not direct causation (p. 171; endnote, p. 335). |
| Legibility (as continuum) | p. 172 | Institutions ranked by how readily they can be monitored, directed and taxed from the centre; the table pairs illegible and legible forms in settlement, economic units, property, water, transport, energy and identification (p. 172). Note that private property is here less legible than state property, whereas ch01 treated freehold as the legible form. |

## Leading propositions

- Soviet high modernism started from an almost leveled social terrain, and so was more audacious than retrofitting (p. 151).
- Soviet high modernism continued Russian absolutism: Gellner's point that the Enlightenment reached Central Europe as a centralizing force (p. 151), and Stites's administrative utopianism of the czars (p. 151). Endnote 2 extends this to J. S. Mill, who considered backwardness a justification for authoritarian modernizers (endnote, p. 328).
- Czarist experiments, notably Arakcheev's military colonies of 750,000 people, quickly failed through resistance, corruption and inefficiency, so the landscape was already littered with miscarried authoritarian planning before 1917 (pp. 151-152).
- War, revolution, famine and civil war dissolved prerevolutionary society and schooled the Bolsheviks in the coercion of War Communism (p. 152).
- The Bolshevik project was cultural as well as physical: atheism campaigns, invented rituals, communal housing as social condensers, and the kultura of the new man (p. 152).
- Soviet cultural revolution emphasized public form; Lunacharsky's mass reenactments gave the revolution a retroactive order it never had (p. 153), and Stites suggests an inverse relation between this rational facade and the chaos beneath (p. 153).
- When a miniature of order is confused with the reality, the consequences can be disastrous (p. 153).
- Faith in huge, rationalized farming was international, and American and Soviet planners knew themselves to be in a common endeavour (pp. 153-154).
- In the United States, agricultural engineers inspired by Taylor sought to make the farm a factory between about 1910 and the late 1930s; because crop work is not repetitive, they standardized buildings and machinery and pushed mechanization of grain (p. 154).
- The industrial model fits some agriculture but was applied indiscriminately as an article of faith (p. 154).
- Campbell's Montana Farming Corporation (95,000 acres, 1918) chose the simplest crops and a near-blank site, yet gave no evidence that industrial farms beat family farms; its real advantages were credit, political influence and market muscle (pp. 154-155).
- Chain farms of the 1920s and 1930s were no more competitive, and their high fixed costs made them more vulnerable than family farms, which could retreat into subsistence (p. 155).
- Ezekiel and Johnson's 1930 plan for a national farming corporation, and voices hailing collectivization as historical necessity, show the American side of the faith (p. 156).
- The mutual admiration rested on a shared high-modernist faith, not a political ideology; Americans saw Soviet farms as experiment stations free of American political constraints (pp. 156-157).
- Wilson, Ware and Riggin planned a 500,000-acre farm in a Chicago hotel room in two weeks, which shows they took the key issues to be context-free (p. 157).
- The resulting sovkhoz Verblud (375,000 acres) was an economic failure whose causes can be summed up as context (pp. 157-158).
- Collectivization is an "extreme but diagnostic case" of authoritarian high modernism, imposed by force by officials ignorant of the rural economy (p. 158).
- Between 1930 and 1934 the state waged a virtual war on the countryside; Stalin read peasant withholding as a quiet war of starvation (p. 158). Deaths estimates run from 3-4 million to over 20 million, with higher figures gaining credibility (p. 158).
- Collectivization failed every specifically socialist goal: production, cheap food, technical efficiency, electrification, new men, closing the town-country gap; yields stagnated for half a century (p. 159).
- It was a qualified success at appropriation and control: the state could set crops, fix rural wages, take a large share of grain and politically emasculate the countryside (p. 159).
- Like scientific foresters, the Bolsheviks replaced an acephalous, footloose rural society with large, hierarchical, legally immobile farms (p. 159).
- The system persisted for sixty years thanks less to the plan than to informal improvisation, gray markets and barter, an analogue of informal Brasília (pp. 159-160).
- Collectivization cannot be laid solely at Stalin's feet; it was always on the Bolshevik map, followed from forced industrialization, and the faith outlived him in Khrushchev's virgin lands scheme (p. 160).
- Until 1930, Bolshevik rural policy shows more continuity than difference with the czarist state: reform from above, faith in large farms, ignorance, grain raids (p. 160).
- The revolution made the village more opaque: land seizures compressed rural society, records became useless, holdings changed constantly (pp. 161-162).
- The revolution also destroyed the marketed-grain sector (large farms had supplied nearly three-quarters of marketed grain), so extraction now collided with peasant subsistence (pp. 161-162).
- The revolution strengthened village capacity to resist; villages acted as autonomous republics, and the Bolsheviks arrived as a reconquest (p. 162).
- The Bolsheviks lacked cadastre, census, local base, the old state apparatus and native trackers (p. 162).
- Village opacity was also a conscious strategy: communes underreported land and overreported population; arable was underestimated by about 15 percent in the 1917 census (p. 163).
- Where tried, collective farms revealed the administrative attraction of radically simplifying the landholding and taxpaying unit (p. 163), as Yaney explains (p. 163).
- War Communism's quotas were fictional; over 150 uprisings and the Kronstadt revolt forced the NEP retreat (pp. 163-164).
- The drive of 1929-30 came from the conjunction of high-modernist faith and a crisis of appropriation (p. 164).
- The collectivized sector of the 1920s, with 10 percent of the labour force, produced 2.2 percent of output, so crash industrialization required the grain of middle and rich peasants (p. 164).
- Historians agree that the overriding purpose of collectivization was grain seizure (p. 165), and peasants saw it that way too (p. 165).
- The determination to seize grain was filtered through a high-modernist lens, a vision of agriculture "as much visual as scientific" (p. 166).
- Planning was abstract: agronomists composed a plan for a nonexistent commune without leaving their offices, and a scheme was cut into 32 equal map squares ignoring terrain and villages (p. 166).
- The modernist vision is semiotically a negation of the existing rural world, and the kolkhoz was meant to efface the peasantry (p. 166).
- Sowing plans were often unreasonable; monoculture and specialization reduced variables for agronomists and increased central power (p. 167).
- Procurement quotas were mechanically derived; meeting them raised the next round; the 1933-34 famine was a collectivization and procurement famine (p. 167).
- The kolkhoz literally resembled serfdom, and the resulting order was a cross between plantation agriculture and feudal servitude (p. 168).
- Collectivization aimed to destroy the mir as the unit of resistance, though most kolkhozy eventually coincided roughly with old communes (p. 168).
- All focal points of autonomous public life disappeared; nonstate public spaces gave way to state spaces (p. 169).
- The Bolshevik retrofit of rural Russia was more thoroughgoing than Haussmann's of Paris: legible kolkhoz for opaque mir, dependent employees for a petite bourgeoisie, homologous accounting units like McDonald's franchises (p. 169).
- Collectivization failed in each high-modernist aim and succeeded only in traditional statecraft (p. 170).
- Three sources of failure: an unfree labour force that resisted, a central machine unresponsive to local knowledge, and a Leninist structure that gave officials no incentive to negotiate with rural subjects (p. 170).
- Stalin's 1929-30 choice was to impose a designed, legible rural landscape; it was the newest state meeting the oldest class (pp. 170-171).
- Authoritarian high-modernist states impose institutional preferences that can mostly be deduced from legibility, appropriation and centralization (p. 171).
- These goals resemble premodern statecraft; the crucial difference is the new scale of ambition and intervention (pp. 171-172).
- More legible forms are more easily converted into rent, private or state (p. 172).
- Soviet agricultural failure was overdetermined by causes beyond high modernism: Lysenko, Stalin, war conscription, weather (pp. 172-173).
- Centralized high-modernist solutions can be best for some tasks: space exploration, transport networks, flood control, epidemic and pollution control (p. 173).
- Collective farms succeeded with major grains and failed with fruit, vegetables, small livestock, eggs, dairy and flowers, which came mostly from private plots (p. 173).
- Delicate crops need attentive care of crop and labour; small family enterprises have a comparative advantage, and success demands local knowledge (pp. 173-174).

## Arguments

**A. Industrial farming was a high-modernist faith, not a Marxist one (pp. 153-158)**

- **Premises**: (p. 154) American agricultural engineers, trained in Taylorism, set out to turn farms into factories. (p. 154) The model was applied as a creed. (pp. 154-155) Campbell's farm gave no evidence of superior efficiency. (p. 156) Ezekiel and others called for collectivized farming in the United States. (pp. 156-157) Americans admired Soviet farms for their scale and technical direction, not for Marxism, according to Fitzgerald and Feuer. (p. 157) American experts planned Verblud in a hotel room.
- **Unstated premises**: that the convergence of American and Soviet enthusiasm shows a common cause (a modernist faith) rather than common responses to the same technology and the same agrarian depression; endnote 18 notes that the American farm depression began after World War I, which made bold experiments cheap (endnote, p. 329), and endnote 17 shows that the market for American machinery lay in large-farm countries abroad (endnote, p. 329), both material reasons. That the period 1910-1930s is the right test of the industrial model; Scott's American evidence stops in the 1930s. Outside knowledge: large, highly mechanized and corporate grain farming later became normal in the United States, which would complicate the verdict that industrial farms were no better than family farms; the chapter does not address the later record. Nearly all the American material comes from one source, Fitzgerald's then unpublished draft manuscript (endnote, p. 329).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 156) "Behind these admiring references to Russia was less a specifically political ideology than a shared high-modernist faith."

**B. Collectivization failed as production and succeeded as statecraft (pp. 158-160, 169-170)**

- **Premises**: (p. 159) Collective farms failed in grain output, cheap food, efficiency, electrification and cultural change. (p. 159) Yields per hectare stagnated for fifty years. (p. 159) The kolkhoz let the state fix crops and wages, take grain and neutralize rural opposition. (p. 169) It replaced opaque, autonomous units with homologous, legible, dependent ones. (p. 170) The state got enough grain for industrialization.
- **Unstated premises**: that the "success" is measured against the regime's appropriation goals, while the "failure" is measured against its stated socialist goals; the two-level verdict depends on treating appropriation as the regime's real aim and productivity as the declared one. That grain extraction would have been impossible or costlier without collectivization; Scott does not consider the counterfactual of market procurement under NEP, which the regime rejected. That the 1933-34 famine and the slaughter of half the livestock (p. 158) count against production rather than also against appropriation; a regime that starves its producers is extracting from a shrinking base. Endnote 78 records 4.8 and 5.2 million tons of grain exported in 1930 and 1931 (endnote, p. 335), which supports the extraction claim.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 170) "Its successes, paradoxically, were in the domain of traditional statecraft."

**C. The revolution made the village illegible, and collectivization was the state's answer (pp. 160-165, 170-171)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 161-162) Land seizures, egalitarian redistribution and constant change made tenure unfathomable to tax officials. (p. 162) Villages became autonomous republics, and the Bolsheviks lacked cadastre, census, cadres and native trackers. (p. 163) Communes deliberately hid land. (p. 163) Large collective units simplified technical and fiscal problems, as Yaney argues. (pp. 164-165) The procurement crisis of 1928-29 made total collectivization the regime's solution. (pp. 170-171) Each annual turn of the screw produced more evasion.
- **Unstated premises**: that illegibility rather than price policy caused the procurement crisis; Scott himself reports the delivery price was one-fifth of the market price (p. 165) and endnote 43 notes there was little to buy with grain money (endnote, p. 332), which are incentive explanations that do not need legibility at all. That peasants' perception of collectivization (as grain seizure, as serfdom) is the true reading; this is well supported by the historians cited, but the peasant voices come through Platonov's novel (p. 165), Hindus via Davies (endnote, p. 333) and Figes, so they are literary and secondhand. The endnote on the mir (endnote, p. 334) adds that the commune's solidarity was itself shaped by state taxation of the commune as a unit, so the "opaque" unit the state attacked was partly a product of earlier state demands.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 171) Stalin "imposed a designed and legible rural landscape that would be far more amenable to appropriation, control, and central transformation."

**D. High modernism shaped the form, appropriation drove the timing (pp. 164-168)**

- **Premises**: (p. 164) The drive came from the conjunction of high-modernist faith and a crisis of appropriation. (p. 164) Stalin shared Lenin's faith in large, mechanized, scientific farms. (p. 166) The Bolshevik image of modern farming was visual: large, mechanized, Taylorist, proletarian. (p. 166) Stalin favoured grain factories of 125,000 to 250,000 acres. (p. 166) Plans were drawn on maps without regard for terrain. (p. 166) The vision was a negation of the peasant world.
- **Unstated premises**: that the faith did independent causal work; Scott's own account says the decision was not a planned initiative but came out of the grain war (p. 165), and that most kolkhozy eventually coincided with old communes for practical reasons (p. 168), so the high-modernist form gave way where it conflicted with control. The relative weight of ideology and appropriation is never settled, and Scott's retreat to "elective affinity" (p. 171) avoids having to settle it. This answers, only partly, the question left open by pt03 about what high modernism adds to the appropriation logic.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 166) the determination to seize grain "was a determination filtered through a high-modernist lens."

**E. The institutional preferences of authoritarian high modernism (pp. 171-172)**

- **Premises**: (p. 171) High-modernist ideologies prefer certain arrangements; authoritarian states impose them. (p. 171) Arrangements easily monitored, directed and taxed are promoted. (p. 171) Legibility is a prerequisite of appropriation and of transformation. (p. 172) The table ranks settlements, economic units, property regimes, resource organizations and identification by legibility.
- **Unstated premises**: that legibility, appropriation and centralization travel together; the table shows that they can diverge (private property is ranked illegible against state property, though a national cadastre, also legible, is what makes private property taxable, ch01). That the preferences are "deduced" from criteria rather than observed; Scott calls the scheme "crude and provisional" (p. 171). That the difference from premodern statecraft is only scale (p. 172); the cultural and aesthetic material at the start of the chapter (pp. 152-153) suggests a difference in kind as well, which this section drops.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 171) "Most of the preferences can be deduced from the criteria of legibility, appropriation, and centralization of control."

**F. Where high modernism works and where it fails (pp. 172-174)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 172-173) Soviet failure had many causes unrelated to high modernism. (p. 173) Centralized solutions suit some tasks, such as flood or epidemic control. (p. 173) Collective farms did well with grains and badly with fruit, vegetables, eggs and dairy, which came from private plots. (p. 173) Wheat tolerates mechanized, standardized handling; raspberries need adaptive, attentive care. (p. 174) Where delicate crops are grown at scale, firms must care for the crop and the workforce, and family farms have a comparative advantage.
- **Unstated premises**: that the crop typology, not the incentive structure, explains the split; private plots were also where peasants kept the fruits of their own labour, and Scott's first source of failure (unfree labour, p. 170) predicts the same split. That grain was a success; this sits awkwardly with the claim that kolkhozy were "an evident failure in raising the level of grain production" (p. 159). The resolution may be that grain was relatively less bad, but the text does not say so. Endnote 35 says high yields on show farms came at economically irrational cost (endnote, p. 331).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 174) "Successful raspberry growing requires, in other words, a substantial stock of local knowledge and experience."

## Evidence offered

- **Soviet culture and aesthetics**: Stites, Revolutionary Dreams, the chapter's main source for Soviet high modernism (endnote, p. 328), for administrative utopianism, festivals, Kaganovich's aesthetics and the facade/chaos thesis (pp. 151-153; endnotes, pp. 328-329). Fitzpatrick for gigantomania (p. 152; endnote, p. 329). Zamiatin and Orwell as literary witnesses to kultura (p. 152).
- **Czarist precursors**: Saint Petersburg's plan, Potemkin's model cities, Arakcheev's model estate and military colonies (750,000 people by the late 1820s) (pp. 151-152), all via Stites.
- **American industrial farming**: entirely from Deborah Fitzgerald's draft manuscript Yeoman No More, cited by draft chapter and page (endnote, p. 329), plus her article on Americans in the Soviet Union (endnote, p. 330) and Feuer on American travellers (endnotes, pp. 330-331). Campbell's figures for land, machinery and labour (pp. 154-155); chain farms (p. 155); Ezekiel and Johnson in the New Republic (endnote, p. 330); Michael Gold's pro-collectivization quotation (p. 156; endnote, p. 330); Fairway Farms and its Rockefeller money and New Deal links (endnote, p. 330); a list of enthusiastic visitors including Dewey, Tugwell and Campbell (endnote, p. 330). Worster's Dust Bowl is cited for ecological links (endnote, p. 330).
- **Verblud**: planned for 500,000 acres, established at 375,000 near Rostov-on-Don; an "abject failure" economically despite large early wheat output (p. 157). The detailed reasons are explicitly not given (p. 157).
- **Collectivization war**: Stalin's reply to Sholokhov (p. 158) and Khateyevich's statement that a famine showed peasants who was master (endnote, p. 331), both via Conquest. Death toll range of 3-4 to over 20 million and livestock losses (p. 158), uncited in the endnote, which instead turns to China's Great Leap Forward (endnote, p. 331). Yield stagnation and electrification (one farm in twenty-five) (p. 159), uncited.
- **Revolutionary village**: 494 peasant party members (p. 161; endnote, p. 332, Davies); land redistribution figures (248 million acres, about 70 acres per household) (p. 161; endnote, p. 332, Conquest); three-quarters of marketed grain from large farms before 1917 (p. 161); 15 percent underestimate of arable (p. 163); over 150 uprisings (p. 164); urban workers down from 3.6 million to 1.5 million (endnote, pp. 332-333). Figes is the main source for village autonomy and kombedy conflict (pp. 162-163; endnotes, p. 332). Yaney is block-quoted on the logic of simplification (p. 163; endnote, p. 332), with the note that some czarist agronomists became Bolshevik collectivizers (endnote, p. 332).
- **Procurement and collectivization**: delivery price one-fifth of market (p. 165; Davies); collectivized sector's 2.2 percent of output (p. 164; Davies); Fitzpatrick's and Conquest's statements on grain seizure as purpose (p. 165); 63 percent of the 1931 harvest confiscated (p. 166), uncited; grain exports of 1930-31 (endnote, p. 335, Lewin).
- **Kolkhoz practice**: Fitzpatrick's Stalin's Peasants for planning in offices, the 32 squares, sowing absurdities, Iakovlev's 1935 remarks, and the resemblances to serfdom (pp. 166-168; endnotes, pp. 333-334); peasant voices from Figes and Conquest ("second serfdom") (p. 168); half a million khutor dwellers resettled from 1939 (p. 168). Pryor on farm sizes in 1970 (endnote, p. 333). Stalin's 1935 defence of larger private plots, alongside his 1932 decree making withheld grain a shooting offence (endnote, p. 331).
- **Verchnyua Troitsa**: map and photographs from Teodor Shanin's research teams (p. 169; endnote, p. 334). Scott admits he does not know whether the old village's regular layout was itself administratively planned (endnote, p. 334), which weakens its use as an unplanned contrast.
- **Scott's own observation**: six weeks in 1990 on an ex-collective farm in Mecklenburg with world-class but uneconomic yields (endnote, pp. 334-335). This is the chapter's only first-hand evidence, and it is from East Germany, not the USSR.
- **Generalizing sources**: Weber for elective affinity (endnote, p. 335); Ardant on taxation (endnote, p. 335); Taine via Crozier on French schools (p. 172; endnote, p. 335); Sachs on planners preferring one giant firm (endnote, p. 334); Bakunin's forecast of industrial and agricultural armies under state engineers (endnote, p. 335).
- **Crop typology**: wheat and raspberries described from general agronomic knowledge, without a source (p. 173). The claim that private plots supplied most fruit, vegetables, eggs and dairy rests on Timofeev (endnote, pp. 335-336).
- **Perspective of the sources**: Western historians of Soviet Russia (Stites, Fitzpatrick, Conquest, Davies, Figes, Lewin, Yaney), much of whose work draws on party and state records and on newly opened archives (endnote, p. 331). Peasant views reach the text through a novel (Platonov), a 1931 travel account (Hindus) and historians' quotations. The American story rests on one unpublished manuscript. Scott twice disclaims expertise on the Soviet case (p. 151; p. 170). Planners speak mainly through hostile or ironic quotations (Stalin, Kaganovich, the Urals complaint).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **What high modernism adds to appropriation.** pt03 left open whether the appropriation logic alone explains Part 3's cases (pt03, p. 145). ch06 gives a split answer: appropriation drove the timing and the decision (p. 165), high modernism shaped the form (p. 166). But the decision came out of a grain war "not as a carefully planned policy initiative" (p. 165), most kolkhozy fell back onto the old commune boundaries (p. 168), and the successes were all in "traditional statecraft" (p. 170). On Scott's own account, the high-modernist content mostly explains the failures (giant farms, map squares, monoculture), while appropriation explains the persistence. "Elective affinity" (p. 171) names the relation without weighing it.
- **The diagnostic case is overdetermined.** Scott calls collectivization "diagnostic" of authoritarian high modernism (p. 158), then concedes that the failure was overdetermined by causes "having little to do with high modernism per se" (pp. 172-173), and two of his three sources of failure (unfree labour, the Leninist incentive structure) are about coercion and political structure rather than about high-modernist vision (p. 170). Endnote 77 adds bureaucratic pathologies and predicts such schemes go further off the rails in authoritarian than in parliamentary settings (endnote, p. 335). The case is strong evidence for the danger of authoritarian high modernism, weaker evidence for the danger of high modernism as such.
- **Grain: success or failure?** Kolkhozy were "an evident failure in raising the level of grain production" (p. 159), yet "successful at growing some crops, especially the major grains" (p. 173). Read charitably, grain was relatively less bad, but the crop typology needs the stronger claim.
- **Legibility and property.** ch01 made the freehold cadastre the paradigm of legible property. Here private property is ranked illegible against state property (p. 172), with a continuum offered to reconcile them (p. 172). Legibility is now relative to the degree of central direction, not just to the presence of a standard record. The rent remark (p. 172) quietly ties legibility to capitalist and state appropriation alike, which bears on the book's stance toward markets (anticipating later chapters, cannot verify from this unit).
- **Capitalist and socialist high modernism.** The chapter title and the American section establish that high modernism crosses the Cold War divide (pp. 153-157), echoing ch03's claim that high modernism is not tied to one ideology. The American schemes failed commercially (pp. 154-155) but did not become coercive; the difference Scott implies is the authoritarian state, which supports ch03's three-element model (high-modernist aspiration, unrestrained state power, prostrate civil society) (ch03, p. 71). The "bulldozed site" here (p. 151) is the prostrate civil society in action.
- **Forests, cities and franchises.** The Bolsheviks as scientific foresters (p. 159), wheat as the Norway spruce (p. 173), informal Brasília (pp. 159-160), Niemeyer against Haussmann (p. 151), Haussmann outdone (p. 169) and the McDonald's comparison (p. 169) tie this chapter to ch01, ch02 and ch04. Magnitogorsk as a Brasília-like start from zero (p. 171) links to ch04.
- **Lenin and the machine economy.** The economy "as a well-ordered machine" that Lenin foresaw (p. 152) and the Platonov epigraph rejecting the revolution as a steam engine (p. 158) connect to ch05's account of Lenin's admiration for German war planning and Taylorism.
- **State spaces inside the village.** pt03's state and nonstate spaces (pt03, p. 146) are reapplied at the scale of the village public sphere (p. 169), and the destruction of the mir echoes pt03's demobilized community (pt03, pp. 150-151). The endnote on the mir (endnote, p. 334) complicates the nonstate/state contrast: the commune's solidarity was itself a response to collective taxation by overlords.
- **Miniaturization of order** (p. 153) is a new concept that ch07's subtitle takes up; here it rests on festivals and processions rather than on agriculture, though demonstration farms with uneconomic yields (p. 170; endnotes, pp. 331, 334-335) are a farming instance of the same idea.
- **Local knowledge set up for later.** The raspberry passage (pp. 173-174) and the "chapter 8" pointer (p. 173) prepare the metis argument; "context" as the cause of Verblud's failure (p. 157) is metis described negatively.
- **Normative hedges.** Scott grants that centralized solutions are best for some tasks (p. 173), and that Stalin was not the sole cause (p. 160). He does not consider whether any version of large-scale grain farming could have served Soviet industrialization with less coercion.
- **Details that look wrong or garbled in the text.** (a) The heading reads "Authontarian High-Modernist Theory" (p. 165), an OCR error for Authoritarian. (b) "malokultumyi" (p. 166) for malokul'turnyi (outside knowledge). (c) "Kaganivich" (p. 153); outside knowledge: Kaganovich. (d) "Ezekial" throughout (p. 156; endnote, p. 330); outside knowledge: Mordecai Ezekiel. (e) "the states grain seizures" (p. 164) and "the states devising" (p. 171) lack apostrophes. (f) The quotation from a report on bailiffs (p. 168) has a stray doubled opening quote and an ellipsis in the source, so it cannot be quoted here. (g) Endnotes 60, 68 and 71 cite "Fitzgerald, Stalin's Peasants" (endnotes, pp. 333-334); elsewhere the book is correctly Fitzpatrick's (endnote, p. 331), so these are misattributions. (h) Endnote 39 is "Ibid., p. 432" following a note on Fitzpatrick's Stalin's Peasants (endnote, p. 331), but the phrase it supports, on czarist tribute-collecting methods (p. 160), sounds like Yaney; the source of this quotation cannot be verified. (i) Endnote 62's list repeats "engines" (endnote, p. 333). (j) Outside knowledge: the Soviet famine is usually dated 1932-33, not 1933-34 (p. 167). (k) Endnote 74 quotes Sachs without a page (endnote, p. 334). (l) "Theorie sociologique de l’impôt" lacks its accent and the publisher appears as CEVPEN (endnote, p. 335), probably SEVPEN (outside knowledge, not certain). None of these affects the argument.

## Open questions

- How much causal weight does high-modernist ideology carry in collectivization, against the procurement crisis and the incentive problem created by a delivery price one-fifth of market (p. 165)? Would a non-high-modernist authoritarian regime with the same grain needs have done much the same?
- Does the American record after the 1930s, which this chapter does not treat, support or undercut the verdict that industrial farms showed no efficiency advantage (pp. 154-155)?
- Is the wheat/raspberry split better explained by crop characteristics (p. 173) or by the fact that private plots let peasants keep their own product (p. 168)? The two explanations predict the same pattern from Soviet data.
- If legibility, appropriation and centralization can diverge (the private property row of the table, p. 172), which of the three is doing the explanatory work in the table?
- Is miniaturization of order (p. 153) a cause of failure (planners mistaking models for reality) or a consolation that follows failure (a facade covering chaos, as Stites suggests)? The chapter states both readings.
- How reliable is the death-toll range (p. 158), given that the endnote gives no source for it (endnote, p. 331)?
