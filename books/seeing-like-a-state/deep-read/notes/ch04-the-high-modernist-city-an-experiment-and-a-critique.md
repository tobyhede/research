# ch04: The High-Modernist City: An Experiment and a Critique (pp. 83–115)

## Question this chapter answers

What does high modernism look like when it is applied to the design of a whole city, what happens when such a city is actually built, and what is the best available critique of it?

The chapter has three parts: Le Corbusier's doctrine as the "textbook case" (pp. 83-94), Brasilia and Chandigarh as the doctrine built (pp. 94-103), and Jane Jacobs as the critic (pp. 103-115).

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High-modernist urbanism | p. 83 | The huge, machine-age, hierarchical, centralized city designed as a single composition on a cleared site. Le Corbusier is its "embodiment"; his views are called extreme but representative of the logic implicit in high modernism (pp. 83-84). |
| Bird's-eye view / view from above | p. 85 | The vantage from which the planner's order exists. The disorder Le Corbusier wanted to correct was a function of distance, not of ground level. Contrasted later with Jacobs's street-level view (p. 103). |
| Formal (geometric) order | p. 85 | Straight lines, right angles, repetition. For Le Corbusier a precondition of efficiency; for Scott an aesthetic ideology presented as science (p. 86). |
| Standardization | p. 86 | Uniform building elements and measures, to be legislated internationally (p. 87). One of the steps "in the direction of simplification" (p. 87). |
| Functional segregation / functional separation | p. 87 | One function per district and per facility: work, residence, commerce, traffic and monuments kept apart. Scott calls it perhaps the most decisive simplifying step, because single-purpose planning needs a single value to maximize (p. 88). |
| The death of the street | p. 87 | Le Corbusier's second principle: complete separation of pedestrians from vehicles. In Brasilia it means the elimination of the street and square as places of public life (p. 96). |
| The Plan (as dictator / despot) | p. 88 | The single rational plan, capitalized by Le Corbusier, whose authority overrides elected bodies, voters, constitution and law (p. 90). |
| Technocracy | p. 90 | The belief that the human problem of urban design has a unique solution which an expert can discover and execute, so that politics and bargaining can only produce the wrong answer. |
| Legible | p. 86, p. 93 | First used of the grid that can be grasped at a glance and repeated; then of the geometric city that eases police work. On p. 100 Scott notes that what is legible to administrators can be mystifying disorder to residents. |
| Abstract, schematic subject | p. 92 | The simplified human for whom needs are stipulated in square meters, air, light and services. The planner sets the needs; the subject is not consulted (pp. 92-93). |
| Superquadra | p. 98 | Brasilia's residential unit: a group of apartment blocks with its own nursery and school, meant to foster collective life. |
| Public visiting room | p. 96 | Holston's phrase for the traditional Brazilian square: an enclosed, flexible space open to all classes and uses. |
| Brasilite | p. 99 | Residents' mock-clinical word for the trauma of daily life without street sociability in Brasilia. |
| Unplanned Brasilia | p. 101 | The squatter settlements of the poor and the private enclaves of the rich that grew round the planned core, and on which the core depended. |
| Utopian miniaturization | p. 102 | Building a model city from zero when transforming the whole nation is out of reach. |
| Visual order versus functional (experienced) order | pp. 103-104 | Jacobs's distinction: the tidy look of geometric regularity is no evidence that a system works; the order of a thing is fixed by the purpose it serves. |
| Eyes on the street | p. 105 | Informal surveillance by shopkeepers, passers-by and residents that keeps the public peace. |
| Sidewalk terms | p. 106 | Casual public acquaintance without private commitment, which carries small public services. |
| Mixed primary uses / cross-use | p. 107 | The mingling of residence, commerce and work in one district; for Jacobs the most vital condition of diversity. |
| Taxidermy (urban) | p. 109 | Jacobs's word for the result of confusing art with life: a planned city is a stuffed specimen. Echoes "social taxidermy" in ch03. |
| Unslumming | p. 110 | The capacity of a stable slum, given time and credit, to improve itself. |
| Social capital | p. 113 | Jacobs's term for the neighbourhood networks built by a continuity of residents; lost capital returns only slowly. |
| Thin city / thick city | p. 113 | A city planned from zero is a thin version of a city with its own history; only time and residents' work make it thick. |

## Leading propositions

- Le Corbusier matters as an exemplar, not for what he built: his influence was out of proportion to his structures, and he shows the key elements of high-modernist planning in exaggerated form (pp. 83-84, 94).
- His plans make no compromise with the existing city and no reference to the history or tastes of the place; they are designed to be seen from a great distance (p. 84).
- His scorn for the existing city was partly functional and partly aesthetic, and the aesthetic motive was at least as strong (p. 85).
- The preference for straight lines and right angles cannot be justified by the machine, by science or by nature; it is an aesthetic ideology (p. 86). Scott adds that such geometry was rarely cost effective (p. 86).
- Functional segregation is the most decisive simplification, and its logic is the planner's convenience: one purpose per zone makes planning and comparison possible (pp. 87-88).
- Efficiency criteria that are reasonable for a road do violence when applied to a home, whose uses are many and resist reduction (p. 88).
- Le Corbusier's doctrine is hierarchical and authoritarian in every direction: plan over city, centre over periphery, city over provinces, planner over politics (pp. 89-90).
- He knew his planning required authoritarian measures and sought a patron of any political colour who would give him a free hand (pp. 90-91).
- His city plans for stipulated needs of an abstract subject and never admits that residents might have something to say or that their needs might be plural (pp. 92-93).
- A legible, geometric city also serves police control; Le Corbusier understood this as Haussmann had (p. 93).
- Brasilia is the nearest thing to a built high-modernist city, and the conditions were as favourable as could be: empty site, state-owned land, single administrative function (pp. 94-95).
- Brasilia was meant to negate the old Brazil, and what it negated above all was the street and the square as public life (p. 96).
- The plan designed out the unauthorized places where casual encounters and crowds could occur (p. 98).
- Brasilia stands to São Paulo or Rio as scientific forestry stands to the unplanned forest (p. 98).
- Order and legibility for administrators produced disorientation for residents (p. 100).
- By 1980, 75 percent of Brasilia's population lived in settlements never anticipated by the plan; the planned centre and the unplanned margins were symbiotic (pp. 101-102).
- The poor gained their foothold because a reasonably competitive political system let squatters mobilize and be heard (p. 101).
- Jacobs's critique is the most carefully observed one, and its strength comes from its street-level, outsider perspective (p. 103).
- Planners' basic error was an aesthetic view of order, which led to the further error of rigid segregation of functions (p. 104).
- Urban public order is kept by nonspecialists through daily practice; formal institutions of order work only when this informal life underlies them (pp. 105-106).
- Scott's own extension: a diverse neighbourhood is, like an old-growth forest, more resilient and durable than a single-purpose district (p. 108).
- A "woman’s eye" was essential to Jacobs's frame of reference, because she saw activities that have many purposes or no single conscious purpose (pp. 108-109).
- Once comprehensive planning is wanted, uniformity and regimentation follow almost inexorably, because every concession to diversity costs administrative time and money (p. 111).
- Single-use zoning lets planners turn formulas into self-fulfilling prophecies (p. 111).
- A city develops as a language evolves: no one designs it, some weigh in more heavily than others, and its openness lets it serve purposes not yet conceived (p. 113).
- Jacobs is neither a free-market libertarian nor a Burkean conservative (pp. 112, 114).
- The two flaws of hubris in high-modernist planning are the presumption of reliable prediction and the presumption of knowing how to create a community (p. 114).
- It is no coincidence that the high-modernist cities actually built have mostly been administrative capitals, where the state can virtually stipulate the success of its grid (p. 115).

## Arguments

**A. Le Corbusier's geometry is taste, not science (pp. 85-86)**

- **Premises**: (p. 86) The machines he admired have rounder shapes than right angles. (p. 86) Any shape is geometrical, and a circle or sphere encloses most space for least surface. (p. 86) The logic of living forms is complex and has only recently been understood. (p. 86) Straight avenues over irregular ground are ruinously expensive.
- **Unstated premise**: that if the science-and-machine justification fails, what remains is only taste. Scott himself supplies a third motive in the same paragraph, the grid as a way of mastering space that is graspable and repeatable (p. 86), which is administrative, not aesthetic. The "aesthetic ideology" verdict therefore undersells his own point.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 86) Le Corbusier was expressing "no more, and no less, than an aesthetic ideology".

**B. Why functional segregation follows from planning from above (pp. 87-88, 110-111)**

- **Premises**: (p. 88) Planning is far easier when a zone or facility has one purpose. (p. 88) With many purposes the variables become unmanageable. (p. 88) Efficient planning for large populations requires that the values maximized be sharply specified and few. (pp. 110-111) It is a short step from simplified assumptions to shaping the environment so that it satisfies them.
- **Unstated premises**: that the planner's cognitive limit is the operative cause, and not, for example, the public-health case for separating housing from noxious industry, which the chapter does not discuss. Also that planning from above must be optimizing; a planner who sets rules and leaves outcomes open (Jacobs's own planner, p. 112) escapes the argument.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 88) Le Corbusier's doctrine delineates urban space by use so that single-purpose planning and standardization become possible; (p. 111) once comprehensive planning is desired the logic of uniformity is "well-nigh inexorable".

**C. The doctrine is authoritarian (pp. 88-91)**

- **Premises**: (p. 90) The plan is said to express universal scientific truth and to have a unique solution. (p. 90) Then politics and bargaining can only yield the wrong solution. (p. 90) The plan is drawn up away from the electorate and ignores the constitution in force. (pp. 90-91) Le Corbusier sought a patron with a free hand, in the USSR, Vichy, and India.
- **Unstated premise**: that the rhetoric of a planner without power tells us how planners with power behave. The premise is tested only by Brasilia and Chandigarh, neither of which he controlled politically.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 90) "we are in the presence of a dictatorship of the planner".

**D. Brasilia as the test (pp. 94-102)**

- **Premises**: (p. 95) Brasilia was built along CIAM lines under the most favourable conditions. (pp. 96-98) It eliminated street, square and informal public space. (pp. 99-100) Residents report anonymity, sameness and isolation. (p. 101) Three quarters of the population ended up outside the plan.
- **Unstated premises**: that what residents missed is a cost of the doctrine and not of newness or of an administrative monoculture; Scott concedes the first in part (p. 99, the inhabitants have not had much time). That Holston's account is representative: nearly all the Brasilia evidence comes from this one book (p. 95). That the unplanned periphery was required by the planned centre; this is asserted with "one could say" (p. 101) and not argued. An alternative reading is that the plan simply failed to house its builders.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 102) Success was considerable if measured by departure from older urban Brazil, minimal if measured by power to transform Brazil or to inspire love of the new way of life.

**E. Jacobs: visual order is not functional order (pp. 103-108)**

- **Premises**: (p. 104) Complex systems do not display surface regularity; their order lies deeper. (p. 105) Street safety comes from informal surveillance, which needs continual mixed use. (p. 107) Mixed uses, short blocks and buildings of varied age produce the diversity that draws people. (p. 108) Apparent disorder is in fact a developed form of order.
- **Unstated premises**: that the neighbourhoods Jacobs observed (Boston's North End, her Manhattan street) generalize. That the people on the street share a basic community feeling; Scott grants this in an endnote, noting that hostile eyes do not enhance security (endnote, p. 316). That residents' revealed activity is a sufficient standard (p. 112), which sets aside what people cannot afford or cannot choose.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 112) Planning should make room for the unofficial plans of many people and can at best modestly enhance, not impede, urban complexity.

**F. Why planned cities are thin (pp. 112-115)**

- **Premises**: (p. 112) City diversity is the unplanned creation of many hands over a long time. (p. 113) No individual or committee can encompass the present and future purposes of residents. (p. 114) Prediction of the things plans depend on is poor or nil. (p. 114) We know little about how satisfactory communities are fostered.
- **Unstated premise**: that the knowledge needed is in principle not collectable, not merely uncollected. This is the knowledge argument Scott elsewhere distinguishes from Hayek's; here he flags the kinship and the difference only in an endnote (endnote, pp. 317-318).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 113) A city planned from zero "would necessarily be a thin and pale version of a complex city with its own history".

## Evidence offered

- **Le Corbusier's own words**, chiefly The Radiant City (1933, republished 1964), which Scott says the analysis draws on heavily (p. 84; endnote, p. 310). Several key quotations come second-hand through Fishman's Urban Utopias and Cohen's Le Corbusier and the Mystique of the USSR (endnotes, pp. 310-313). Three unbuilt designs for Paris, Buenos Aires and Rio (p. 84); the CIAM manifesto of 1928 and the 1929 report (p. 87); the Van Nelle factory passage, in Scott's own translation (p. 89; endnote, p. 311); the recycling of the Moscow plan as La ville radieuse (p. 91); El Lissitzky's attack on a "city of nowhere" (p. 91).
- **Brasilia**: James Holston's The Modernist City is the source for nearly everything (p. 95; endnotes, pp. 314-315). Figures: the superquadra of roughly 360 apartments (p. 98); 25 square meters of green space per resident (p. 98); a workforce of more than sixty thousand (p. 100); 75 percent living outside the plan by 1980 and the planned city at less than half of a projected 557,000 (p. 101). Ethnographic items: the term brasilite (p. 99); the nine-year-olds who all drew a freestanding house (p. 100), which an endnote admits could be a mere representational convention (endnote, p. 315). Paired photographs of older Brazilian streets and squares against Brasilia (pp. 97-98, 101); the images themselves are not in the text.
- **Chandigarh**: brief, from Kalia and three essays in Walden's The Open Hand (endnote, p. 315). One observer's description of the empty chowk, from Sarin (pp. 102-103).
- **Jacobs**: The Death and Life of Great American Cities, quoted and paraphrased throughout (pp. 103-115). Her cases are anecdotal and local: the East Harlem lawn (pp. 103-104), Boston's North End (p. 105), the attempted abduction on her street (p. 106), the deli owner's key drawer (p. 106), the Wall Street restaurant (pp. 107-108), the Louisville arts centre (p. 115).
- **Scott's own analogies** carry part of the argument where evidence is thin: the forest (pp. 88, 98, 108, 110), the army on parade versus in combat (pp. 104-105), prison uniforms (p. 111), language and Esperanto (p. 113), the tailor who trims the customer (p. 115).
- **Qualifications placed in endnotes**: Le Corbusier's actual buildings were more varied and more interesting than his doctrine, and neither cheap nor efficient (endnotes, p. 311); his smaller projects succeeded better than his grand ones (endnote, p. 314); residents like some things about Brasilia, including its safety for children and high standard of living (endnotes, pp. 314-315); Lynch's imageability, order as pictured by inhabitants, may be negatively correlated with a planner's legibility (endnote, p. 315); the term fascism is defended with a Parthenon passage (endnote, p. 312); Christaller's service to the Nazi regime and later the Communist Party is given as a parallel search for an autocrat (endnotes, pp. 311-312).
- **Perspective of the sources**: the doctrine is reported from its author's manifestos, the built cities from critics (Holston, Sarin), and the alternative from its advocate. No planner's defence of Brasilia or of zoning is heard, and no resident survey beyond Holston's informants.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **The three-element model against the cases.** ch03 says catastrophe needs high-modernist ideology, unrestrained state power and a prostrate civil society (ch03, p. 71). Le Corbusier had the ideology and no power; most of his schemes were never built (p. 83). Brasilia was built by an elected populist president in a "reasonably competitive political system" (p. 101), and that system is exactly what let the squatters win. So the chapter's one large built case is not an authoritarian one, and its outcome confirms ch03's claim about representative institutions as a barrier (ch03, pp. 82-83) more than it shows a catastrophe. The harm claimed is anomie and monotony, not disaster. Scott does not draw attention to this difference of degree.
- **Stretching "high modernism".** The closing list of high-modernist cities includes Saint Petersburg and Canberra (p. 115). ch03 dates high modernism from the nineteenth century and its strong form from World War I (ch03, pp. 72, 79). A city founded by Peter the Great fits the baroque planning of the Mumford epigraph (p. 83), not the ch03 definition. The term here slides toward any state-built capital on a cleared site.
- **Legibility changes sides.** In ch01 and ch02 legibility is what the state gains. Here Scott states the corollary for the first time in a built setting: legibility to the centre can mean illegibility to the resident (p. 100; endnote, p. 315). This prepares the later contrast between formal schemes and practical knowledge. The word metis does not appear in this chapter; "practical knowledge" appears only inside the Holston quotation (p. 100).
- **Forestry analogy.** ch01's scientific forest is invoked four times (pp. 88, 98, 108, 110). The resilience claim for diverse neighbourhoods (p. 108) rests on this analogy and on "virtually by definition", not on urban evidence. Incomplete.
- **Haussmann.** ch02's retrofitted Paris returns as the precedent that Le Corbusier wanted to outdo (p. 93); both are also credited with an emancipating vision (p. 98).
- **Taxidermy.** Jacobs's word (p. 109) is the source of, or at least matches, the "social taxidermy" of ch03 (ch03, p. 75).
- **Scott's distance from Jacobs and Hayek.** The body text grants her critics "some justice" (p. 114) and says she is no libertarian (p. 112). The endnotes go further: she naturalizes the unplanned city by treating it as the sum of notionally equal small acts, when commercial and speculative interests are reshaping it daily (endnote, p. 317), and the market was itself imposed by a coercive state, citing Polanyi (endnotes, pp. 317-318). This is the same reservation as in the Introduction about the market as a simplifier (fm01, p. 12). The body of the chapter leans on Jacobs much more heavily than these reservations would suggest.
- **Concessions that are not weighed.** Le Corbusier's city would have been "far more healthful and functional" than the slums he attacked (p. 92); Brasilia was rational, healthy and rather egalitarian (p. 98) and avoided land speculation and rent gouging (p. 98). As in ch03 (ch03, p. 78), the gains are stated and then left out of the final account.
- **Sets up ch05.** The Le Corbusier and Jacobs pairing is reused as Lenin against Luxemburg and Kollontay (p. 115).
- **Details that look wrong or inconsistent in the text.** (a) The same Le Corbusier sentence is quoted twice in different wording, "refuse to afford even the slightest concession" (p. 85) and "refuse even the slightest consideration" (p. 94), with the follow-on sentence ending "there" in one and "here" in the other; the endnotes give Fishman for the passage on p. 85 and The Radiant City, p. 121, for p. 94 (endnotes, pp. 310, 314). (b) Brasilia is placed "1620 kilometers from the Pacific Ocean in the northeast" (p. 95); outside knowledge: Brazil has no Pacific coast, so this is presumably the Atlantic. (c) The 1929 CIAM report is said to be by Le Corbusier "and his brother Pierre" (p. 87); outside knowledge: Pierre Jeanneret was his cousin. (d) An endnote cites Jacobs, Death and Life, p. 737 (endnote, p. 317); outside knowledge: the book is far shorter, so the page number is likely a typo. None of these affects the argument.

## Open questions

- How much of Brasilia's failure is high modernism and how much is the single-function administrative capital? Scott says the administrative character simplified planning (p. 95) and that such capitals are where the state can stipulate success (p. 115), which makes them the easy case for the planner and a hard case from which to generalize to ordinary cities.
- Is the "unplanned Brasilia" a refutation of planning or a failure of this plan to include its own workforce? What would a plan that housed the candangos have looked like?
- Jacobs's planner still plans: short blocks, mixed uses, no large depots (p. 111). What separates acceptable rule-setting from high-modernist design, other than modesty? The chapter gives a direction (foreclose as few options as possible, p. 115) but no criterion.
- If the unplanned city is shaped by developers and financiers, as Scott's endnotes insist (endnote, p. 317), who protects the street-level order Jacobs describes, and with what power?
- The gender claim (pp. 108-109) is offered as Scott's impression, supported by one Jacobs passage (endnotes, pp. 316-317). Is it doing argumentative work or is it a gloss on the many-purposes point?
- Does the thin-to-thick claim (p. 113) imply that planned cities recover with time? If so, the critique is of the first generation's experience, and a 1980s snapshot of Brasilia cannot settle it.
