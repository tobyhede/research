# ch09: Thin Simplifications and Practical Knowledge: Mētis (pp. 242–271)

## Question this chapter answers

What is the practical knowledge that high-modernist schemes ignore or suppress, how is it created and kept alive, how does it relate to formal, codified knowledge, and why do states and large firms work to destroy it (p. 244)? Scott's answer: formal order is always "parasitic" on informal processes (p. 243); the knowledge that sustains those processes is mētis, a local, practical, experiential and plastic know-how that resists codification (pp. 245-251); it is denigrated partly because its form does not fit scientific discourse (p. 254) and partly because its destruction serves "control and appropriation" (p. 266). The danger is not epistemic knowledge or state simplification as such, but the combination of epistemic universalism with authoritarian social engineering (p. 269).

The chapter opens Part 4, "The Missing Link" (p. 242). It is the book's conceptual core: the term was flagged in the introduction (fm01, p. 11) and given a two-sentence definition in ch05 (p. 142), and this chapter is the promised full treatment.

The unit runs: Tolstoy epigraph and recapitulation of forest, city, revolution and collectivization (pp. 242-243); formal order parasitic on informal process, the work-to-rule strike and Caterpillar (p. 243); aims (p. 244); "Mētis: The Contours of Practical Knowledge": Squanto and the almanac, Quetelet, the term, Odysseus, physical, emergency and interactive skills, Peirce, preliminary generalizations (pp. 244-248); "The Art of the Locality": Oakeshott, piloting, Twain, the loom, partisan knowledge, grammar and speech (pp. 248-251); "The Relation with Episteme and Techne": techne as ideal type, actual science, Plato, Descartes, Bentham, risk and uncertainty, economics, Aristotle and Socrates (pp. 251-254); "Practical Knowledge Versus Scientific Explanation": vernacular measures, flora, practical success, bricolage, why cultivators observe, prescientific discoveries, variolation, Howard's Japanese erosion control, Lindblom and Hirschman (pp. 254-259); "Learning Beyond the Book": the syphilis doctor, Bugis captains, diphtheria, maple syrup, Bugis tides (pp. 259-262); "The Dynamism and Plasticity of Mētis": maize in West Africa, the dark twin, Oakeshott on tradition, oral cultures, language (pp. 262-263); "The Social Context of Mētis and Its Destruction": Mat Isa's ants, the veillée, two ironies, welcome losses, destruction as project, Marglin, Taylor, Noble, Kusterer, plantations and contract farming (pp. 263-269); "The Case Against Imperial Knowledge" (pp. 269-271).

Only the ch09 portion of p. 242 (after ch08's closing sentences) and of p. 271 (before ch10 begins) is covered. Endnotes are on pp. 358-367 (ch09 notes 1-90).

## Leading propositions

- Thin, formulaic simplifications imposed by state power failed in forests, planned cities, the revolution and collectivization (pp. 242-243); the schemes that survived did so through measures the plan did not foresee or prohibited (p. 243).
- Formal order is always parasitic on informal processes that it does not recognize and cannot create (p. 243). Scott calls this "less an analytical insight than a sociological truism" (p. 243).
- The work-to-rule strike shows that actual work depends more on informal understandings than on formal rules (p. 243); Caterpillar workers reverted to the engineers' inefficient procedures (p. 243).
- Authoritarian high-modernist schemes are destructive because they ignore and suppress the practical skills that underwrite complex activity (p. 244).
- High modernism replaced a collaboration between practical and formal knowledge with an imperial scientific view (p. 244), and the relation between the two is a political struggle for institutional hegemony (p. 244). Taylorism and scientific agriculture are strategies of control and appropriation (p. 244).
- Squanto's oak-leaf maxim is local but travels anywhere in temperate North America with oaks and squirrels (p. 245); it probably relied on many redundant signals (p. 244); the almanac's fixed date needs adjustment by latitude and altitude and is commercially cautious (pp. 244-245).
- Practical knowledge is as economical and accurate as it needs to be for the problem at hand; Quetelet's lilac formula is knowledge but not practical (p. 245).
- Mētis is a wide array of practical skills for adapting to changing environments; Odysseus's mētis included holding his men together and repairing his ship, not only deception (pp. 245-246).
- Skills such as sailing or riding a bicycle are hard to teach apart from the activity, hence apprenticeship (p. 246); you would prefer an experienced captain to a physicist who has never sailed (p. 246).
- Emergency specialists exemplify mētis: each fire is unique and half the battle is knowing which rule to apply and when to improvise (pp. 246-247). Red Adair and a routine clerk sit at opposite ends of a spectrum (p. 247).
- Mētis applies equally to human interaction: boxing, dancing, team sports, war, diplomacy and politics (p. 247).
- Peirce's weight experiment shows knowledge acquired only by practice that defies verbal communication (p. 248).
- Mētis fits similar but never identical situations, resists deduction and book learning, and lies between genius and rote (p. 248).
- Oakeshott: written rules are abridgements of an activity; mastery of principles may coexist with complete inability to perform (p. 248).
- Mētis matters most in settings that are mutable, indeterminate and particular (p. 249). The local pilot's knowledge prevails over the captain's navigation (p. 249); river pilots, trackers and guides are the same (pp. 249-250).
- Every application of general knowledge needs imaginative translation; it is not preposterous to speak of "the art of one loom" (p. 250).
- Situated knowledge is partisan: the sailor cares about one voyage, not the probability distribution (p. 250).
- All the state schemes of earlier chapters concern spatially and temporally unique settings, and generic rules applied mechanically invite failure (p. 250). The rules of thumb themselves are codifications derived from practice (p. 251).
- Mētis relates to rules as speech to grammar (p. 251). Learned by daily practice, it may favour conservation over daring innovation (p. 251).
- For Plato, techne and episteme differ completely from mētis (p. 251): techne is universal, decomposable, verifiable, teachable, impersonal (pp. 251-252).
- Techne describes science's self-image, not its practice; no rules of techne explain invention, which needs genius "and perhaps mētis" (p. 252).
- Western philosophy and social science have tried to bracket uncertainty to gain Euclidean rigor (p. 253): Descartes, Bentham (p. 253), probability theory (p. 253), GNP and polls (p. 253), and neoclassical economics, which takes preferences as given and exiles invention and genuine uncertainty (pp. 253-254).
- Like scientific agriculture, such disciplines gain precision by narrowing their field, at the cost of surprises from outside (p. 254). Techne suits activities with a single, separately specifiable, measurable end (p. 254).
- Aristotle held that some practical choices cannot be captured in universal rules, naming navigation and medicine (p. 254); Socrates refused to write because philosophy belonged to mētis (p. 254).
- Mētis is denigrated because its findings are practical and contextual, not integrated into scientific discourse (p. 254). Vernacular measures were mētis, and their local variation made mētis unassimilable for statecraft (pp. 254-255).
- The test of mētis is practical success, not causal explanation (p. 255). Until recently virtually all agricultural improvements came from the field (p. 255); mētis innovates by bricolage, not invention from scratch (p. 255).
- Cultivators observe closely because they consume their own conclusions (p. 255), because poverty forces experiment (pp. 255-256), because they live in the field of observation, and because their community is a living library (p. 256).
- Prescientific peoples discovered cinchona, rhubarb against scurvy, and bread mould, and fled or sealed themselves against plague, without knowing the mechanisms (p. 256). Gupta: about three-quarters of the modern pharmacopoeia derive from traditional medicines (p. 256).
- Variolation was practised across four regions by the sixteenth century and anticipated Jenner's vaccination (pp. 256-257). Its ingredients were need, promising leads, many freelance experimenters, time, and shared results (p. 257).
- Mētis is not a superseded precursor of science but the most appropriate mode of reasoning under daunting uncertainty (p. 258). Howard's Japanese erosion control is "an art of one valley" (p. 258); any task with many interacting, unforecastable variables belongs here, and social tasks multiply the uncertainty (p. 258).
- Lindblom and Hirschman amount to a strategic retreat from comprehensive planning, and bounded rationality and satisficing resemble mētis (pp. 258-259).
- Breaking a task into steps does not let a novice take charge; only experience can read the result of each step (p. 259).
- Mētis is often so implicit its bearer cannot explain it: the syphilis doctor's eye-tremor cue (p. 259), Bugis captains waking at a change of swell (p. 260), the smell of diphtheria (p. 260), the grazier's glance (p. 260).
- Most such judgments can be checked by tests, but the epistemic alternative is slower, costlier and not always decisive, and mētis decides which tests to run (pp. 260-261).
- Rules of thumb are the codification of experience and require having made the mistake once (p. 261).
- Bugis tide forecasting uses the monsoon as a proxy for the moon's declination and is learned orally (p. 261).
- "Traditional" is a misnomer (p. 262): West Africans had ritualized maize within two generations (p. 262), and people readily adopt useful goods (p. 262).
- High modernism needed this dark twin as a rhetorical foil (p. 262), and institutions made their way by denigrating mētis (p. 262).
- Mētis is plastic, local and divergent, with no doctrine, and its market is near perfect competition (p. 262). Oakeshott: practical knowledge is "preeminently fluid" (p. 263). Oral cultures drift without seeming to (p. 263). The best analogy for a society's stock of mētis is its language (p. 263).
- Mat Isa's use of black ants against red ants is a field experiment in biological control that no extension official could have devised (pp. 263-264); it presupposes lifelong observation in a stable, multigenerational community (p. 264).
- Mētis needs a community of interest, accumulated information and ongoing experimentation (p. 264); the veillée was a daily seminar on practical knowledge (pp. 264-265).
- First irony: mētis is not democratically distributed and may be monopolized (p. 265). Second irony: some mētis depends on preindustrial conditions, and communities marginal to markets and the state keep most (p. 265).
- Many losses of mētis can be welcomed: fire-making, washboards, darning, steering without charts (p. 265). Standardized knowledge democratizes skills (p. 265).
- But the destruction of mētis is also a project inscribed in the state and bureaucratic capitalism (pp. 265-266), whose logic is control and appropriation, because dispersed local knowledge is unappropriable (p. 266).
- De-skilling in agriculture is more compelling and less successful than in factories (p. 266).
- Marglin: profit requires efficiency combined with control (p. 266); the factory's advantage was control of hours, intensity and materials (p. 266).
- Taylor explicitly aimed to gather workmen's traditional knowledge into management's rules (pp. 266-267).
- Numerical control failed because it designed out mētis (p. 267); Kusterer shows "unskilled" workers still need individual skills (p. 267).
- Smallholder agriculture may be most efficient but is illegible and hard to tax (pp. 267-268). A state concerned with appropriation prefers sedentary to mobile, large to small, and plantation or collective farms to both (p. 268).
- Plantations were private collectivization relying on state sanctions (p. 268). Contract farming replicates plantation control without its costs (pp. 268-269), transforming farms to fit the contract grid (p. 269); contract farmers have little more control than assembly-line workers (p. 269).
- The case is not against high modernism, state simplification or epistemic knowledge per se (p. 269); citizenship, public health, social security and early Bolshevik and Chinese land reforms depend on such simplifications (p. 269).
- The danger is the combination of universalist epistemic pretension and authoritarian social engineering (p. 269), implicit in scientific agriculture and explicit in its colonial practice (pp. 269-270).
- Universalism seems inherent in rationalist knowledge (p. 270); mētis is pluralistic (p. 270). Democratic and commercial pressures, Meiji technical teams, and resistance at Brasilia and in ujamaa can check the imperialism, but from outside the paradigm (p. 270).
- The danger grows when leaders treat people as a blank page, as with Mao and Owen (p. 270).
- Against Oakeshott, Scott says high modernism appeals to people with good reason to hold the past in contempt (p. 270); understanding this does not excuse the damage done with authoritarian power (pp. 270-271).

## Arguments

**A. Formal order is parasitic on informal practice (pp. 242-244)**

- **Premises**: (p. 242) Scientific forests needed restoration and planned cities survived through illegal improvisation. (p. 242) The revolution relied on luck and improvisation. (p. 243) Collective farms survived on private plots and theft. (p. 243) Work-to-rule halts production. (p. 243) Caterpillar workers' own procedures beat the engineers'.
- **Unstated premises**: that the informal processes on which formal order depends are kinds of knowledge (mētis), rather than also kinds of evasion, theft and coercion. The Soviet informal economy is "theft" (p. 243) and the Tanzanian case is flight (p. 243); these show that formal plans fail, not that the informal side is skilled. The chapter later narrows to skill. That the work-to-rule case generalizes from organizations with strong unions and well-specified rules to whole societies. Scott calls the claim a truism (p. 243), so its load-bearing role is not as a finding but as a premise for the claim that suppression of mētis is destructive (p. 244).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 243) "Formal order, to be more explicit, is always and to some considerable degree parasitic on informal processes, which the formal scheme does not recognize, without which it could not exist, and which it alone cannot create or maintain."

**B. What mētis is: practical, local, implicit (pp. 244-251)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 244-245) Squanto's maxim beats the almanac on growing time while staying safe. (p. 245) Quetelet's formula is accurate but impractical. (pp. 245-246) Odysseus's mētis spans deception and seamanship. (p. 246) Physical skills are learned only by practice. (pp. 246-247) Emergencies are unique. (p. 247) Interactive skills need responsiveness. (p. 248) Peirce's subjects learned to discriminate without knowing how. (p. 248) Oakeshott: principles abridge practice. (p. 249) Pilots know one harbour. (p. 250) Every loom differs. (p. 251) Grammar derives from speech.
- **Unstated premises**: that the examples share one property. They mix at least four things: bodily skill (bicycle, Peirce), situated factual knowledge (the pilot's sandbars), strategic cunning (boxer's feint, generals), and accumulated collective lore (Squanto, Bugis tides). The pilot's knowledge of reefs and tides (p. 249) is explicit and could in principle be charted; it is local, not tacit. Scott's list of definitions slides between "local" (p. 250), "implicit" (p. 248), and "practical" (p. 255) as if they coincided. Squanto is a reconstruction: the attribution is a legend with two versions (p. 244), and Scott says "We almost certainly distort" the advice and supplies the redundant signals from what "we know about indigenous technical knowledge" (p. 244). Endnote 6 concedes settlers quickly built comparable rules of thumb (endnote, p. 359), which weakens the vernacular-versus-universal contrast: the almanac's caution is commercial (p. 245), not a flaw of abstraction. Endnote 7 notes Quetelet's formula starts from a last frost known only in retrospect (endnote, p. 359), so it fails as practical guidance for a reason internal to it. The grammar analogy (p. 251) is strong for native language learning but Scott himself grants modern language teaching introduces rules later to codify practice (p. 251), which is a collaboration, not an opposition.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 249) "Knowing how and when to apply the rules of thumb in a concrete situation is the essence of mētis."

**C. Techne is an ideal type, and the effort to extend it narrows inquiry (pp. 251-254)**

- **Premises**: (p. 251) Plato set techne and episteme against mētis. (pp. 251-252) Techne is universal, decomposable, verifiable and teachable. (p. 252) It describes science's self-image, not its practice of discovery. (p. 252) Plato tried to turn love into techne (Nussbaum). (p. 253) Descartes, Bentham, probability and GNP each narrowed their field to what could be measured. (pp. 253-254) Neoclassical economics brackets taste and exiles uncertainty (Marglin). (p. 254) Aristotle and Socrates recognized irreducibly practical domains.
- **Unstated premises**: that there is a single Greek contrast to borrow. Endnote 17 shows the terminology is contested: Scott takes Nussbaum's usage, where techne sits with episteme, while relying on Marglin's contrasts, where "techne" means roughly what Scott calls mētis (endnote, pp. 360-361). Scott's own Aristotle quotation derives techne from "many notions gained from experience" (p. 252), which makes techne grow out of experience rather than oppose it. Outside knowledge, not in the book: in Aristotle and in the Platonic dialogues, medicine and navigation are standard examples of techne, so presenting them as mētis-laden (p. 254) depends on Nussbaum's reading of practical wisdom, not on ordinary Greek usage. The Plato-on-love passage (p. 252) is an analogy for the purification drive, not evidence about states. The economics critique relies on Marglin's unpublished and forthcoming papers (endnotes, pp. 361-362). Endnote 22 calls Polanyi's Personal Knowledge "the most searching" treatment (p. 361) in an endnote, and endnote 56 aligns mētis with Ryle's knowing how (endnote, p. 364), so the concept's philosophical work is largely outsourced to those authors, unnamed in the body. Endnote 34 quotes Hayek as a fellow skeptic of "pretended knowledge" (endnote, p. 362).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 254) "Formulas of efficiency, production functions, and rational action are specifiable only when the ends sought are simple, sharply defined, and hence measurable."

**D. Practical success is a valid test, and mētis has produced real discoveries (pp. 254-259)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 254-255) Vernacular measures and folk plant classifications served local use. (p. 255) Practitioners judge by results. (p. 255) Most agricultural improvements came from the field. (pp. 255-256) Stakes, poverty, residence and community make cultivators good observers. (p. 256) Cinchona, rhubarb, bread mould and plague avoidance worked without theory. (pp. 256-257) Variolation anticipated vaccination. (p. 257) Its ingredients are ordinary: need, leads, many experimenters, time, sharing. (p. 258) Howard's Japanese engineers proceed by step and observation. (pp. 258-259) Lindblom and Hirschman describe policy the same way.
- **Unstated premises**: that the survival of a practice is evidence of efficacy, and that a pool of freelance experimenters filters out failures. Scott concedes the risk of false inferences (p. 258) and endnote 50 notes "thousands of competitors for cures" (p. 364) in an endnote, but does not say how the stochastic process separated variolation from the many useless remedies those competitors also sold. The examples are selected for success: discoveries later vindicated by science (quinine, vitamin C, penicillin) are evidence that mētis sometimes works, not of its general reliability. The pharmacopoeia figure rests on one conference paper (p. 256; endnote, p. 363); cannot verify. The claim that until recently virtually all agricultural improvement came from the field (p. 255) is asserted without a cite. Scott disclaims defending traditional medicine (p. 256). The Lindblom and Hirschman move (pp. 258-259) shifts mētis from craft knowledge held by practitioners to a policy method for planners, which is a significant extension: "muddling through" is a strategy available to experts, not a kind of local knowledge.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 258) "It is the mode of reasoning most appropriate to complex material and social tasks where the uncertainties are so daunting that we must trust our (experienced) intuition and feel our way."

**E. Mētis is implicit but checkable, and faster (pp. 259-262)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 259-260) The syphilis doctor, the Bugis captain, the diphtheria smell, the infant clinic, Howard's grazier. (p. 260) These judgments can be verified by tests. (pp. 260-261) Tests are slower, costlier and not always decisive. (p. 261) Rules of thumb are the residue of mistakes. (p. 261) Bugis tide reckoning is accurate and uses a proxy.
- **Unstated premises**: that verification by tests does not make episteme the final arbiter. Scott's own framing concedes that epistemic method checks mētis (p. 260), so the case for mētis here is about speed and cost, a pragmatic division of labour, not epistemic parity. The syphilis case ends with the cue "universally recognized" (pp. 259-260), that is, codified; it illustrates mētis as a source of science, not a rival. Several anecdotes are hearsay: "I have been told" (p. 259), "Another doctor once told me" (p. 260), and endnote 58 says "I don’t recall the name of the disease" (p. 365) in an endnote. The Bugis evidence is strong and documented (Ammarell's dissertation, endnotes, pp. 360, 365).
- **Conclusion**: (p. 261) "When rapid judgments of high (not perfect) accuracy are called for, when it is important to interpret early signs that things are going well or poorly, then there is no substitute for mētis."

**F. Mētis is plastic, not traditional, and its stigma is institutional (pp. 262-263)**

- **Premises**: (p. 262) West Africans adopted and ritualized maize within two generations. (p. 262) Variolation spread across four continents. (p. 262) Goods that solve problems are readily adopted. (p. 262) Development discourse needed a backward other. (p. 262) Institutions advanced by denigrating mētis. (pp. 262-263) Oakeshott: tradition is fluid. (p. 263) Oral cultures drift. (p. 263) Language changes from below.
- **Unstated premises**: that adoption of outside goods (matches, sewing machines, antibiotics, p. 262) shows mētis is plastic. It shows people adopt epistemic products; endnote 65 confines the claim to tools and leaves out identity-laden norms (endnote, p. 366). The near-perfect-competition metaphor (p. 262) assumes knowledge circulates freely, which the next section contradicts: mētis is monopolized by guilds, classes, men (p. 265). The institutional-interest claim (p. 262) cites Ferguson (endnote, p. 366) and repeats ch08's privilege argument without new evidence. The language analogy (p. 263) is the third use of language in the chapter (pp. 251, 263) and in the book (ch07's Esperanto); it carries much of the conceptual weight.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 262) "Mētis, far from being rigid and monolithic, is plastic, local, and divergent."

**G. Its destruction is partly welcome, partly a project of control (pp. 263-269)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 263-264) Mat Isa's ant war needed a stable, observant community. (p. 264) Mētis needs community, accumulated information, experiment. (p. 265) Mētis is monopolized and depends on preindustrial conditions. (p. 265) Many losses liberate. (pp. 265-266) But destruction is inscribed in state and capitalist logic, since local knowledge is unappropriable. (p. 266) Marglin: control matters as much as efficiency. (pp. 266-267) Taylor's own words. (p. 267) Noble's failed numerical control and Kusterer's skilled "unskilled" workers. (pp. 267-268) States prefer legible farming. (pp. 268-269) Contract farming standardizes farms to the contract.
- **Unstated premises**: that a line can be drawn between welcome losses (washboards, darning) and destructive ones (p. 265). Scott gives no criterion, and his own Mat Isa admission that he would have used a cheap pesticide (p. 265) puts the choice with the practitioner, which implies the test is voluntary versus imposed displacement. That is a liberal criterion Scott does not state. The control thesis is functionalist: "inscribed" (p. 266) and "logic" (p. 266) attribute purpose to structures. The Taylor quotation is direct evidence of intent for one actor (pp. 266-267); Marglin's argument that factories won by control rather than efficiency is a contested thesis in economic history (outside knowledge), presented here as "convincingly shown" (p. 266). Endnote 77 concedes the putting-out system had its own advantages for capitalists (endnote, p. 367), and Scott grants Ford plants were "remarkably efficient" (p. 267). The contract-farming case shows control without the state, as in ch08 (pp. 268-269). Endnote 71 concedes epistemic knowledge is also restricted, but calls its secrets "open secrets" (p. 366) in an endnote; endnote 74 calls many apprenticeships disguised indentured labour (endnote, p. 366). Both strengthen the case for codification that Scott is resisting.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 266) "The logic animating the project, however, is one of control and appropriation."

**H. The case against imperial knowledge, not against knowledge (pp. 269-271)**

- **Premises**: (p. 269) State simplifications underpin citizenship, public health, social security and enfranchising land reforms. (p. 269) Epistemic knowledge is never separate from mētis in practice. (p. 269) The dangerous cases combine universalist pretension with authoritarian engineering. (p. 270) Rationalism admits no other knowledge (Pascal via Oakeshott). (p. 270) Checks come from outside the paradigm. (p. 270) Mao's and Owen's blank page. (p. 270) Oakeshott misses why modernizers despise the past.
- **Unstated premises**: that the universalism is "inherent" in rationalist knowledge (p. 270), offered with "Although I am no philosopher of knowledge" (p. 270). This sits awkwardly with the concession that epistemic practice always involves mētis (p. 269) and with Howard, "himself a meticulous scientist" (p. 270), who recognized farming's art. If scientists can recognize mētis, the imperialism is a feature of a codified self-image and its institutions, not of knowledge as such, which is the distinction Scott drew on p. 252. The two-part combination here (p. 269) is narrower than ch03's four elements; the weak civil society element is absent, and "Lenin’s view of revolution (but not his practice)" (p. 269) preserves ch05's reading. The Oakeshott critique (p. 270; endnote, p. 360; endnote, p. 367) is the chapter's main defence against being read as Burkean conservatism.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 269) the danger "is the combination of the universalist pretensions of epistemic knowledge and authoritarian social engineering."

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **How mētis was introduced, and how it shifts here.** fm01 calls metis "the knowledge that can come only from practical experience" and a "portmanteau word" (p. 11), and notes the anarchists' "mutuality" covers some of the same ground (p. 11); the introduction spells it both "metis" and "mētis" in one paragraph (p. 11). ch05 gives the first working definition: "knowledge that can be acquired only by long practice at similar but rarely identical tasks" (p. 142), invoked to explain why revolution and new forms of production cannot be planned (p. 142). ch09 keeps the "similar but never identical" core (p. 248) but widens the term in at least five directions: bodily skill (p. 246), local situated knowledge (p. 249), partisan outcome-shaping (p. 250), a stochastic method of collective trial and error (pp. 256-258), and a society's stock of knowledge on the model of language (p. 263). It also makes mētis ubiquitous, present in factories, laboratories and hacking (endnotes, pp. 359, 366), and plural (p. 265). The ch05 use for revolution, a task novel by definition, sits uneasily with ch09's statement that mētis may favour "conservation of skills rather than daring innovation" (p. 251) and innovates by bricolage (p. 255). The chapter reconciles them only through the Lindblom and Hirschman move (pp. 258-259), where mētis becomes the incremental method appropriate to uncertainty.
- **Thin and thick, and the dark twin (ch07, ch08).** The title's "thin simplifications" picks up ch07's thin versus thick cities, villages and languages (ch07, pp. 201, 205). The general law of p. 243, formal order parasitic on informal process, is the abstract form of ch07's dark twin (ch07, p. 205) and ch08's "dark twin" of informal practice and landraces (ch08, p. 213). Yet when the phrase "dark twin" appears in ch09 (p. 262) it means something else: the rhetorical "other" of backwardness that high-modernist discourse needs. Across three chapters the term covers an unofficial reality that keeps the plan alive, a stock of past knowledge drawn on as a resource, and a stigmatized image. ch09 supplies the missing piece that links them: mētis is the content of what is "thick" and what the dark twin carries. The work-to-rule example is reused from ch07 (ch07, pp. 200-201), here with Caterpillar replacing Paris taxis (p. 243), and the language analogy (pp. 251, 263) continues ch07's Esperanto.
- **Back to legibility (ch01-ch02).** Mētis is tied to the book's first theme at three points: vernacular measures as mētis that was "unassimilable for purposes of statecraft" (p. 255), as in ch02, destruction of mētis as replacement by formulas "legible only from the center" (p. 266), and smallholding as illegible and hard to tax (pp. 267-268). The contract farming passage explicitly invokes scientific forestry: the goal is to change the environment so it is standardized to begin with (p. 269; ch01).
- **Back to ch08.** The polycropping cultivators (p. 258), farmers as experimenters with a community library (p. 256; ch08, pp. 240-241), and practice preceding theory (p. 242) are ch08's material theorized. ch08's endnote placing mētis in "chapter 8" (ch08, endnote, p. 355) points here.
- **Two explanations of the war on mētis.** Through most of the chapter the problem is epistemic: imperial knowledge cannot see mētis (pp. 244, 254, 270). In the destruction section it is interest: control and appropriation (pp. 244, 266). The conclusion combines universalist epistemic pretension with authoritarian power (p. 269). The opening of ch10, on the same page, says the planners' actions were "far from being cynical grabs for power and wealth" (p. 271). Scott does not reconcile the control thesis (firms and states destroy mētis to appropriate) with the tragic thesis (well-meaning planners blinded by faith); they may describe different actors, capitalists versus high-modernist intellectuals, but the chapter does not say so.
- **Science versus scientism, again.** As in ch08, Scott protects science: techne is an ideal type, not the practice of science (p. 252), and epistemic knowledge is "never separate in its practice from mētis" (p. 269). Yet he also says universalist claims "seem inherent" in rationalist knowledge (p. 270). The first claim locates the problem in the codified self-image and its institutions; the second locates it in knowledge itself. The first is better supported by his own examples (Howard, Meiji teams, the syphilis cue entering medicine).
- **Hayek (outside knowledge).** The chapter's core claim, that knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place is dispersed and cannot be centralized, closely parallels Hayek's "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945), which the book does not cite. Hayek appears in ch09 only in an endnote as a skeptic of "pretended knowledge" (endnote, p. 362), and in ch07 for the price system (ch07, p. 201). Scott's departure from Hayek is that he applies the argument to large capitalist firms (Taylorism, plantations, contract farming, pp. 266-269) as much as to the state, which extends ch08's market-without-state thread.
- **Oakeshott and conservatism.** Scott borrows Oakeshott's account of practical knowledge (pp. 248, 262-263, 270) while attacking his complacency about inherited privilege (endnote, p. 360; endnote, p. 367) and his blindness to why modernizers despise the past (p. 270). This is how the chapter separates its defence of mētis from a defence of tradition, together with the rejection of "traditional" (p. 262) and the admission that mētis is monopolized (p. 265).
- **Link to ch03.** The closing combination (p. 269) restates ch03's dangerous mix in two parts, universalist knowledge plus authoritarian social engineering. ch03's other elements, administrative ordering and a prostrate civil society, are not named here, although resistance at Brasilia and in ujamaa (p. 270) stands in for civil society.

## Open questions

- Is mētis one concept or a family? Bodily skill, local factual knowledge, cunning, collective lore and incremental method have different epistemic properties; which of them does each of Scott's arguments actually need?
- If mētis is defined by practice at "similar but never identical" situations (p. 248) and favours conservation over daring innovation (p. 251), can it be the knowledge that makes revolutions and new forms of production, as ch05 claimed (p. 142)?
- How does a stochastic process of many freelance experimenters (p. 257) separate working practices from surviving ones, given the risk of false inferences Scott admits (p. 258) and the thousands of useless remedies (endnote, p. 364)?
- Which losses of mētis are welcome and which destructive (p. 265)? Is the implicit criterion voluntary versus imposed displacement, and if so, how does it apply to markets that make mētis obsolete without coercion?
- Does the control-and-appropriation thesis (p. 266) fit the high-modernist planners whom ch10 calls sincere (p. 271), or only capitalist firms and fiscal states?
- If epistemic practice always involves mētis (p. 269) and scientists like Howard can recognize it (p. 270), is universalism really "inherent" in rationalist knowledge (p. 270), or a feature of particular institutions and their codified self-image?
- How does the chapter relate to Hayek's dispersed-knowledge argument and to Polanyi's tacit knowledge, both of which it leaves in endnotes or omits?
- How do the Meiji technical teams (p. 270) and other cases of epistemic and practical collaboration work, and could they be generalized into the "valuable collaboration" Scott says high modernism replaced (p. 244)?
