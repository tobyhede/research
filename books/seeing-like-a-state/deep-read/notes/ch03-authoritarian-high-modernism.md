# ch03: Authoritarian High Modernism (pp. 69–83)

## Question this chapter answers

What is high modernism, where did it come from, who carried it, and under what conditions does it become dangerous?

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Map (as model of state simplification) | p. 70 | An instrument that summarises what interests the mapmaker. Its power to transform lies not in the map but in the power of those who deploy it. |
| High modernism | p. 71, p. 72 | Named first as the aspiration to the administrative ordering of nature and society raised to a comprehensive level, then defined as a strong version of nineteenth-century beliefs in scientific and technical progress, applied, usually through the state, to every field of human activity. A faith held across the political spectrum. |
| The three elements | p. 71 | High-modernist aspiration (the desire), unrestrained use of modern state power (the means), a weakened or prostrate civil society (the levelled terrain). |
| Discovery of society | p. 74 | The reification of society as an object separate from the state and describable by statistics; the precondition for designing it. |
| Social engineering | p. 73 | Simplification and rationalisation applied to the design of society as a whole; almost exclusively a project of the nation-state. |
| Gardening (Bauman) | pp. 74-75 | The metaphor for the designing state: a selected, pruned and weeded order imposed on a natural site. |
| Social taxidermy | p. 75 | The tendency of planned order to fix positions and arrest the plasticity of social life. |

## Leading propositions

- State simplifications are maps; a map is to be judged by whether it serves its purpose, and its transforming power belongs to whoever wields it (p. 70).
- The state has no monopoly on utilitarian simplification, but it aspires to a monopoly of legitimate force, which is why its maps have been the most transformative (p. 70).
- The eighteenth-century state was still mainly a machine for extraction; it lacked the power, grid and knowledge for intrusive social engineering (p. 71).
- The most tragic episodes of state development combine three elements: high-modernist aspiration, unrestrained state power and a prostrate civil society (p. 71).
- There is a high modernism of the right (Nazism is called "the diagnostic example") as well as of the left (pp. 71-72).
- Much state social engineering has been the work of progressive elites because they came to power with a comprehensive critique of society and a mandate to transform it (p. 72).
- Utopian aspirations are not dangerous in themselves; they go wrong in the hands of elites with no commitment to democracy or civil rights, and brutally wrong where society cannot resist (p. 72).
- The move from description to prescription was deliberate, not inadvertent (p. 73).
- The idea that the state's purpose includes the improvement of all members of society was novel in the nineteenth century, and welfare came to be seen as an end in itself (pp. 73-74).
- State social engineering was "inherently authoritarian": a single planning authority replaces multiple sources of invention and change (p. 75).
- High modernism claims the authority of science and disallows competing sources of judgment (p. 75).
- It implies a radical break with history and tradition, rule by those with scientific knowledge, and the devaluing of politics (pp. 75-76).
- Any democratic or liberal convictions a high modernist holds are external to, and often at war with, the high-modernist ones (p. 76).
- Its temporal emphasis is on the future, which lets certain future benefits justify present sacrifice (p. 77).
- It is the ideology of the bureaucratic intelligentsia, technicians, planners and engineers, whose status it raises (p. 77).
- The account is in one respect "grossly unfair": the enemies of nineteenth-century high modernists were often prejudice and vested interest, the interventions had citizens' support, and everyone now benefits from them (p. 78).
- Favourable soils are war, depression, revolution and colonial rule (pp. 78-79).
- German mobilisation in World War I under Rathenau is the best candidate for the birth of twentieth-century high modernism (p. 79).
- Lenin took the German war economy as the prototype of a socialised economy and embraced Taylorism (pp. 81-82).
- Liberal democratic ideas and institutions have often resisted the authoritarian temptation; the most important barrier is representative institutions, by analogy with Sen on famines (pp. 82-83).

## Arguments

**A. From description to design (pp. 73-74)**

- **Premises**: (p. 73) Enlightenment codes and measures were meant to create a cultural community, not mirror one. (p. 74) Statistics made society an object with lawlike regularities. (p. 74) If nature could be reshaped into a better forest, society could be reshaped into a better population.
- **Unstated premise**: that the step from knowing regularities to designing society was "small" (p. 74); this treats a large change in ambition as nearly automatic once the statistics existed.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 74) Society became an object the state might manage and transform with a view to perfecting it.

**B. Why high modernism is authoritarian (pp. 75-77)**

- **Premises**: (p. 75) Science is taken to give a single answer to every empirical question. (p. 76) Then inherited practice has no standing, and only those with scientific knowledge are fit to rule. (p. 76) Opponents must be educated or swept aside; politics can only obstruct. (pp. 76-77) Projects on the scale envisaged need a public authority able to condemn property and move people.
- **Unstated premises**: that the ideology, and not the interests or circumstances of those who hold it, does the work. Scott partly withdraws this when he says individual high modernists were restrained by democratic or liberal views (p. 76), which makes the authoritarianism a tendency checked by other commitments.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 76) The sources of the view are "deeply authoritarian".
- **Endnote on interests**: "The larger, the more capital-intensive, and the more centralized the schemes, the greater their appeal in terms of power and patronage." (p. 308) in an endnote. Here Scott does give interests a causal role alongside ideology.

**C. The conditions for catastrophe (pp. 71-72, 78-79, 82-83)**

- **Premises**: (p. 71) Ideology supplies desire, the state supplies means, a prostrate society supplies terrain. (pp. 78-79) Revolution and colonial rule give regimes unusual power over a society unable to resist. (pp. 82-83) Where a private sphere, a market economy and representative institutions exist, schemes must accommodate local opinion.
- **Unstated premise**: that the liberal-democratic cases differ in kind and not only in degree. The chapter names Robert Moses, Lilienthal and McNamara in its pantheon (p. 71) but examines no scheme carried out under liberal democracy.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 72) Utopian planning becomes lethal only when joined to unbridled state power and a society that cannot resist.

## Evidence offered

- Intellectual history from secondary sources: Condorcet in 1782 on the moral sciences (p. 73); Hacking on statistical "budgets" of homicide (p. 74); Bauman's gardening metaphor (pp. 74-75); Marx and Engels in the Manifesto on the subjection of nature (p. 76); Harvey on the emancipatory promise (p. 78).
- Rathenau and the Office of War Raw Materials (pp. 79-81); Rabinbach on productivism, Taylorism and the Kaiser Wilhelm Institut für Arbeitsphysiologie (pp. 79-80).
- Lenin on the Taylor system, quoted at length; the 1921 congress on scientific management and at least twenty institutes (pp. 81-82).
- Foucault on the unknowability of the economy for the sovereign (p. 82); Sen on famines and open societies (pp. 82-83).
- The right-wing cases (Nazism, apartheid, the Shah, Gezira) are listed, not analysed (pp. 71-72).
- Endnotes for this chapter (pp. 304-310) were read.
- The term is borrowed from David Harvey, who places high modernism after World War II and is concerned "particularly with capitalism and the organization of production" (p. 305) in an endnote. Scott moves it back to the nineteenth century and onto the state, so the term carries less of Harvey's anticapitalist framing than its source suggests.
- The intellectual-history spine rests on secondary works whose perspectives travel with them: Bauman (gardening, Nazism as modernism), Hacking on how the statistical average became the normal and then a norm for social engineering (endnote, p. 307), Rabinbach and Maier on productivism, Foucault on governmentality, and Yaney for the account of Rathenau's wartime planning (endnote, p. 308). Little is primary beyond Lenin's texts.
- Liberal democracies ran coercive high-modernist programmes too: "By 1926, twenty-three of the forty-eight U.S. states had laws permitting sterilization." (p. 307) in an endnote.
- Domestic before colonial: civilising programmes for the home lower classes preceded colonial ones, the difference being that colonial officials "had greater coercive power over an objectified and alien population" (p. 307) in an endnote.
- The Rathenau model's apparent success had a price: Maier notes that wartime harmony of capital and labour was "achieved at the cost of an eventually ruinous policy of inflation" (p. 309) in an endnote.
- Soviet Taylorism did not last: most labour institutes were closed and their experts "deported or shot in the Stalinist purges of the 1930s" (p. 309) in an endnote. Smolinski reports Lenin and Trotsky saw electric power stations making the farm population dependent on the centre (endnote, p. 309).
- Extraction slip: on p. 305 an unnumbered paragraph on Jean Monnet follows note 4; a note number has been lost, though the sequence 1-51 otherwise runs on.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- The Introduction lists four elements (fm01, pp. 9-10); this chapter lists three (p. 71). Here legibility is folded into high modernism, which is defined as the administrative ordering of nature and society "raised to a far more comprehensive and ambitious level". The definitions of high modernism therefore differ between the two places: in the Introduction it is the ideology only.
- The pantheon mixes builders working under liberal democracy with Lenin and Nyerere (p. 71). The case chapters take only the authoritarian ones, plus Le Corbusier, who held no state power.
- The sympathetic paragraph (p. 78) is the book's fullest acknowledgment of what high modernism achieved. Nothing later weighs those gains against the failures.
- Foucault's point about the unknowability of the economy (p. 82) is close to Hayek's knowledge argument; the body cites it through Foucault, but the endnote to that sentence names Hayek directly: "This point has been made forcefully and polemically in the twentieth century by Friedrich Hayek" (p. 310) in an endnote, calling him "the darling of those opposed to postwar planning and the welfare state" (p. 310) in an endnote. Scott acknowledges the debt while keeping his distance, as in fm01 (p. 12).
- The endnotes widen the right-wing side: Ferguson reminded Scott that reactionary high-modernist schemes "are about as ubiquitous as progressive variants" (p. 306) in an endnote. That sits uneasily with the body's claim that much social engineering came from progressive elites (p. 72).
- "This is not by any means meant to be a brief for conservatism." (p. 306) in an endnote. Scott separates conservatives, who may be brutal but have limited ambitions, from reactionary modernists who want new people. The danger he tracks is ambition to remake society, not cruelty as such.
- Settler colonies and counterinsurgency resettlements are set aside because "even the pretense that the comprehensive social planning was for the welfare of the affected populations has been paper-thin" (p. 308) in an endnote. Welfare intent is thus part of the definition, which excludes some of the largest forced resettlements from the analysis.
- Lenin's Taylorism (pp. 81-82) sets up ch05; Rathenau's electrical centralism sets up the electrification theme in ch06.

## Open questions

- If nineteenth-century high modernism was beneficial and twentieth-century high modernism disastrous, is the difference the ideology or the political setting? The chapter's own answer points to the setting.
- Nazism is called the diagnostic case of right-wing high modernism and then left aside. Does the book's account fit it? The endnote confirms the omission is deliberate: "I will not pursue the argument here, but I think Nazism is best understood as a reactionary form of modernism." (p. 305) in an endnote, deferring to Bauman and Herf.
- What distinguishes "high" modernism from ordinary confidence in applied science, other than outcome? An endnote gives a partial answer: "What is new in high modernism, I believe, is not so much the aspiration for comprehensive planning." (p. 306) in an endnote. What is new is the administrative technology and social knowledge that make it plausible to organise a whole society like a barracks or monastery. That locates the novelty in capacity, not belief, which fits the Introduction's four-element version better than this chapter's definition.
