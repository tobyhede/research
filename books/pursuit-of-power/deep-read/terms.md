## Full ledger by chapter

### fm01: Preface (pp. 1–13)

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| long nineteenth century | p. 3 | Hobsbawm's name for 1789-1914. Evans grants its influence but calls it broken-backed, split by 1848 into two unlike halves, and does not adopt it. |
| ancien régime économique et social | p. 3 | The Annales idea that the old social and economic order (serfdom, high birth and death rates, marginal industry) lasted into the second half of the century although the political old regime had fallen. |
| persistence of the ancien régime | p. 3 | Arno Mayer's thesis that aristocratic elites stayed dominant to 1914. Evans reports that historians have not widely accepted it. |
| global history | p. 4 | History that links parts of the world instead of telling their separate stories; here, bringing other continents in to explain what happened in Europe (p. 5). |
| global hegemony | p. 5 | Europe's lead over, and dominion in, other parts of the world between 1815 and 1914, gained through specific historical circumstances and not intrinsic superiority. It is the main justification for the period. |
| transnational history | pp. 5–6 | History of what European countries had in common and what wider processes affected them, as against parallel national narratives. |
| Europe | p. 6 | Not a geographical area with fixed edges but a social, economic, political and cultural region with common characteristics, from Britain and Ireland to Russia and the Balkans. |
| master-narrative | p. 7 | An overarching story that binds all themes, such as Hobsbawm's placing of capitalism at the core. Evans says historians of his time can no longer use one. |
| lines of development | p. 7 | Blanning's substitute for a master-narrative: long processes that can be traced without claiming they determine everything. Evans names the march of the state, the public sphere, communications and economic growth. |
| public sphere | p. 7 | The cultural space of periodicals, coffee houses and reading clubs, confined in 1815 to a small educated stratum and widened by 1914 into mass participation. |
| progress | p. 8 | How most contemporaries saw the century: linear and open-ended. Evans treats this as a view to be warned against, because progress had a price. |
| inequality | p. 8 | A running theme: older forms such as serfdom give way to newer ones such as wage labour in the factory. |
| emancipation | p. 8 | The grant of greater equality of status to the rural majority, women and religious minorities, notably Jews; always partial and conditional. |
| power | pp. 9–10 | The book's organising word, used in many senses: state, imperial, military, revolutionary, party, economic, the power of lords over serfs and men over women, power over nature, and physical power sources. It may be formal or informal, violent or persuasive. |
| glory | p. 9 | The priority of the elites of 1648-1815 in Blanning's volume; the value that power displaced. |

### ch01: The Legacies of Revolution (pp. 13–87)

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subsistence crisis | p. 21 | The post-1815 conjuncture of harvest failure (Tambora, cold decade), trade dislocation, demobilisation and unemployment. Evans treats it as the fundamental cause of the unrest of 1816-19, ahead of politics. |
| Bonapartism | p. 23 | Patriotism, universal manhood suffrage, national sovereignty, efficient centralised administration treating citizens equally, plebiscites, and an implicit contract of order and glory. Close to Republicanism but with more stress on strong leadership and military prowess. |
| Napoleonic legend / the *liberal Emperor* | p. 23 | The myth, fed by Napoleon's liberal turn in the Hundred Days and his writings in exile, that let liberals, officers and students treat him as the Revolution's purposeful heir. |
| Holy Alliance | p. 32 | Alexander I's 1815 pact with Austria and Prussia for mutual aid when religion, peace or justice was threatened. In practice: a standing prospect of Russian intervention against revolution, but in concert, not alone (p. 33). |
| Vienna Settlement | pp. 33-35 | The territorial and political order of 1814-15 (German Confederation of thirty-nine states, buffer states round France, Congress Poland). Its *central thrust* is defined later as being against the threat of revolution (p. 59). |
| German Confederation | p. 34 | Successor to the Holy Roman Empire with much the same borders; not a national state; chaired by Austria. |
| Balance of Power | p. 36 | The doctrine that no single state should be allowed to dominate the rest. Evans says it was replaced as the organising idea by the Concert, though an equilibrium still in fact counted. |
| Concert of Europe / Congress system | p. 36, p. 44 | A network of collaborative institutions whose main purpose was keeping the peace: the powers meeting often and sometimes acting in common. Driven by fear of revolution. Includes the Quadruple Alliance; France admitted informally from 1819 (p. 44). |
| Sovereignty (shift of) | p. 37 | After 1815 sovereignty rests in nations and states, not individuals and families; treaties bind states and survive a monarch's death; the ruler is the executor of state sovereignty. |
| Restoration | p. 24, pp. 37-38 | The regimes re-established in 1814-15. Evans's usage stresses what was not restored (land, the Code, départements) as much as what was. |
| Ultras | p. 39 | Hard-line French royalists elected on the narrow franchise, aiming at an undiluted pre-revolutionary monarchy. |
| Liberals / liberalism | p. 43, p. 85 | First as a label taken up in Germany from Spanish reformers, for those favouring constitutional reform and national unity (p. 43). Later defined by its debt to the moderate first phase of 1789: freedom, popular sovereignty, representative government, constitutional rule (p. 85). |
| Secret societies (carbonari, charbonnerie, Freemasons) | p. 40, p. 47 | Masonic-style conspiratorial networks of officers, ex-officials and students. Evans: an embryonic revolutionary international, but far weaker than governments believed (p. 52). |
| Pronunciamiento | p. 45 | Public declaration by army officers for a constitution; begun in Spain in January 1820 and lasting as a tradition for over a century. |
| Gendarme of Europe | p. 56 | Sobriquet of Nicholas I for his readiness to use Russian arms against revolution abroad. |
| Barricade | p. 70 | The Parisian crowd's new tactic of 1830, to become standard in later Parisian uprisings. |
| The middle classes | p. 83 | A concept Evans says was born in the Reform Bill debates, in Grey's description of those who form the mass of public opinion. |
| Absolutism (principle of) | p. 86 | What 1830 destroyed almost everywhere west of the great eastern empires, while the principle of monarchy survived. |
| Nationalism / national self-determination | p. 85 | The belief, stimulated by Napoleon among educated elites, that freedom from oppression required national self-determination; from the 1820s it colours liberalism and from 1830 national movements diverge. |

### ch02: The Paradoxes of Freedom (pp. 87–162)

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Serfdom | p. 87 | A customary, locally variable system obliging the peasant to unpaid labour, dues in kind and menial services for a seigneur, with restricted movement and subjection to the lord's justice. Not slavery (serfs had rights as well as duties, pp. 90-91) but a servile status that was always clear (p. 91). Its core, for Evans, is powerlessness (p. 88). |
| Seigneur | p. 87 | The landowning lord holding rights over serfs: labour services, dues, monopolies (milling, liquor, hunting, fishing), courts and police (pp. 91-93). Evans uses the French word for lords everywhere in Europe. |
| Emancipation | p. 94 | The ending of servile status and seigneurial rights by state legislation between the French Revolution and the 1860s (later in Bulgaria, Iceland, Bosnia). Personal freedom was granted free; loss of labour services and dues was compensated (p. 97). |
| Redemption payments | p. 97 | Instalments former serfs paid, usually without state help, to compensate lords for lost services and dues: over 15 years in Romania, 25 in Saxony, 49 in Russia (p. 97). The mechanism by which freedom became debt. |
| Landless rural proletariat | p. 101 | Former peasants bought out or dispossessed after emancipation who live wholly or partly by farm wages; later generalised as people who only had their labour to sell (p. 102). |
| Quasi-serfdom | p. 102 | Evans's label for post-emancipation legal regimes restricting rural labourers (Prussian Statute of Farm Servants 1810, Hungarian Farm Servants Act 1907); also the labour-rent dependency of Romanian peasants (p. 104). |
| Sharecropping | p. 106 | Contract under which a family works a landowner's fields for a share of the crop (half in Tuscany), with controls on marriage, movement and labour that Evans presents as close to servile (pp. 107-108). |
| Jacquerie | p. 103 | Traditional peasant rising: storming and burning manor houses and their records, seizing land. Romania's is called the last great one in European history (p. 104). |
| Demographic ancien régime | p. 112 | Historians' term for a population regime with very high birth and death rates (about 45 births and 30-40 deaths per 1,000). |
| Pauperism | p. 116 | The growing mass of destitute people needing support, seen by middle-class elites as a product of idleness and a political threat (p. 117). |
| Less eligibility | p. 118 | Principle of the 1834 New Poor Law: the pauper's condition must be made worse than the independent labourer's, so relief is given only in a deliberately unpleasant workhouse. |
| Political Economy | pp. 118-119 | The body of liberal economic doctrine (capitalised by Evans) holding that the able-bodied destitute are idle; applied to poor relief and, with calamitous results, to famine relief (p. 119). |
| The hungry forties | p. 119 | The subsistence crisis of 1845-9 (potato blight plus grain failure), called the deepest and most devastating of the century (p. 129). |
| Proto-industry / putting-out | p. 122, p. 129 | Rural domestic manufacture (handloom, spinning wheel) sold through a middleman; seasonal, unreliable, dependent on women's and children's labour (p. 129). Its collapse alongside agriculture deepened the 1840s crisis in Flanders and Silesia (pp. 122, 129). |
| Industrial revolution | p. 136 | A term first used in France in the 1820s. For Evans the decisive element is not the large factory, which existed earlier, but steam replacing natural power (p. 136). |
| Time-discipline | p. 134 | The regime of bells, fixed hours, locked doors and fines that the factory imposed, contrasted with the handloom weaver's control over his own hours. |
| Industrial substitution | p. 152 | Continental countries replacing imported British rails and locomotives with their own production once railways were under way. |
| Guilds | p. 153 | Incorporated bodies regulating craft production, limiting output and entry to secure a decent living and high standards; also sources of welfare, identity and collective action (pp. 153-154). |
| Compagnonnage | p. 156 | French journeymen's secret societies supporting the tour de France; had some trade-union functions but were divided by status rivalry and in decline by the 1830s (pp. 156-157). |
| Proletariat / working class | p. 160 | A new class comprising poor single masters, factory workers, miners and urban wage labourers: all who lack property and live entirely from their own and their families' physical labour. |
| The social question | p. 160 | Contemporaries' name for the anxiety about industrial poverty and class division, dramatised in the social novels of the 1840s. |

### ch03: The European Spring (pp. 162–253)

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Utopian socialists | p. 164 | Mainly French writers since 1789 who sketched an ideal society and expected model communities to convert the world by example. Evans agrees with Tristan that they lacked realism (p. 165) but credits them with coining the vocabulary: communism (Cabet, p. 165), socialism (Leroux in French, p. 166; Owen in English, p. 167), feminism (Fourier, p. 167). |
| Nationalism (two types) | pp. 170-171 | Type one: freeing a nation from a foreign yoke, which for most meant autonomy or recognition of language and culture, not independence. Type two: uniting one nation split among several states (German, Italian), where full sovereignty was the aim from the start. Before mid-century it was often a means to liberal reform. |
| Moderate liberals | p. 173 | Propertied constitutional monarchists who took Britain as their model, wanted parliaments, legal equality, free trade and a restricted franchise, and sought reform in order to avoid revolution. The chapter's central actors and, in Evans's account, the hinge on which 1848 turned. |
| Democrats / radicals | p. 176 | Those who wanted universal male suffrage and usually a republic, inspired by 1789-93 not by Britain (p. 207). Many were former carbonari who now led crowds instead of conspiracies (p. 201). |
| English gradualism / French insurrectionism | p. 179 | The two rival scripts of political change. 1848 is the moment the second temporarily displaced the first on the Continent. |
| March Demands | p. 182 | The items of Struve's Mannheim petition (free press, jury trial, militia with elected officers, constitutions, an all-German parliament) as they circulated through the German states. |
| National Guard / citizens' militia | p. 180, p. 208 | The moderate liberals' instrument for replacing royal armies and keeping order; a double-edged sword because its members wavered between order and the street, and no match for regular troops (pp. 208-209). |
| June Days | p. 204 | The Paris workers' rising of late June 1848 after the closing of the National Workshops (narrated pp. 190-191); the event that pushed liberals and conservatives together across Europe. |
| Magyarization | p. 191 | The Hungarian nationalists' programme of imposing Magyar on Croats, Serbs, Slovaks, Saxons and Romanians; Evans's main example of liberalism and nationalism contradicting each other. |
| Dialectic of radicalization and reaction | p. 201 | The late-revolution mechanism: the more democrats turned to the masses, the more liberals called on the army of the established authority. |
| Plebiscitary dictatorship | p. 217 | Napoleon III's regime: legitimacy drawn from popular votes that were managed by bribery and intimidation, rule through bureaucracy, police and army, with prosperity and foreign glory to keep support. |
| Neo-absolutism | p. 226 | The Austrian system after the New Year's Eve Patent of 1851: centralized, German-speaking bureaucratic rule, an *a-national* not multinational state, combined with railway-building and peasant emancipation. |
| Concert of Europe | p. 225 | Great Power co-operation to maintain the status quo since 1815; Evans dates its effective end, and the end of monarchical solidarity, to the Crimean War. |
| Iron and blood | p. 235 | Bismarck's phrase of September 1862. Evans glosses it concretely: Prussia's disjointed territory had to be joined up and the German Confederation destroyed by force. |
| Indemnity Bill | p. 239 | Bismarck's 1866 request for retrospective approval of four years of unbudgeted rule; in Evans's reading a deliberate recognition of parliament that split the liberals and sidelined hard-line conservatives. |
| Ausgleich (Compromise) / Dual Monarchy | pp. 240-241 | The 1867 division of the Habsburg Empire into Austrian and Hungarian halves with common army, foreign policy and monarch. |
| Socialists versus anarchists | p. 253 | After 1872: followers of Marx who relied on open parties and the ballot box, against followers of Bakunin who relied on insurrection, assassination and the rural masses. |

### ch04: The Social Revolution (pp. 253-327)

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decline of the aristocracy | p. 253 (heading), p. 260 | Loss of political power and legal distinctness by the titled landowning nobility, caused mainly by the growing state taking over the seigneur's functions, plus franchise extension and economic change. A loss of power and economic identity, not of prestige. |
| Persistence of the old regime | p. 260 | A thesis of *some historians* (Evans names none) that aristocratic dominance lasted to 1914. Evans rejects it flatly. |
| Embourgeoisement | p. 259 | The steady replacement of nobles by commoners in officer corps and bureaucracies. |
| New elite / hybrid social elite | p. 267, p. 326 | An upper class based above all on wealth, mixing large landowners, bankers, industrialists and investors, titled and untitled, sharing one style of life; its values are bourgeois (thrift, hard work, sobriety, responsibility). |
| Order, class, status group | pp. 268-269 | An order is fixed by legal privilege; a class is defined economically. Evans holds the aristocracy was always also a class (wealth from land), and in the nineteenth century became ever more a status group: prestige without a distinct economic base. |
| Workshop of the world | p. 270 | Britain's mid-century position, producing over 40 per cent of traded manufactures and dominating a global division of labour; by 1914 no longer alone (p. 326). |
| Vertical combination / integration | p. 273, p. 280 | Firms uniting coal, ore, smelting and engineering to cut costs; typical of the Ruhr and of Russian metallurgy (p. 276). |
| Second industrial revolution | p. 277 (heading), p. 280 | The late-century wave based on chemicals, electricity and science-trained staff, in which Germany and others outpaced Britain. Evans qualifies it: Britain lagged *in parts of it* (p. 280). |
| Organized capitalism | p. 280 | A term of *some historians* for the German interweaving of banks, cartels and large firms, often close to government, which controlled open-market competition. Evans reports it and supports it with cartel figures. |
| Hygienic revolution | p. 308 | The late-century provision of clean water, sewers and food control in towns; the phrase appears in passing, the substance is at pp. 285-288. |
| Bourgeoisie (economic and educated) | p. 293, p. 295 | Literally city-dwellers; in many ways a new class created by industry. Divided into an entrepreneurial stratum living off the market and a professional stratum living off qualifications, increasingly joined by clubs and marriage. |
| Respectability | p. 300 | The outward sign of bourgeois status, won by work, probity and lifestyle rather than title or descent. |
| Intelligentsia | p. 299 | The small educated group, many of them noble, that stood in for a middle class in Russia for most of the century. |
| Petty bourgeoisie / Mittelstand | p. 301, p. 304 | Lower middle class of clerks, teachers, shopkeepers, small masters. Marx thought it doomed; Evans says change both threatened and renewed it. *Mittelstand* is the German self-description as a middle order between workers and the bourgeoisie. |
| Proletariat (permanent, hereditary) | p. 305, p. 312 | Wage-earning manual workers and their families, the largest class in industrial countries; becomes a class in the full sense only when workers stop returning to the land and a second generation is born into it. |
| Landflucht | p. 312 | Prussian term for the flight from the land of young men to better-paid urban work. |
| Dangerous classes / Lumpenproletariat | p. 313 | Contemporaries' image of a separate, irredeemable criminal underclass. Evans treats the separate class as mostly a literary invention. |
| Charivari (rough music, Haberfeldtreiben) | p. 316 | Communal shaming rituals by which villages policed their own rules; evidence that rural society administered its own justice until the state suppressed it. |
| First age of globalization | p. 325 | The peak, just before 1914, of flows of capital, goods, people and ideas between continents, with Europe dominant; mass emigration is its social dimension. |

### ch05: The Conquest of Nature (pp. 327–407)

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conquest of nature | p. 345 | The extension of human control over the wilderness and its animals, the elements, the *fundamental factors that framed life and experience* (space, time), and, in the later sections, the human body and human nature. Always qualified: it met limits and produced unwelcome consequences (p. 406). |
| Nature / state of nature | pp. 329–330 | Both the non-human world and a condition attributed to supposedly backward peoples (the Hungarians, in the opening). Evans plays the two senses against each other, including a person's own nature (p. 329). |
| Mastery over nature | p. 332 | What animal shows, zoos, hunting and mountaineering displayed to an urban public that had lost contact with the wild. |
| Shrinkage of space | p. 345 | The reduction of effective distance by railways, steamships, post, telegraph, cars, bicycles, lifts and flight; includes the standardisation of how space is measured. |
| Standardisation | p. 346 | Replacement of local measures, local times and incompatible rules with uniform national and international ones (metre, railway gauge and regulations, postal union, time zones), driven by trade, railways, telegraphs and armies. |
| Standard time / modern time | pp. 358–359 | Uniform clock time set nationally and then by the Greenwich meridian, replacing local solar time; more broadly a time that is uniform, linear and organised, and by 1914 also hurried and beginning to seem malleable (pp. 361–363). |
| Speed | p. 362 | The felt acceleration of life in the later century, quoted from W. R. Greg and Frederic Harrison; expressed in railway races, tea clippers and transatlantic liners. |
| Sanitary revolution | p. 369 | The provision of clean water and proper sewage disposal in European cities in the second half of the century; for Evans a more important cause of falling epidemic disease than medical discovery. |
| Social medicine | p. 367 | The approach exemplified by Virchow's 1848 typhus report: disease explained by deprivation and lack of rights, not by national character. |
| Germ theory of disease | p. 369 | The bacteriological explanation associated with Pasteur and Koch; it supplied the scientific basis for antisepsis (p. 386) but before 1914 did little to cure. |
| Demographic transition | p. 375 | The fall in death rates followed, at an interval, by a corresponding fall in birth rates; Evans attributes the phrase to historians. |
| Victorian prudery | p. 377 | Not mere squeamishness but, in Evans's reading, a reinforcement of female sexual abstinence at a time when abstinence was the only reliable contraceptive. |
| Regulation (of prostitution) | p. 379 | Police registration, compulsory medical inspection and confinement of prostitutes, justified as protecting male clients from disease; *neo-reglementarism* names its tightened French form after 1906 (p. 381). |
| Social purity | p. 380 | The campaigning current (Butler, the morality societies, later the Suffragettes) that targeted male lust and the double standard, and that also underlay the criminalisation of male homosexuality (pp. 381–382). |
| Antisepsis | p. 387 | Lister's practice of preventing wound infection with carbolic acid, handwashing and dressings; with asepsis, slow to become routine. |
| Lunatic asylum / mental hospital | p. 389 | A separate, medically supervised institution for the insane, product of Enlightenment rationalism and reforming optimism; by the end of the century a place of custody for the deviant (p. 396). |
| Deviance | p. 395 | Behaviour outside bourgeois norms (alcoholism, petty crime, breaches of gender expectations) that later-century psychiatry and criminology redefined as inherited disorder. |
| Penitentiary | p. 400 | The purpose-built reforming prison on the Philadelphia model: isolation, hard labour, religion and surveillance meant to remould character. |
| Atavism | p. 403 | Lombroso's idea that born criminals are throwbacks to an earlier stage of evolution; little accepted in that form, but the broader idea of inherited criminality spread. |
| Gendarmerie | p. 404 | The French-style mounted, semi-military rural police, Evans's most important instrument of restored state order after 1815. |

### ch06: The Age of Emotion (pp. 407–489)

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biedermeier | p. 410 | Central European culture of 1815-48 that, under Metternich's repression of open political discourse, turned inward to home, family, nature and depoliticised country life; also the plain, affordable furniture style that appealed to the new middle classes. Evans treats it as one of two main currents of the age, with Romantic emotionalism (p. 409). |
| Romanticism | p. 411 | The artistic movement that reacted against Enlightenment rationalism by emphasising the emotions, the exotic and the wild; feeling rather than intellect as the source of truth (p. 412). Early Romantic emotion is predominantly inward and private (p. 414). |
| Cult of the genius / virtuoso | p. 411 | The obverse of Biedermeier domesticity: the performer or artist whose gifts are beyond ordinary people (Paganini, Liszt), and the suffering genius (Beethoven) (p. 412). |
| *Improvement* of the past | p. 415 | Evans's ironic label for nineteenth-century adaptation of old music and buildings to current taste (Mendelssohn's cut Bach, Viollet-le-Duc's gargoyles); later displaced by heritage preservation and scholarly authenticity (p. 416). |
| Ultramontanes | p. 416 | Catholic clergy and laity north of the Alps who, freed from aristocratic patrons, looked to Rome; militant, anti-revolutionary, and mobilised through new emotional devotions (Immaculate Conception, Sacred Heart, pilgrimage) (pp. 416-417). |
| Awakening | p. 420 | Protestant revival movements that reacted both to Enlightenment rationalism and to state-sponsored quasi-Catholic ritual: itinerant preaching, Bible, private prayer, strict morals. |
| Kulturkampf | p. 425 | The German liberals' name, *struggle of civilizations*, for Bismarck's 1871-5 legislation against the Catholic Church; Evans presents it as one case of a Europe-wide Church-State conflict in new or restructured states. |
| Secularization of the masses | p. 426 | Loss of the habit of worship among urban workers, driven largely by migration and the shortage of churches and clergy in cities; not the same as unbelief, since rites of passage stayed religious (pp. 427-428). |
| Disenchantment of the world | p. 429 | Weber's term, adopted by Evans: replacement of a magical view of nature by goal-oriented, rational, scientific belief; at first confined to parts of the urban working class and the liberal bourgeoisie. |
| Scientific materialism | p. 430 | The most serious intellectual challenge to Christianity: geology (Lyell), Chambers's *Vestiges*, Darwin. |
| Emancipation (Jewish) | p. 435 | Grant of civil equality to Jews, state by state from 1791 to 1923; judged a triumph of social emancipation from the vantage point of 1900, though incomplete (p. 439). |
| Antisemitism | p. 437 | A new and more virulent prejudice based on supposedly scientific doctrines of racial difference, growing out of older Christian hostility; the term coined by Steinschneider and popularised by Marr (1879). Politically limited before 1914 in Germany, instrumentalised in Austria. |
| Locality versus nationality | p. 440 | Before mass literacy, identity was rooted in village, valley and region, marked by dialect; national consciousness was largely absent. |
| Standard language | p. 444 | A dialect chosen (or a language constructed) by nationalists to justify claims to nationhood and statehood: Tuscan, West Bulgarian, Serbo-Croat, Nynorsk, Katharevousa. |
| Progress (shared road) | p. 446 | The early-century assumption, common to nationalists and their critics, that all European nations were travelling the same road to civilisation at different speeds; replaced later by innate, unalterable racial character (pp. 446-447). |
| Humboldtian university | p. 451 | Integration of teaching and research, freedom to teach and learn, study for its own sake; in practice vocational and state-controlled for much of the century, but the model widely imitated. |
| Positivism | p. 454 | Comte's doctrine that scientific observation is the only legitimate basis for action; extended to history as a claim to completeness and certainty (p. 455). |
| Gendered emotion | p. 455 | The nineteenth-century doctrine, absent or marginal in earlier encyclopedias, that men think and women feel; used to justify legal minority and exclusion from public life. |
| Manliness | p. 456 | Key term of approval in the second half of the century; impassivity, expressed in beards, dark clothes, clubs and the cult of the hero. |
| Public sphere | p. 456 | The world of politics, press, clubs and associations, assumed to be male; women enter it through philanthropy, writing and feminist campaigning (pp. 461, 463). |
| Feminism | p. 459 | Organised movements, mostly from the 1860s and 1870s, for legal, educational and property rights; almost exclusively middle-class until the late century (p. 463). |
| Realism | p. 475 | Art and fiction addressing contemporary life in a true-to-life manner, predominant after mid-century; called forth above all by industrialisation (p. 476); still an appeal to emotion (p. 478). |
| Verismo | p. 479 | Italian literary and operatic Realism: the actual lives of the people rather than historical or fantastic subjects. |
| Primitivism | p. 487 | Use of non-European and folk forms left raw (Picasso and African art, Bartók, Stravinsky) to break with representational and tonal convention; the disruptive side of the folk revival. |
| Modernism / modernist | p. 488 | Evans's umbrella for the pre-1914 movements that separated art from representation and music from tonality and shook the prewar cultural world (p. 489). |

### ch07: The Challenge of Democracy (pp. 489–570)

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Democracy | p. 517 | Defined conditionally as resting above all on universal adult suffrage plus the responsibility of government to parliament and electorate. Both halves matter: Germany had the first (for men) without the second (pp. 548, 556). |
| Challenge of democracy | p. 499 | Pressure from below on existing political systems: organised working-class interests (p. 499), feminism, and in Russia the intelligentsia (p. 556). Not a settled condition but a contest. |
| Suffragettes / suffragists | p. 490 | *Suffragettes* is the Daily Mail's label for the militant Women's Social and Political Union (p. 490); the moderate National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies was far larger (p. 493). |
| Equal suffrage | p. 499 | Votes for women on the same terms as men, which under property franchises meant only a small propertied minority of women; contrasted with universal adult suffrage (pp. 495, 499). |
| Welfare state | p. 499 | State insurance against sickness, accident and old age, plus regulation of work, introduced from the 1880s. For Evans in essence a response to left-wing politics (p. 504). |
| State socialism | p. 502 | Bismarck's own name for his welfare measures, linked in his mind to aristocratic paternalism. |
| Anarcho-syndicalism | p. 500 | The doctrine of overthrowing the capitalist state through a universal general strike (Spanish CNT, French CGT). |
| Revisionism / pragmatists | p. 506 | Revisionists (Bernstein) argued Marx's predictions had failed and the SPD should convert to liberal reformism; they lost. Pragmatists, to whom ideology was irrelevant, were more numerous and influential. |
| Propaganda by the deed | p. 512 | Anarchist direct action: destabilising the state by individual violence and terrorism. |
| Age of the masses | p. 517 | The condition, reached in almost every country by 1900, in which parties of every kind competed for mass allegiance. |
| Nationalization of the masses | p. 521 | The drawing of newly enfranchised and literate populations into national (and minority-national) identity; Evans puts the phrase in inverted commas and gives no source. Reused at pp. 536 and 567. |
| Liberal hegemony | p. 521 | Dominance of parliamentary politics by middle-class or aristocratic liberals under restricted franchises; its decay is the most obvious consequence of franchise extension. |
| Tory Democracy | p. 524 | Disraeli's remaking of the Conservatives as a modern party in organisation and ideology, appealing to patriotism. |
| Home Rule | p. 525 | A separate Irish parliament within the United Kingdom; the issue that split the Liberals in 1886 and returned in 1912 (pp. 526, 529). |
| Curiae | p. 531 | The class-based electoral colleges (landowners, towns, chambers of commerce, rural communities, later a fifth general one) that chose the Austrian Reichsrat until 1907. |
| Magyarization | p. 535 | Imposition of Magyar as the language of administration, justice, place names and secondary schooling in Hungary. |
| Trasformismo | p. 539 | The art of converting a failing coalition into a winning one by changing its composition. Evans attaches it to Giolitti. |
| Solidarism | p. 543 | The centrist Republican alliance of Radicals and Opportunists after Boulanger, which passed France's social legislation. |
| Camarilla | p. 553 | Wilhelm II's court circle, credited by critics with more power than it probably had. |
| Intelligentsia | p. 556 | A group defined by civic activism, especially in literature, not by education alone; in Russia the carrier of the democratic challenge in the absence of a large middle class. |
| Narodniki (Populists) | p. 559 | Students who went to the people in 1874 to convert peasants to revolution. |
| Zemstva | p. 562 | Elected local government bodies created under Alexander II; their professional employees became the base of Russian liberalism. |
| Democratic centralism | p. 563 | The Bolshevik doctrine that, in Evans's words, legitimised an autocratic system of party administration. |
| Americanization | p. 568 | Term coined by W. T. Stead (1901); used for the growing American influence on European economy, culture and political imagination. |

### ch08: The Wages of Empire (pp. 570–647)

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Imperialism | p. 579, p. 585 | An explicit ideology asserting the superiority of European civilization and justifying colonies as policy. Evans stresses it did not exist for much of the century; the word entered English in the 1870s. Distinct from the earlier unplanned growth of empire. |
| Age of imperialism / high imperialism | p. 585, p. 596 | The period from the 1870s to 1914, especially 1878-1914, when Europe added over 8.6 million square miles to its territory (p. 586). |
| Free trade (as imperial method) | p. 579 | Britain's reliance on naval and commercial dominance instead of annexation; also a weapon, as when low tariffs forced on Tunisia and the Ottomans flooded them with European goods (pp. 587, 610). |
| Scramble for Africa | p. 589 | A British term coined in 1884 for the rush of annexations set off by Bismarck's protectorates and the Berlin Conference; over by the mid-1890s (p. 591). |
| Effective occupation | p. 589 | The Berlin Conference's test for a valid colonial claim; in Evans's view a dead letter, since it applied only to coasts. |
| Paper colonies | p. 596 | Territories claimed by lines on a map but not controlled; the story of the 1890s and 1900s is their conversion into real ones. |
| Colonies of settlement / colonies of occupation | pp. 597-598 | Settlement: climate allows mass European emigration (Canada, Australia, German South-West Africa). Occupation: a handful of officials over a large indigenous population (Cameroon, Togo). The distinction explains different patterns of violence. |
| Debt Commission | p. 588 | International creditor bodies that took over the finances of Tunis, Egypt, the Ottoman Empire and later Greece and Serbia; Evans treats them as a standard mechanism of informal control (pp. 587, 614, 626). |
| Concentration camps | p. 597 | First official German use in South-West Africa for interned Herero and Nama; also the British camps for Boers and black Africans in the South African war (p. 602). |
| Indirect rule | p. 605 | Governing through existing indigenous institutions and elites; derived from British India and adopted in French thinking as the civilizing mission retreated. |
| Civilizing mission | p. 605 | The earlier French belief in spreading the benefits of the Revolution; partly abandoned on grounds of cost. |
| White man's burden | p. 609 | Kipling's 1899 phrase for the ideology of improvement; Evans inverts it to mean the burden the imperialist imposed on the colonized (p. 610). |
| Indentured labour | p. 608 | A kind of quasi-slavery: paid work without freedom or rights, drawn mainly from India. |
| Eastern Question | p. 610 | The problem of who would benefit from the decline of the Ottoman Empire in south-eastern Europe. |
| Tanzimat | p. 610 | Ottoman reforms of the 1850s and 1860s: civil rights, law codes, a state bank, equal rights for religious groups, an Ottoman national identity. |
| Pan-Slavism | p. 611 | The doctrine, emerging in Russia in the 1870s, that the smaller Slav nations form a family headed by Russia; a pressure on tsarist policy in 1876-7 and 1914 (pp. 612, 643). |
| Great Game | p. 584 | London's name for Anglo-Russian jockeying in Central Asia; the reason Britain saw Russia as its main enemy until the 1900s (pp. 615, 633). |
| Social Darwinism | p. 620 | The application of Darwin's ideas, or a version of them, to human society; it had a progressive welfare variant as well as the harsh competitive one. |
| Scientific racism | p. 622 | The arrangement of racial types on an evolutionary scale, with mixing thought to drag down the higher races. |
| Weltpolitik | p. 634 | Bülow's *world policy*: loud German intervention in world affairs in pursuit of a place in the sun, backed by Tirpitz's battleship fleet. |
| Concert of Europe | p. 615 | Great Power co-operation to manage crises; revived at Berlin in 1878, crumbling after 1890 (p. 633). |
| Blank cheque | p. 640 | Germany's unconditional promise of 6 July 1914 to support whatever Austria did against Serbia; for Evans most probably the crucial moment of the crisis (p. 645). |
| European hegemony | p. 574, p. 647 | Europe's worldwide dominance, real only from the third quarter of the century (p. 595) and ended by the war. |
