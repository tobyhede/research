# Map: The Pursuit of Power: Europe, 1815-1914

- **Author**: Richard J. Evans
- **Edition/year**: Allen Lane / Penguin, 2016 (ISBN 978-0-241-29577-9). Volume VII of the Penguin History of Europe, general editor David Cannadine. It follows Tim Blanning's *The Pursuit of Glory* (1648-1815) and precedes Ian Kershaw's *To Hell and Back* (1914-1949).

## Classification

- **Kind**: theoretical (explains what was), in the form of a narrative synthesis.
- **Genre**: history. A single-author survey for the general reader, transnational and thematic instead of country by country. It has no footnotes or endnotes, by series design (p. 11), so the evidence is carried in the text and the Further Reading. Read with the history strategy: separate what happened from what Evans is trying to prove, and ask in each chapter what kind of source he leans on and whose view it carries.

## The whole-book question

What made the century between Waterloo and the First World War a distinct period in European history, and how did the struggle for power in all its forms, inside Europe and between Europe and the rest of the world, shape it?

## Unity statement

Evans treats 1815-1914 as the century of Europe's global hegemony, held together not by a master-narrative but by lines of development: the growth of the state and the public sphere, faster communications and economic growth, and the partial emancipation of serfs, women and religious minorities (pp. 5, 7-8). What the era's rival movements shared was the wish to acquire and use power, over other people, other states, other continents and nature, and power displaced glory and honour as the governing value (pp. 9-10). Progress had a price, visible by 1914 in machine guns, colonial concentration camps and racial thinking, and Europe paid it after 1914 (p. 8).

## Structure

Each chapter opens with the life of one individual (four men, four women, eight countries) and is divided into titled sections (p. 10). Chapters 1, 3, 7 and 8 are mainly political, 2 and 4 social and economic, 5 and 6 cultural (p. 10).

## High-value chapters for this reading

Ranked against the stated reading purpose: "build a reusable, source-anchored account of what Evans argues about nineteenth-century Europe, how the argument is built, and what evidence carries it"

1. fm01 — the only place the argument is stated in general terms; the chapters mostly narrate.
2. ch08 — empire and the road to 1914, where the title's theme (power, reconceived in racial terms) is cashed out and the book ends.
3. ch02 — serf emancipation and its limits, the clearest case of the emancipation-with-limits theme.
4. ch07 — the state transformed by mass politics, the second half of the preface's state and public-sphere line.
5. ch05 — the most original chapter in range: power over nature and over human nature.
6. ch03 — 1848 as the hinge of the "broken-backed" century (p. 3).
7. ch04, ch06, ch01 — necessary for coverage; more descriptive.
