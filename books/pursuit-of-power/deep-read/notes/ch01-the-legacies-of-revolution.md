# ch01: The Legacies of Revolution (pp. 13–87)

## Question this chapter answers

What did a quarter-century of revolution and war leave behind in Europe, and why did the order built in 1815 to contain that legacy both hold together for so long and fail to restore the old regime? Evans's answer: the wars left physical ruin, a subsistence crisis and a permanent elite fear of revolution; that fear, more than any balance of power, produced a habit of Great Power co-operation (the Concert of Europe) that kept the peace for a century. But the Revolution and Napoleon had changed the basis of sovereignty, administration and political expectation so deeply that the Restoration was a new kind of conservatism, not a return. The revolts of the 1820s failed because their makers (junior officers, secret societies, educated minorities) were cut off from the masses; the revolutions of 1830 succeeded only as far as moderate constitutional reform, because the middle classes feared the crowd and the peasantry stayed out.

## Section guide

- **The Aftermath of War (pp. 13-22)**: opens with the memoir of the conscript Jakob Walter, then counts the human and material cost of the wars, the Tambora eruption and the 1816-17 famine, epidemics, grain riots and the Hep-Hep riots. Ends by deriving two results: state acceptance of a duty of relief, and a settlement aimed at preventing revolution.
- **After Napoleon (pp. 22-30)**: the Hundred Days and Waterloo, the Napoleonic legend and Bonapartism, Napoleon's administrative and legal legacy in Europe, the collapse of the Iberian empires in the Americas, and the shift in the global balance in Europe's (above all Britain's) favour.
- **The Congress of Vienna (pp. 30-37)**: the Congress as spectacle; Metternich, Alexander I and the Holy Alliance; the territorial settlement; the exclusion of extra-European questions; the argument about why the century 1815-1914 was comparatively peaceful; the shift of sovereignty from dynasties to states.
- **Reforging the Chain of Time (pp. 37-44)**: Restoration France under Louis XVIII and Charles X (Charter, Ultras, liberal opposition, charbonnerie), and Restoration Germany (Burschenschaft, Wartburg, Kotzebue, Karlsbad Decrees, bureaucratic government).
- **Revolt and Repression (pp. 44-54)**: the Congress system at work; revolutions of 1820-25 in Spain, Naples, Piedmont, Portugal and Russia (Decembrists) and their suppression; secret societies and the fear of them; Cato Street; why liberals would not ally with the masses.
- **The Gendarme of Europe (pp. 54-59)**: Nicholas I's regime (Third Department, Uvarov, censorship) and the Polish uprising of 1830-31 and its punishment.
- **Greek Independence (pp. 59-69)**: Ottoman weakness, Ali Pasha, Ypsilantis, massacres on both sides, philhellenism, Navarino, the Russo-Turkish war, the London conference, Kapodistrias, Otto of Bavaria, Serbia and Bosnia. Closes with a balance sheet of the 1820s.
- **The July Revolution (pp. 69-73)**: the fall of Charles X, the barricades, Louis-Philippe's settlement, and the instability and repression of the 1830s.
- **European Repercussions (pp. 74-83)**: 1830 in Belgium, Portugal, Spain, Italy, the German states (Hambach and the reaction), Switzerland, and the British Reform Act of 1832.
- **The Changing Shape of Politics (pp. 83-87)**: the analytical conclusion: new conservatism, Romanticism turning liberal, liberalism acquiring a nationalist tone, the social forces of 1830 compared with 1789, and the absent peasantry (the bridge to ch02).

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subsistence crisis | p. 21 | The post-1815 conjuncture of harvest failure (Tambora, cold decade), trade dislocation, demobilisation and unemployment. Evans treats it as the fundamental cause of the unrest of 1816-19, ahead of politics. |
| Bonapartism | p. 23 | Patriotism, universal manhood suffrage, national sovereignty, efficient centralised administration treating citizens equally, plebiscites, and an implicit contract of order and glory. Close to Republicanism but with more stress on strong leadership and military prowess. |
| Napoleonic legend / the *liberal Emperor* | p. 23 | The myth, fed by Napoleon's liberal turn in the Hundred Days and his writings in exile, that let liberals, officers and students treat him as the Revolution's purposeful heir. |
| Holy Alliance | p. 32 | Alexander I's 1815 pact with Austria and Prussia for mutual aid when religion, peace or justice was threatened. In practice: a standing prospect of Russian intervention against revolution, but in concert, not alone (p. 33). |
| Vienna Settlement | pp. 33-35 | The territorial and political order of 1814-15 (German Confederation of thirty-nine states, buffer states round France, Congress Poland). Its *central thrust* is defined later as being against the threat of revolution (p. 59). |
| German Confederation | p. 34 | Successor to the Holy Roman Empire with much the same borders; not a national state; chaired by Austria. |
| Balance of Power | p. 36 | The doctrine that no single state should be allowed to dominate the rest. Evans says it was replaced as the organising idea by the Concert, though an equilibrium still in fact counted. |
| Concert of Europe / Congress system | p. 36, p. 44 | A network of collaborative institutions whose main purpose was keeping the peace: the powers meeting often and sometimes acting in common. Driven by fear of revolution. Includes the Quadruple Alliance; France admitted informally from 1819 (p. 44). |
| Sovereignty (shift of) | p. 37 | After 1815 sovereignty rests in nations and states, not individuals and families; treaties bind states and survive a monarch's death; the ruler is the executor of state sovereignty. |
| Restoration | p. 24, pp. 37-38 | The regimes re-established in 1814-15. Evans's usage stresses what was not restored (land, the Code, départements) as much as what was. |
| Ultras | p. 39 | Hard-line French royalists elected on the narrow franchise, aiming at an undiluted pre-revolutionary monarchy. |
| Liberals / liberalism | p. 43, p. 85 | First as a label taken up in Germany from Spanish reformers, for those favouring constitutional reform and national unity (p. 43). Later defined by its debt to the moderate first phase of 1789: freedom, popular sovereignty, representative government, constitutional rule (p. 85). |
| Secret societies (carbonari, charbonnerie, Freemasons) | p. 40, p. 47 | Masonic-style conspiratorial networks of officers, ex-officials and students. Evans: an embryonic revolutionary international, but far weaker than governments believed (p. 52). |
| Pronunciamiento | p. 45 | Public declaration by army officers for a constitution; begun in Spain in January 1820 and lasting as a tradition for over a century. |
| Gendarme of Europe | p. 56 | Sobriquet of Nicholas I for his readiness to use Russian arms against revolution abroad. |
| Barricade | p. 70 | The Parisian crowd's new tactic of 1830, to become standard in later Parisian uprisings. |
| The middle classes | p. 83 | A concept Evans says was born in the Reform Bill debates, in Grey's description of those who form the mass of public opinion. |
| Absolutism (principle of) | p. 86 | What 1830 destroyed almost everywhere west of the great eastern empires, while the principle of monarchy survived. |
| Nationalism / national self-determination | p. 85 | The belief, stimulated by Napoleon among educated elites, that freedom from oppression required national self-determination; from the 1820s it colours liberalism and from 1830 national movements diverge. |

## Leading propositions

- The Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars killed an estimated five million people, proportionately as many as, or more than, the First World War; the older view that their damage was light is wrong (p. 15).
- Ordinary Europeans, like the conscript Jakob Walter, had no political stake in the wars; war-weariness was general by 1815 (pp. 13-14).
- The unrest of 1816-19 was caused fundamentally by the subsistence crisis, unemployment and fear, not by local or national politics (p. 21).
- The crisis produced a general acceptance that states were obliged to relieve the poorest, though their capacity to do so was limited (pp. 21-22).
- Fear of a new 1789 made the post-war settlement as concerned with preventing and repressing revolution as with containing France (p. 22).
- Napoleon's final defeat was never in doubt; Waterloo was not the hinge that legend made it (p. 25).
- The Allied intervention of 1815 was new in being wholly preventive, and set the pattern for later interventions against revolution (p. 25).
- Wherever Napoleon ruled he replaced custom and privilege with rational, uniform administration and law, and much of this could not be reversed (p. 26).
- The end of the European empires in the Americas was inseparable from events in Europe, and British sea power tipped the balance (pp. 27-28).
- The wars, not any long-run European superiority, shifted the global balance; by 1815 Europe's assumed superiority had for the first time a basis in reality, resting above all on British command of the seas (pp. 28-30).
- The assault on the hereditary principle fatally undermined monarchy, aristocracy, slavery and serfdom in the longer term (p. 30).
- Austria in 1815 was the most powerful state in Europe and, being unreformed, read victory as a vindication of traditional methods (pp. 31-32).
- Excluding global issues from the Vienna Settlement let imperial rivalry proceed without disturbing politics inside Europe, until the last quarter of the century (p. 35).
- Europe from 1815 to 1914 had few wars, limited in scope; battle deaths were seven times lower than in the previous century (pp. 35-36).
- The peace is best explained by the replacement of balance-of-power thinking with the collaborative Concert of Europe, itself driven by fear of revolution; but a real equilibrium, following the end of French hegemony, also counted (p. 36).
- Despite continuities of manner, the Revolution had changed the nature of sovereignty: treaties, armies and legitimacy now belonged to states, and dynastic disputes lost independent force (p. 37).
- Restoration France was an absolute monarchy with dispensable constitutional provisos, not a constitutional monarchy; yet it could not undo the land settlement, the Code or the administrative map (pp. 38-39).
- By the late 1820s the threat to the Ultras came from liberal parliamentarism, not military conspiracy (p. 41).
- In Germany repression triumphed after 1819, but representative institutions existed in most states, enlightened despotism was dead, and rule was bureaucratic and rule-bound (pp. 43-44).
- Governments' picture of a co-ordinated international conspiracy was greatly exaggerated; the secret societies were an embryonic, feeble international, mirror to Metternich's conservative one (p. 52).
- Politics had become internationalised by 1815 because nearly every country had been both occupied and occupier (p. 52).
- Liberal revolutionaries after 1815 would not enlist the common people, deterred by the memory of the Jacobin alliance with the sans-culottes and the Terror (p. 53).
- Nicholas I's regime kept dissent down despite inefficiency and corruption; its education policy unintentionally laid the ground for the intelligentsia (pp. 54-56).
- The Polish rising failed because its aristocratic leaders would not rouse the peasantry by land reform (pp. 57-59).
- In crushing Poland Nicholas broke the letter of the Vienna Settlement and upheld its anti-revolutionary thrust (p. 59).
- The Greek revolt was the gravest threat to the Concert so far; the prospect of Ottoman collapse was what pulled the Concert back together (p. 65). Greek independence was an exception; the powers still needed the Ottoman Empire (p. 68).
- The revolutionaries of the 1820s were a minority of the educated, without popular support; popular risings pursued their own interests; and the fear the revolutionaries caused helped keep the Concert together (pp. 68-69).
- The July Revolution was undramatic and Parisian but a real revolution, producing a new kind of monarchy that was no less committed to order and remained chronically unstable (pp. 71-73).
- The Belgian question was settled without Great Power war because Europe's collective rights were held to override those of single states; this is a main reason 1830 did not become a general conflict (p. 77).
- The British Reform Act belongs with the continental changes of 1830: similar issues, a similar small electorate, a similar result, but more durable (pp. 82-83).
- The conservatism of the Restoration was new: religion, tradition and historical sense set against Enlightenment reason; its extreme theorists were marginal (p. 84).
- Liberalism in the 1820s took on a nationalist tone because Napoleon had spread popular sovereignty and alien rule together (p. 85).
- The revolutions of 1830 were aftershocks of 1789, but the middle classes now feared the crowd, so results stopped at moderate constitutional reform; absolutism, not monarchy, was the casualty (pp. 85-86).
- Junior officers were the new revolutionary force of the 1820s and were displaced by the middle classes and the urban crowd by 1830 (p. 86).
- By 1830 statesmen were less willing to intervene, because revolutions no longer seemed to threaten disorder and war (pp. 86-87).
- The peasantry, decisive in 1789, was almost wholly absent in 1830 (p. 87).

## Arguments

**A. From material catastrophe to the politics of fear (pp. 13-22)**

- **Narrative fact**: death toll of about five million; 685,000 marched on Russia and fewer than 70,000 returned (p. 15); Moscow, Spanish towns and the Rhineland devastated (pp. 15-16); Tambora, April 1815, and yields in many areas down to about a quarter of normal by the end of 1816 (p. 17); famine, emigration, typhus, the last European plague (pp. 18-20); grain riots, Peterloo, Hep-Hep (pp. 20-21).
- **Interpretive steps**: (p. 21) the riots look local and political one by one, but viewed across Europe they share a cause in dearth and unemployment. (p. 21) Even the French *White Terror* prosecutions were mostly about grain, taxes and wood. (p. 21) Where students and better-off citizens joined in, governments read politics into it. (p. 22) Elites feared a repeat of 1789.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 21-22) two legacies: states accept an obligation to relieve distress; the settlement is built to prevent revolution as much as to contain France.
- **Unstated premise**: that a common Europe-wide timing shows a common cause, and that economic cause and political meaning can be ranked instead of combined. Evans's own examples (Lyon 1817, Pentrich, Breslau) show politics present.

**B. The Napoleonic legacy could not be undone (pp. 22-30)**

- **Premises**: (p. 22) The Hundred Days showed popular attachment to the Revolution's gains, especially the land settlement. (p. 23) Bonapartism became a durable political tradition. (pp. 25-26) Borders had been shown to be changeable; the Church's power cut; (p. 26) law, administration, weights, tolls, guilds and serfdom reformed across annexed and satellite territory. (p. 29) Opponents such as Prussia and Russia reformed themselves to beat Napoleon.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 26) much of the change was irreversible; restoration could only be partial.
- **Global extension**: (pp. 26-27) the wars broke the Spanish and Portuguese empires; (p. 28) the shift in global balance was contingent on the wars, not the product of a long-run process of European superiority; (p. 29) other economies had kept pace through the eighteenth century and fell behind by 1815; (p. 29) reformed European states linked military efficiency to economic growth as China and the Ottomans did not; (pp. 29-30) British sea power let Europe colonise and throttle rival manufacturing.
- **Unstated premise**: the contingency thesis (p. 28) is asserted against unnamed long-run accounts; Evans does not engage the opposing evidence, and his own later remark that western growth and military technique were already leaving the Ottomans behind in the late eighteenth century (p. 60) pulls the other way.

**C. Why 1815-1914 was peaceful (pp. 35-37)**

- **Premises**: (p. 35) Europe before 1815 was almost continuously at war. (pp. 35-36) After 1815 wars were few, short and involved few states. (p. 36) The best explanation is the abandonment of balance-of-power doctrine for collaborative institutions aimed at peace. (p. 36) Behind this lay fear of revolution, which was believed to cause international conflict. (p. 36) In addition, French hegemony was finished, by battlefield losses and stagnating population, so a rough equilibrium existed. (p. 35) Colonial questions were kept out of European politics and British maritime dominance was accepted. (p. 37) Dynastic succession no longer caused wars because sovereignty had passed to states.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 36) the Concert, underpinned by fear of revolution and an actual equilibrium, explains the long peace.
- **Note on form**: the explanation is introduced in the passive (it has been most persuasively explained) with no historian named (p. 36). Evans endorses it and then qualifies it. The argument is multi-causal and the weights are not assigned.

**D. Restoration as a new regime in old dress (pp. 37-44, 84)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 37-38) Louis XVIII used the symbols of divine right. (p. 38) He kept the land sales, the Code, careers open to talent, religious freedom, the départements, and created a two-chamber legislature. (p. 39) He retained emergency powers that could set the constitution aside; the electorate was about 90,000 in 28 million. (p. 44) In Germany, narrow and gagged assemblies nonetheless existed and rule was bureaucratic. (p. 84) The restored rulers knew the old order was gone; the trappings masked a conservatism that was new.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 37) the restored sovereigns had to adapt or perish; (p. 84) the Restoration was not the ancien régime.
- **Unstated premise**: that bureaucratic rule-bound administration is a real limit on arbitrary power (p. 44), a claim reported as what was *widely regarded* and supported only by a Bismarck remark.

**E. Why the revolts of the 1820s failed (pp. 44-54, 68-69, 86)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 45-46, 47, 50-51) The risings in Spain, Naples, Piedmont, Portugal and Russia were led by junior officers and secret societies. (p. 86) These men were politicised by the wars and sidelined in 1815. (p. 53) Outside Russia a public sphere drew in civilians, but they recoiled when the masses stirred, as in Sicily (p. 48). (p. 53) The Jacobin precedent deterred alliance with the common people. (p. 69) Where ordinary people did rise it was for their own interests. (pp. 48-49) The powers intervened (Austria in Italy, France in Spain). (p. 86) The Concert was still nervous enough to act.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 68-69) the revolutionaries shook the Vienna order nearly everywhere but lacked the support to overturn it; by the end of the 1820s it was dented but intact.
- **Corollary**: (p. 52) the conspiracies were less dangerous than the fear of them, which Metternich used to justify co-ordinated repression; (p. 69) that fear itself helped hold the Concert together.

**F. Poland and Greece as tests of the Concert (pp. 56-69)**

- **Poland**: (p. 57) the rising was triggered by Nicholas's mobilisation against the French revolution of 1830; (p. 57) aristocratic radicals refused agrarian reform; (pp. 58-59) the peasants stayed quiet and the rising stayed urban; (p. 59) no power intervened. Conclusion: isolation from the masses lost it (p. 58).
- **Greece**: (pp. 60-61) the revolt was chaotic and marked by massacre on both sides; (pp. 62-63) western opinion was mobilised by Chios and Byron; (pp. 63-65) Russian and British interests diverged; Navarino exceeded instructions; war followed; (p. 65) the threat of Ottoman collapse made agreement possible. Conclusion: the Concert held, and Greece was an exception explained by international support that Bosnian rebels lacked (p. 68).
- **Unstated premise**: that success or failure of a national rising at this date turned on Great Power interest more than on the rising's internal strength. Evans states this for Bosnia (p. 68) and implies it for Greece.

**G. 1830: aftershock of 1789 with a changed social alliance (pp. 69-87)**

- **Premises**: (p. 74) By 1830 educated liberals were numerous enough in western and central Europe for Paris to set off risings elsewhere. (pp. 70, 74, 79, 82) Economic downturn and high bread prices brought artisans onto the streets. (pp. 79-80) The middle classes feared the mob, formed militias, and used that fear to extract constitutions. (p. 77) The powers settled Belgium by conference. (pp. 86-87) Statesmen had become less willing to intervene, partly through sympathy for the Greeks, partly because inefficient absolutism was discredited and revolutions stopped short of rule by the crowd. (p. 87) The countryside was quiet.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 86) results were limited to moderate liberal constitutional reform; absolutism was destroyed almost everywhere outside the eastern empires, monarchy was not.
- **Unstated premise**: that the peasantry's absence is the main difference from 1789. This is stated, not argued; it sets up ch02.

## Evidence offered

- **Soldier's memoir**: Jakob Walter's account of the retreat from Moscow, quoted at length (pp. 13-15). A view from below, written years later by a Catholic conscript; Evans uses it to stand for the apolitical majority. He notes its transmission through the family to Kansas and to scholarship in the 1930s (p. 14). No edition named.
- **Casualty and damage statistics**: five million dead; one in five Frenchmen born 1790-95; Moscow's houses, shops and churches; Puerto Real; Extremadura (pp. 15-16). Evans gives no source. The five-million figure is called an estimate.
- **Climate and famine data**: Tambora's cloud height and audibility, Paris Observatory temperatures, crop yields, grain prices (pp. 17-18); deaths in Transylvania and Hungary (p. 19); death rates up 8 or 9 per cent in western Europe (p. 20). No source given.
- **Local chroniclers and clergy**: a Württemberg yearbook, Franciscans of Šarengrad, the parish priest of Zminj (p. 18); an Irish physician (p. 20). Local, clerical and official observers.
- **Elite observers of the poor**: Clausewitz in the Rhineland, Emperor Franz I on Lombardy, unnamed visitors to Munich and Appenzell (p. 19). The poor are described, not heard.
- **Unnamed historian**: one historical study on the plague in Bosnia, quoted without author (p. 20); *some historians* on the triumph of the ancien régime (p. 36); a historian on the severed hand in Spain (p. 78). Evans names no modern scholar anywhere in the chapter.
- **Prosecution statistics**: 2,280 White Terror prosecutions, mostly for subsistence offences (p. 21). No source. This is the chapter's best single piece of evidence for the economic reading of unrest.
- **Statesmen's letters and sayings**: Metternich (pp. 37, 51, 54, 66, 74, 81, 83), Castlereagh (pp. 32-33), Canning (p. 27), Wellington (p. 82), Carlo Felice (p. 49), Nicholas I (pp. 54, 58), Charles X (p. 69), Louis XVIII (p. 38). The perspective of rulers dominates the political sections.
- **Congress diarist**: Anna Eynard-Lullin on the balls (pp. 30-31). Aristocratic social colour.
- **Novelists and artists as evidence of mentalities**: Stendhal and Hugo on Waterloo (p. 24), Goya (p. 16), Delacroix (pp. 62, 85), Gogol (p. 55), Byron (pp. 17, 63), Heine (p. 42), Pushkin and Chopin on Poland (p. 59), Hugo and Jal on Romanticism (p. 85).
- **Population and electoral figures**: state populations in 1815 (pp. 31-32); French electorate of 90,000 against Britain's 440,000 (p. 39); French electorate nearly doubled in 1830 but still about 5 per cent of adult men (p. 71); British electorate up about 45 per cent in 1832 (p. 83). No source.
- **Comparative war record**: list of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century wars; battle death rate seven times lower after 1815 (pp. 35-36). No source for the ratio.
- **Repression counts**: Decembrist inquiry (600 examined, 121 tried, five executed) (p. 51); Poland (about 100,000 soldiers punished, 80,000 civilians transported, 254 death sentences, 5,000 estates) (p. 58); Italy (97 death sentences, most in absentia) (p. 48); purge figures for France in 1830 (p. 72); Lyon casualties (pp. 72-73).
- **Massacre reports**: George Finlay on Tripolitsa (p. 61); the mullah of Salonica (p. 61); a French-language Smyrna newspaper and population figures for Chios (p. 62). Evans balances a British philhellene witness against an Ottoman cleric, so atrocities on both sides rest on a witness from the perpetrator's own side or a sympathiser.
- **Police and official voices in Russia**: Third Department reports, a bureaucrat's *black cloud* image, Herzen's encounter with Dubbelt, Queen Victoria on Nicholas (pp. 55-56).
- **Anecdote as illustration**: the stolen teaspoons (p. 30), the eighteenth-century wig (pp. 45, 46), the Pope abolishing street lighting and vaccination (p. 46), Charles X's hat (p. 69), Kapodistrias's guarded potatoes (p. 66), the Brussels opera (p. 74). These carry interpretive weight (restoration as costume, revolution as contagion) without being argued.
- **Language outrunning evidence**: *never been in doubt* on Napoleon's defeat (p. 25); *destroyed forever* on French hegemony (p. 36); *fatally undermined* on the hereditary principle (p. 30); *gone forever* on mercenary armies (p. 37); *had no real impact of their own* on succession disputes (p. 37).

**Apparent slips in the text (book or extraction)**

- Charles X is said to recall the last Ministry of his brother Louis XIV in July 1789; Louis XVI is meant (p. 69).
- Nicholas I is said to have had himself proclaimed on 14 December 1823 and the rebels to have mustered on the Senate Square in Moscow; the context (Alexander's death in November 1825) requires 1825, and the square is in St Petersburg (p. 51).
- Fernando VII is described as restored by Napoleon after the defeat of the French armies (p. 45).
- The Battle of Asseiceira is dated May 1824 within a narrative of 1831-34 (p. 78).
- The burning of the White House is dated 1812 (p. 34).
- Louis-Napoleon's Strasbourg attempt is dated 1835 (p. 73); the Salic Law sentence ends with *female hire* (p. 76).
- These should be checked against a print copy before any is relied on.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Feeds ch02**: the closing paragraph names the peasantry as the absent force of 1830 and the countryside as the setting of most Europeans' lives, and turns to it (p. 87). The Polish and Italian failures to interest the peasantry (pp. 59, 79) and the Wallachian peasants roused by a promise of land (p. 60) are the cases to carry forward.
- **Forward pointers Evans plants** (not yet checked against later chapters): middle-class fear of the masses will reappear with serious repercussions for revolutionaries (p. 54); the internationalisation of politics will recur in stronger forms (p. 52); Thiers and Guizot will play pivotal roles (p. 40); Blanqui (p. 41), Mazzini (p. 48), Garibaldi (p. 28), Louis-Napoleon (pp. 73, 79) and Bismarck (p. 44) are introduced in passing; from 1830 nationalist movements go separate ways, with effects in the mid-century revolutions (p. 85); the reaction of 1832-33 in Germany will prove more fragile than that of 1819 (p. 81); British free-trade dominance and the separation of colonial from European politics last until the final quarter of the century (p. 35); the Ruhr's later importance for Prussia (p. 34); Russia's role as counter-revolutionary intervener lasts until mid-century (p. 33).
- **Tension (my observation)**: the long peace is attributed to the replacement of the balance of power by the Concert, and in the same paragraph to an actual equilibrium of power after French decline (p. 36). The two explanations are compatible but Evans does not say which would have sufficed alone.
- **Tension (my observation)**: unrest in 1816-19 is ranked as economic not political (p. 21), yet the chapter's conclusion is that the Revolution had permanently changed political expectations, including among ordinary people, on the evidence of a Greek bandit and the Turin chamber of commerce (p. 84). The chapter elsewhere insists ordinary people rose for their own interests and did not share liberal ideals (p. 69). How far down the political legacy of 1789 reached is left unresolved.
- **Tension (my observation)**: the shift in the global balance is said not to result from any long-term process (p. 28), but the Ottoman section cites the reorientation of trade to the Atlantic and accelerating western growth in the later eighteenth century (p. 60).
- **Tension (my observation)**: the Concert is praised as a peace-keeping institution (p. 36) and shown to be primarily an instrument for putting down liberal revolutions (pp. 36, 48-49). Evans holds both; the reader should not cite the first without the second.
- **Tension (my observation)**: Nicholas in Poland both defied and upheld the Vienna Settlement (p. 59). This depends on defining the Settlement by its anti-revolutionary purpose and not its terms, which makes almost any repressive act consistent with it.
- **Consistency of terms**: *liberal* is used for the 1789 constitutional inheritance throughout, but covers Napoleonic officers who favoured coups and centralised rule; Evans flags the uneasy fit between the Napoleonic model of a rational centralised state and representative government (p. 69). *Revolution* covers both military coups (1820s) and urban insurrection with constitutional outcome (1830); the July events are defended as a revolution by the scale of the purge and the new basis of monarchy (p. 72).

## Open questions

- Who are the historians behind the Concert-of-Europe explanation (p. 36), the plague study (p. 20) and the ancien-régime-triumphant view (p. 36)? The book has no notes; check the bibliography or further-reading section.
- What is the source and method for the claim that battle deaths in 1815-1914 were seven times lower than in the previous century (p. 36), and for the five-million death toll (p. 15)? Is the comparison per capita?
