# ch06: The Age of Emotion (pp. 407–489)

## Question this chapter answers

How did Europeans between 1815 and 1914 feel, believe, learn, identify themselves and amuse themselves, and how did those habits change? Evans's answer is a cultural history organised around emotion. Romanticism made feeling the test of truth; that opened the door to a religious revival, to nationalism built on language and an imagined past, and to an idea of the sexes as reason versus feeling. Across the century each of these was disciplined, institutionalised or hardened: churches fought states and lost the urban masses without Europe ceasing to be religious; local identities were schooled into national ones; cultural stereotypes turned into racial ones; tears became unmanly; village culture was replaced by commercial mass leisure; and the arts moved from Romanticism through Realism to a modernism that, by 1913, was breaking the surface of a complacent culture just before the war did.

The chapter is survey history. Its thesis is carried by arrangement and by transition sentences more than by sustained argument, so the note marks where Evans is narrating and where he is interpreting.

## Section guide

- **From the Present to the Past (pp. 407-416).** Opens with the Swedish novelist Fredrika Bremer as a life that joins the two currents of the early century, Romantic emotionalism and Biedermeier domesticity. Then Romanticism in music, painting and the novel, its political edge, the turn to the past (Scott, Ranke, Bach revival, neo-Gothic) and the later shift from *improving* the past to preserving it.
- **Romanticism and Religion (pp. 416-423).** The emotional revival of religion: Ultramontane Catholicism, Marian apparitions (Lourdes, Marpingen), Tractarians, Nonconformity, Protestant *awakening*, Russian Orthodoxy as arm of autocracy, national Orthodox churches in Greece and Bulgaria.
- **Dissent, Doubt and Disbelief (pp. 423-431).** Church against State (Italy, the Syllabus and Infallibility, French laicisation, the Kulturkampf, Austria, Belgium, the Netherlands, Spain); secularisation of the urban masses and its limits; the feminisation of piety; missions; intellectual challenges from Strauss, Nietzsche, Kierkegaard, Lyell, Chambers and Darwin.
- **Christianity and Beyond (pp. 431-439).** Rural magic and its decline; Islam in the Ottoman and Balkan lands; Jewish demography, discrimination, emancipation, assimilation, occupations, Zionism; the shift from religious hostility to racial antisemitism (Marr, Stöcker, Lueger, Schönerer, the Protocols).
- **Climbing the Tower of Babel (pp. 439-447).** Literacy, localism and linguistic patchwork; compulsory primary schooling as nation-builder; nationalists standardising or inventing languages and epics; the shift from a shared ladder of progress to innate racial character; Russification and Germanisation; Esperanto's failure.
- **The Passion for Knowledge (pp. 447-455).** Secondary schooling (Gymnasium, lycée, English public schools), universities (the Humboldtian model and its imitators, state control), the professionalisation of science, positivism, and Rankean history as nationalism in scientific dress.
- **The Gendering of Emotion (pp. 455-465).** Reason coded male and feeling female; male tears go out of fashion; manliness, beards and top hats; women's legal disabilities under the Code Napoléon and other codes; access to professions and universities; organised feminism, its middle-class limits and the terms on which it won.
- **The Pursuit of Happiness (pp. 465-474).** Folk song and dance give way to urban leisure; the state and moralists against popular culture; drink and temperance; clubs and football; the popular press, cheap fiction, music hall, cabaret, dance halls, the gramophone, photography and cinema.
- **Realism and Nationalism (pp. 474-482).** Realism in painting, sculpture, the novel, theatre and opera, tied to industrialisation and a new middle-class book market; its coincidence with cultural nationalism in literature, painting and music.
- **Rites of Spring (pp. 482-489).** Late Romantic music and the *war of the Romantics*; Impressionism, Symbolism, Aestheticism, Post-Impressionism, Fauvism, Expressionism, abstraction, Cubism, Futurism; folk revival and primitivism; atonality; the riot at *The Rite of Spring* (29 May 1913) as curtain-raiser to 1914.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biedermeier | p. 410 | Central European culture of 1815-48 that, under Metternich's repression of open political discourse, turned inward to home, family, nature and depoliticised country life; also the plain, affordable furniture style that appealed to the new middle classes. Evans treats it as one of two main currents of the age, with Romantic emotionalism (p. 409). |
| Romanticism | p. 411 | The artistic movement that reacted against Enlightenment rationalism by emphasising the emotions, the exotic and the wild; feeling rather than intellect as the source of truth (p. 412). Early Romantic emotion is predominantly inward and private (p. 414). |
| Cult of the genius / virtuoso | p. 411 | The obverse of Biedermeier domesticity: the performer or artist whose gifts are beyond ordinary people (Paganini, Liszt), and the suffering genius (Beethoven) (p. 412). |
| *Improvement* of the past | p. 415 | Evans's ironic label for nineteenth-century adaptation of old music and buildings to current taste (Mendelssohn's cut Bach, Viollet-le-Duc's gargoyles); later displaced by heritage preservation and scholarly authenticity (p. 416). |
| Ultramontanes | p. 416 | Catholic clergy and laity north of the Alps who, freed from aristocratic patrons, looked to Rome; militant, anti-revolutionary, and mobilised through new emotional devotions (Immaculate Conception, Sacred Heart, pilgrimage) (pp. 416-417). |
| Awakening | p. 420 | Protestant revival movements that reacted both to Enlightenment rationalism and to state-sponsored quasi-Catholic ritual: itinerant preaching, Bible, private prayer, strict morals. |
| Kulturkampf | p. 425 | The German liberals' name, *struggle of civilizations*, for Bismarck's 1871-5 legislation against the Catholic Church; Evans presents it as one case of a Europe-wide Church-State conflict in new or restructured states. |
| Secularization of the masses | p. 426 | Loss of the habit of worship among urban workers, driven largely by migration and the shortage of churches and clergy in cities; not the same as unbelief, since rites of passage stayed religious (pp. 427-428). |
| Disenchantment of the world | p. 429 | Weber's term, adopted by Evans: replacement of a magical view of nature by goal-oriented, rational, scientific belief; at first confined to parts of the urban working class and the liberal bourgeoisie. |
| Scientific materialism | p. 430 | The most serious intellectual challenge to Christianity: geology (Lyell), Chambers's *Vestiges*, Darwin. |
| Emancipation (Jewish) | p. 435 | Grant of civil equality to Jews, state by state from 1791 to 1923; judged a triumph of social emancipation from the vantage point of 1900, though incomplete (p. 439). |
| Antisemitism | p. 437 | A new and more virulent prejudice based on supposedly scientific doctrines of racial difference, growing out of older Christian hostility; the term coined by Steinschneider and popularised by Marr (1879). Politically limited before 1914 in Germany, instrumentalised in Austria. |
| Locality versus nationality | p. 440 | Before mass literacy, identity was rooted in village, valley and region, marked by dialect; national consciousness was largely absent. |
| Standard language | p. 444 | A dialect chosen (or a language constructed) by nationalists to justify claims to nationhood and statehood: Tuscan, West Bulgarian, Serbo-Croat, Nynorsk, Katharevousa. |
| Progress (shared road) | p. 446 | The early-century assumption, common to nationalists and their critics, that all European nations were travelling the same road to civilisation at different speeds; replaced later by innate, unalterable racial character (pp. 446-447). |
| Humboldtian university | p. 451 | Integration of teaching and research, freedom to teach and learn, study for its own sake; in practice vocational and state-controlled for much of the century, but the model widely imitated. |
| Positivism | p. 454 | Comte's doctrine that scientific observation is the only legitimate basis for action; extended to history as a claim to completeness and certainty (p. 455). |
| Gendered emotion | p. 455 | The nineteenth-century doctrine, absent or marginal in earlier encyclopedias, that men think and women feel; used to justify legal minority and exclusion from public life. |
| Manliness | p. 456 | Key term of approval in the second half of the century; impassivity, expressed in beards, dark clothes, clubs and the cult of the hero. |
| Public sphere | p. 456 | The world of politics, press, clubs and associations, assumed to be male; women enter it through philanthropy, writing and feminist campaigning (pp. 461, 463). |
| Feminism | p. 459 | Organised movements, mostly from the 1860s and 1870s, for legal, educational and property rights; almost exclusively middle-class until the late century (p. 463). |
| Realism | p. 475 | Art and fiction addressing contemporary life in a true-to-life manner, predominant after mid-century; called forth above all by industrialisation (p. 476); still an appeal to emotion (p. 478). |
| Verismo | p. 479 | Italian literary and operatic Realism: the actual lives of the people rather than historical or fantastic subjects. |
| Primitivism | p. 487 | Use of non-European and folk forms left raw (Picasso and African art, Bartók, Stravinsky) to break with representational and tonal convention; the disruptive side of the folk revival. |
| Modernism / modernist | p. 488 | Evans's umbrella for the pre-1914 movements that separated art from representation and music from tonality and shook the prewar cultural world (p. 489). |

## Leading propositions

Interpretive claims (what Evans is trying to prove):

- The culture of the early nineteenth century is defined by two linked currents, Romantic emotionalism and inward-turning domesticity, the second partly a product of political repression (pp. 409-410).
- Romanticism made feeling, not intellect, the source of truth and authenticity, and its emotion was at first private rather than public, though it carried a political note through Byron's influence (pp. 412-414).
- The search for emotional authenticity sent artists and scholars back past the Enlightenment to earlier ages; the century first remade the past to its own taste and only later joined national heritage to historical authenticity (pp. 414-416).
- By putting emotion first, Romanticism let religion back into the cultural mainstream; the weakening of the Church's secular power in the revolutionary wars helped by turning clergy toward Rome (p. 416).
- Religious revival across the confessions was ultimately a reaction to the Enlightenment and to French revolutionary anticlericalism (p. 419), and popular religious emotion repeatedly escaped the control of both Church and State (p. 417).
- Religion fused with nationality wherever a people was ruled by a state of another confession: Ireland, Poland, Finland, Bulgaria (pp. 418-420, 422-423).
- Revived piety and Church-State conflict were intimately connected; new or restructured states (Italy, the Third Republic, the German Empire) tried to bind Catholic subjects to themselves at the moment Pius IX was binding them to the papacy (pp. 423-424).
- The secularisation of the masses was potentially more serious for the Churches than state hostility, but the contrast between pious countryside and godless town is easily overdrawn, and Europe stayed overwhelmingly religious until 1914 (pp. 426-428).
- Secularisation was gendered: men left, women stayed, and anticlericals read this as female emotionalism, which shaped French Republican resistance to votes for women (p. 428).
- Scientific materialism was the gravest intellectual challenge to Christianity, yet most scientists thought their findings compatible with it (pp. 430-431).
- Racial antisemitism grew from a Christian tradition of hostility but was something new; before 1914 its political effect in Germany was limited, and Jewish civil equality was, on balance, the century's result (pp. 437-439).
- Exclusive, aggressive nationalism depended on national identities built on written language, which depended on literacy and schooling; before these, identity was local (pp. 439-441).
- Primary education was a deliberate instrument of social control, anticlericalism and national unification (pp. 442-443).
- Later in the century cultural stereotypes gave way to racial ones and the idea of a common road of progress to the idea of fixed national character, licensing assimilation policies (pp. 446-447).
- More schooling, travel, tourism and translation did not produce international understanding (p. 447).
- Higher education and science professionalised, with Germany as the model; but the claim of scientific objectivity in history was a cloak for nationalist commitment (pp. 453-455).
- Emotion was gendered in the nineteenth century in a way it had not been before; in the second half of the century public emotion became unmanly, and the beard is best read as a reaction to feminism (pp. 455-456, 459).
- Women's rights nonetheless improved gradually, and feminists won on the argument from women's distinctive moral feeling rather than equal individual rights, with the ironic result that women too learned emotional restraint (pp. 461, 464-465).
- Industrialisation and urbanisation destroyed rural popular culture with the help of state, church and school, and replaced it with drink, then with organised and commercial leisure that authorities tried to police (pp. 465-474).
- Industrialisation above all called forth the Realist novel; a growing middle-class market sustained it; and Realism, like Romanticism, worked by appeal to the emotions (pp. 476-478).
- The turn to Realism coincided, not by accident, with cultural nationalism (p. 480).
- Modernism separated art from representation and music from tonality and had already shaken the prewar cultural world before 1914 (pp. 485, 489).

## Arguments

- **Romanticism to religious revival.**
  **Premises**: The Enlightenment subordinated emotion to intellect; Romanticism reversed this and made feeling the ground of truth (p. 412). The revolutionary and Napoleonic wars cut the Church's secular power and freed parish clergy from aristocratic patrons (p. 416). New devotions and Protestant revival meetings worked through emotional mobilisation (pp. 417, 419).
  **Conclusion**: Religion could escape Enlightenment scorn and re-enter the mainstream, in forms that were emotional, popular and hard for institutions to control (pp. 416-417). Evans asserts the link between Romantic aesthetics and popular piety by parallel, not by showing transmission; Marpingen and Trier are offered as demonstrations of strength, not of Romantic influence.

- **Revival to Church-State conflict.**
  **Premises**: Pius IX, losing temporal power, answered with the Syllabus (1864) and Infallibility (1870), claiming authority over marriage, schooling and conscience (pp. 423-424). New states claimed the same ground: Italy dissolved 38,000 ecclesiastical bodies; Ferry laicised French schooling; Bismarck passed the May Laws (pp. 423-425).
  **Conclusion**: Two centralising authorities competed for the loyalty of the same Catholic populations, so conflict was structural (p. 424). The outcome in Germany and Austria was not a weaker Catholicism but mass Catholic parties (p. 425).

- **Secularisation is real but bounded.**
  **Premises**: Urban attendance figures were low across Europe and the ratio of clergy to people collapsed in big cities (p. 426). But rural observance was also poor (p. 427), and urban workers, even Social Democrats, kept baptism, church marriage and religious burial (pp. 427-428). Missions expanded as never before (p. 428).
  **Conclusion**: Observance declined, belonging did not; Europe remained an overwhelmingly religious culture to 1914 (p. 428). Evans's causal emphasis is on supply (churches, pastors, mobility) rather than on loss of belief (p. 426), with intellectual disbelief treated as a separate, narrower current (pp. 429-431).

- **From Christian hostility to racial antisemitism.**
  **Premises**: Jews were concentrated by long legal exclusion in finance, trade and professions (p. 436). Emancipation proceeded state by state and brought conversion and intermarriage (pp. 435-436). Christian hostility supplied a tradition from which a prejudice based on supposed racial science emerged (p. 437). Politicians (Stöcker, Lueger, Schönerer) used it to court peasants and the lower middle class with mixed success (pp. 437-438).
  **Conclusion**: Racial antisemitism was a warning note, not yet a dominant force; from 1900 the achievement that stood out was civil equality (p. 439). The forward references to Hitler (p. 438) and to the Protocols' later impact (p. 438) pull against this deliberately contemporary vantage point.

- **Literacy, schooling and the making of nations.**
  **Premises**: Literacy was patchy and identity local, with dialects and multiple languages in the same town (pp. 439-441). States made primary schooling compulsory to tame the masses, displace the Church and teach the national language and patriotism (pp. 442-443). Nationalists chose or built standard languages and ancient heritages (pp. 444-445).
  **Conclusion**: National identity was constructed through written language and schooling, and this was the precondition for the more exclusive nationalism of the later century (p. 439). Evans adds a qualification: language was not always the basis of statehood (Switzerland, Belgium, the Water Poles) (p. 445).

- **From progress to race.**
  **Premises**: Early-century observers, however prejudiced, ranked nations on one road of progress (pp. 445-446). After 1863 Russian voices called for merging Polish identity; Ukraine's literature was treated as separatism; Prussia restricted Polish (p. 447).
  **Conclusion**: Belief in innate national character replaced belief in convergence, and denser contact did not produce understanding (pp. 446-447).

- **Objectivity as commitment.**
  **Premises**: Positivism promised complete knowledge through scientific method (pp. 454-455). Ranke's task of finding the leading currents of each age let his successors name Prussia's rise as the current of theirs (p. 455).
  **Conclusion**: Rankean history served a political purpose while claiming detachment (p. 455). This is the chapter's sharpest historiographical judgment, and it bears on Evans's own practice.

- **Gender and emotion.**
  **Premises**: Mid-century reference works newly defined women as feeling and men as thinking (p. 455). Men wept freely in the Romantic era; after mid-century tears signalled weakness or degeneracy (p. 456). Parliamentary public life assumed male rationality (p. 456). Feminism appeared in the 1860s-70s (p. 463).
  **Conclusion**: Beards, top hats and impassivity were a performance of masculinity in reaction to women's entry into public life (p. 459); feminists accepted the premise of female feeling and turned it into an argument for inclusion, and ended by adopting restraint themselves (pp. 464-465). The beard thesis is hedged by Evans himself with *perhaps*, and he offers chronology (Chartists, 1848, Crimea) that fits other explanations too (p. 458).

- **Leisure and discipline.**
  **Premises**: Folk culture was tied to work and the ritual year (p. 465). Railways, schools, priests, enclosure and police eroded it (pp. 465-466). In the new towns drink filled the gap (p. 466). Alternatives arose: tea, clubs, football, the press, cheap books, music hall, cinema (pp. 467-474).
  **Conclusion**: Popular culture was commercialised and nationalised, and each new form drew middle-class moral alarm and state regulation (pp. 471, 473-474).

- **Realism, nationalism, modernism.**
  **Premises**: Photography and industrial society pushed art toward contemporary life (pp. 474-476). The same decades saw literature, painting and music enlisted for the nation (pp. 480-482). The camera then undermined representation itself (p. 483); Symbolists rejected both representation and the conscription of art into nationalism (p. 485).
  **Conclusion**: Art detached itself from representation and convention, and its primitivism broke through the smooth surface of European culture a year before the war (pp. 485, 489). The link between artistic rupture and the coming war is rhetorical juxtaposition, not causal argument.

## Evidence offered

Evans gives no footnotes; sources below are those he names in the text. Where none is named, Evans gives no source.

- **Exemplary life.** Fredrika Bremer's biography and novels, quoted at length, used to join Romanticism, domesticity, women's legal minority and later feminism; she returns at pp. 429 and 461 (pp. 407-409). Evans gives no source for the biographical detail.
- **Works of art as evidence.** Titles, dates and brief descriptions of paintings, novels, operas and symphonies carry most of the sections on culture (pp. 410-416, 474-489). These establish chronology and theme, not reception, except where a riot or a rejection is reported (pp. 476, 480, 483, 488).
- **Contemporary voices, mostly elite.** Heine on Lisztomania (p. 411); Berlioz's memoirs (p. 411); Goethe on Scott and Bach (pp. 414-415); Shaftesbury on Strauss (p. 429); an English bishop in 1852 on migrants losing the habit of worship (p. 426); Isabella Sidgwick's recollection of the Huxley-Wilberforce debate, which itself admits nobody remembered the words (p. 431); Mayhew, Sarah Austin, Custine, Gautier and Iorga on national character (pp. 445-446); Göhre the worker-observer (pp. 472-473). These are educated outsiders describing peasants, workers or foreigners.
- **Officials and inspectors.** A French school inspector in the Lozère in 1864 whose pupils did not know their country (p. 440); an Aude inspector in 1864 on lewd songs replaced by patriotic choirs (pp. 465-466); a lycée inspector in 1877 (p. 449); a French curé on rowdy congregations (p. 427); police on a Cantal newspaper (p. 469); Valuev to the tsar on Ukrainian literature (p. 447). The perspective is that of the agencies doing the disciplining.
- **Religious statistics.** Trier pilgrimage, half a million (p. 417); Lourdes 1876, 100,000 (p. 417); Methodists 489,000 by 1850 (p. 419); 5,343 of 7,782 Prussian congregations using the royal liturgy by 1825 (p. 419); Moravian growth in Courland and Estonia (p. 420); Kulturkampf toll of 989 vacant parishes and 225 priests jailed (p. 425); the 1851 British religious census, under 10 per cent attendance in most large towns (p. 426); pastor ratios in Hamburg and Berlin (p. 426); Orléans Easter communion 10.6 per cent, 11.6 per cent of women against 2 per cent of men (pp. 427-428); Berlin baptisms and Saxon church marriages and funerals (p. 428); nuns in Russia 8,533 to 70,453 (p. 428).
- **Jewish demography and occupation.** About nine million Jews in Europe, 5.2 million in Russia (p. 433); pogrom casualties 1881 and 1903-6 (p. 434); emancipation dates by state (p. 435); conversion and intermarriage rates (pp. 435-436); Budapest 1910 census shares in finance, trade, law and medicine (p. 436).
- **Literacy and schooling.** Signature literacy by country and sex (pp. 439-440); Tuscan used by 2.5 per cent of Italians in 1860 (p. 444); school counts in Britain, Spain and Russia (pp. 442-443); secondary and university enrolments in Prussia, France, Spain and Germany (pp. 448-451); under 3 per cent of French boys in any secondary school (p. 448); German student subject shares 1830-60 (p. 451).
- **Law.** Provisions of the Code Napoléon, the Russian Code of 1836, the German Civil Code of 1900, the Matrimonial Causes Act 1857 and Married Women's Property Acts (pp. 460, 462-463); fiction (Anna Karenina, The Mayor of Casterbridge) used to illustrate legal effects (pp. 460, 464).
- **Leisure.** Bars per head in the Nord, absinthe and vodka revenue, Norwegian and British consumption falls (pp. 466-468); Lille club counts (p. 468); newspaper circulations (pp. 469-470); music halls in London (p. 471); temperance theatre performances in Russia (p. 472); cinemas in Barcelona and Spain (p. 474); book titles per year in Britain and Russia (p. 477).
- **Named scholars.** Very few. Weber's disenchantment (p. 429) and Tönnies (p. 456) are used as period witnesses as much as authorities. No modern historian is named in the chapter, though the Marpingen and French-peasant material plainly rests on specialist monographs.
- **Kind of evidence overall.** Breadth over depth: one or two instances per country, strongest on Britain, France, Germany and Russia, with Scandinavia unusually well represented. Statistics are given without provenance or definition. Anecdote does much of the persuasive work (the fourth place laid for Christ, p. 420; the Etonian's painted back, p. 449; the Rite riot, pp. 488-489).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Back to ch05.** The chapter opens mid-sentence from ch05's close on the representation of nature (p. 407); miasma theory, railways, department stores and steam presses from the earlier material reappear as causes of cultural change (pp. 457, 459, 465, 477).
- **Back to ch01-ch03.** Metternich's repression as the cause of Biedermeier inwardness (p. 410), the Decembrists and Greek independence (pp. 413-414), and 1848 as the hinge for beards, press freedom and Jewish emancipation (pp. 435, 458, 469) all assume the political narrative of the first three chapters. Feminism, marginal in 1848, is here given its organised form from the 1860s (p. 463).
- **Forward to ch07.** Evans flags the Dreyfus affair for Chapter 7 (p. 439); the next chapter opens with Emmeline Pankhurst (p. 489), picking up women's suffrage, which this chapter mentions only in passing (pp. 461, 463). Catholic Centre and Christian Social parties (pp. 425, 437) and the mass press (pp. 468-470) are groundwork for mass politics.
- **Forward to ch08.** Missions riding on European global hegemony (pp. 428-429), African art entering Europe through empire (p. 487), the shift from cultural to racial thinking (pp. 446-447) and the closing image of barbarity (p. 489) prepare the chapter on empire and the road to war.
- **Tension: secularisation.** Evans stresses both the long-run seriousness of mass secularisation (p. 426) and the persistence of an overwhelmingly religious culture (p. 428). The reconciliation offered is the distinction between observance and rites of passage, but he does not say which mattered more for politics.
- **Tension: antisemitism.** The verdict that antisemitism had limited effect before 1914 and that emancipation was a triumph (pp. 437, 439) sits beside pogroms killing some 2,000 in 1903-6 and state-encouraged persecution in Russia (p. 434), and beside teleological pointers to Hitler (p. 438). The optimistic verdict is geographically a western and central European one.
- **Tension: emotion as organising idea.** The title theme fits Romanticism, revival and gender well. It is stretched to cover Realism (appeal to emotion, p. 478), schooling (Ferry on teaching the heart, p. 442) and modernism (Bell on what a picture makes us feel, p. 486), and is absent from much of the material on universities, science and leisure. Emotion is a thread, not a demonstrated cause.
- **Tension: objectivity.** Evans's exposure of Rankean objectivity as partisanship (p. 455) invites the same question of a synthesis that offers confident judgments with no apparatus.
- **Tension: progress.** The chapter records advances (literacy, women's rights, emancipation, science) in a progressive register while arguing that contemporaries' own faith in a common road of progress was being displaced by race (pp. 446-447).

## Open questions

- What is the mechanism linking Romantic high culture to mass piety? Evans states that Romanticism opened the way for religion (p. 416) but the popular movements he describes (Marpingen, Methodism, the awakening) have their own social causes. Is this more than analogy?
- How should the two secularisation claims be weighed for the politics of ch07: did unchurched workers who still baptised their children behave as religious or secular voters (pp. 427-428)?
- The beard as reaction to feminism (p. 459): the fashion begins around mid-century and is dated to the collapse of Chartism and 1848 (p. 458), while organised feminism is dated to the 1860s and 1870s (p. 463). Does the chronology support the claim?
- Evans says feminists won the argument on women's moral difference (p. 464). Won where and by when? The legal record he gives is mixed, and the German Civil Code of 1900 went backwards (p. 460).
- What evidence supports the claim that contact, tourism and translation failed to produce understanding (p. 447) beyond travellers' complaints?
- How representative are the inspectors' and moralists' reports on which the account of popular culture rests (pp. 465-466, 471-473)? The voices of peasants and workers appear only in proverbs and song lines.
- Possible slips to verify before relying on details (the text is quoted as it stands in the chapter file): Hauge is presented as spearheading awakening across Protestant Germany (p. 420), though he was Norwegian; Gaj is said to have switched to the Slovak dialect (p. 444), where Štokavian would be expected; Balzac's series is titled La Condition humaine (p. 476) rather than La Comédie humaine; Nietzsche is said to have influenced Brandes's 1873 lectures (p. 429), which is chronologically doubtful; A. C. Milan's founder is spelled Klipin (p. 468).
- Syntopical check: for the secularisation and Marian revival material, a specialist account (for example on Marpingen or on French rural religion) would test how far Evans's summary flattens the debate.
