# ch05: The Conquest of Nature (pp. 327–407)

## Question this chapter answers

How far did nineteenth-century Europeans extend their power over nature, meaning wild animals and landscapes, the elements, distance and time, disease, death and pain, and finally human nature itself (sexuality, madness, crime), and what did that extension cost? Evans's answer is a qualified one. External nature was largely subdued, though with damaging side effects; space and time were reorganised and standardised, mainly by the railway and the telegraph; mortality fell sharply, but more through sanitation, diet and administration than through medicine; and the attempt to control human nature through asylums, prisons, police and the regulation of sex largely failed, ending in hereditarian pessimism. The conquest "ran up against the limits of the possible" (p. 406), opening possibilities for some people and closing them for others.

## Section guide

- **Taming the Wild (pp. 327–336)**: opens with the life of the Hungarian liberal Bertalan Szemere, then covers uncultivated land, wolves and bears, menageries and zoos, cruel sports, aristocratic mass hunting, wild gardens, and extinctions. Sets up the theme of mastery and its ironies.
- **Mastering the Elements (pp. 336–345)**: mountains, forests and deforestation, land reclamation, floods and river engineering (the Rhine), earthquakes and volcanoes, urban and rural fires and fire brigades, coal smoke and fog. Shows partial control plus unwelcome consequences.
- **The Shrinkage of Space (pp. 345–357)**: local worlds and local measures, the metric system, railway expansion, postal services, the motor car, the bicycle, lifts and tall buildings, balloons, airships, aeroplanes, astronomy and science fiction.
- **The Making of Modern Time (pp. 357–363)**: clocks and watches, railway time, the telegraph and telephone, the 1884 Washington conference, Franco-British rivalry over the meridian, linear time and progress, geological time, the cult of speed, cinema and relativity.
- **The End of the Great Plagues (pp. 363–370)**: malaria, tuberculosis, smallpox, typhoid, plague, typhus and cholera. Argues that prevention and living standards, not cures, drove the decline.
- **Out of the Shadow of Death (pp. 370–375)**: mortality and life expectancy statistics, the public culture of death and mourning, rural death customs, cremation, spiritualism, and the growing urban denial of death.
- **Controlling the Primal Urge (pp. 375–384)**: the fall in birth rates, contraception and abstinence, prudery, prostitution and its regulation, syphilis, the criminalisation of male homosexuality, decadence and sexology.
- **The Management of Pain (pp. 384–388)**: mesmerism, anaesthetics, antisepsis (Semmelweis, Lister), the changing hospital, nursing, diagnostic instruments, and the limits of cure before antibiotics.
- **Madness and Civilization (pp. 388–396)**: phrenology, the reforming asylum, the social definition of insanity by class and gender, overcrowding, and the turn to hereditary explanations.
- **Discipline and Punish (pp. 396–407)**: public executions and their abolition, the decline of capital and corporal punishment, transportation, the penitentiary and its failure, Lombroso and criminal heredity, the growth of gendarmeries and urban police, and the chapter's summary.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conquest of nature | p. 345 | The extension of human control over the wilderness and its animals, the elements, the *fundamental factors that framed life and experience* (space, time), and, in the later sections, the human body and human nature. Always qualified: it met limits and produced unwelcome consequences (p. 406). |
| Nature / state of nature | pp. 329–330 | Both the non-human world and a condition attributed to supposedly backward peoples (the Hungarians, in the opening). Evans plays the two senses against each other, including a person's own nature (p. 329). |
| Mastery over nature | p. 332 | What animal shows, zoos, hunting and mountaineering displayed to an urban public that had lost contact with the wild. |
| Shrinkage of space | p. 345 | The reduction of effective distance by railways, steamships, post, telegraph, cars, bicycles, lifts and flight; includes the standardisation of how space is measured. |
| Standardisation | p. 346 | Replacement of local measures, local times and incompatible rules with uniform national and international ones (metre, railway gauge and regulations, postal union, time zones), driven by trade, railways, telegraphs and armies. |
| Standard time / modern time | pp. 358–359 | Uniform clock time set nationally and then by the Greenwich meridian, replacing local solar time; more broadly a time that is uniform, linear and organised, and by 1914 also hurried and beginning to seem malleable (pp. 361–363). |
| Speed | p. 362 | The felt acceleration of life in the later century, quoted from W. R. Greg and Frederic Harrison; expressed in railway races, tea clippers and transatlantic liners. |
| Sanitary revolution | p. 369 | The provision of clean water and proper sewage disposal in European cities in the second half of the century; for Evans a more important cause of falling epidemic disease than medical discovery. |
| Social medicine | p. 367 | The approach exemplified by Virchow's 1848 typhus report: disease explained by deprivation and lack of rights, not by national character. |
| Germ theory of disease | p. 369 | The bacteriological explanation associated with Pasteur and Koch; it supplied the scientific basis for antisepsis (p. 386) but before 1914 did little to cure. |
| Demographic transition | p. 375 | The fall in death rates followed, at an interval, by a corresponding fall in birth rates; Evans attributes the phrase to historians. |
| Victorian prudery | p. 377 | Not mere squeamishness but, in Evans's reading, a reinforcement of female sexual abstinence at a time when abstinence was the only reliable contraceptive. |
| Regulation (of prostitution) | p. 379 | Police registration, compulsory medical inspection and confinement of prostitutes, justified as protecting male clients from disease; *neo-reglementarism* names its tightened French form after 1906 (p. 381). |
| Social purity | p. 380 | The campaigning current (Butler, the morality societies, later the Suffragettes) that targeted male lust and the double standard, and that also underlay the criminalisation of male homosexuality (pp. 381–382). |
| Antisepsis | p. 387 | Lister's practice of preventing wound infection with carbolic acid, handwashing and dressings; with asepsis, slow to become routine. |
| Lunatic asylum / mental hospital | p. 389 | A separate, medically supervised institution for the insane, product of Enlightenment rationalism and reforming optimism; by the end of the century a place of custody for the deviant (p. 396). |
| Deviance | p. 395 | Behaviour outside bourgeois norms (alcoholism, petty crime, breaches of gender expectations) that later-century psychiatry and criminology redefined as inherited disorder. |
| Penitentiary | p. 400 | The purpose-built reforming prison on the Philadelphia model: isolation, hard labour, religion and surveillance meant to remould character. |
| Atavism | p. 403 | Lombroso's idea that born criminals are throwbacks to an earlier stage of evolution; little accepted in that form, but the broader idea of inherited criminality spread. |
| Gendarmerie | p. 404 | The French-style mounted, semi-military rural police, Evans's most important instrument of restored state order after 1815. |

## Leading propositions

- In the nineteenth century nature was a dominating presence across much of Europe, not only on its supposedly backward margins: in 1815 some 55 per cent of Prussia was mountain, forest and wilderness (p. 330).
- Population growth drove a large expansion of cultivated land, and hunting drove wolves, bears and other animals out of most of western and central Europe (pp. 330–331).
- As town-dwellers lost contact with the wild, they recreated it in zoos, shows, toys and gardens, and these displays advertised human, national and imperial mastery (pp. 331–333, 335).
- Bourgeois opinion moved against cruelty to animals, while elite hunting grew into organised mass slaughter; the idea of preserving wild nature had not yet taken root (pp. 333–336).
- Europe underwent one of the largest deforestations to that date, with recognised effects on soil, climate, floods and landslides; only coal, iron and steel slowed it late in the century (pp. 338–339).
- River engineering, reclamation and fire prevention reduced some hazards; earthquakes and eruptions remained beyond control; smoke pollution grew worse and was left unchecked for political and economic reasons (pp. 340–345).
- Most Europeans lived in highly local worlds for most of the century, a fact expressed in the diversity of local weights and measures (pp. 345–346).
- Railway-building in the second half of the century was the main force behind standardisation, of measures (p. 347) and of time (p. 358), and it carried the state, the market and print into remote places while destroying cottage industry and drawing young men away (p. 349).
- Railways, steamships and the telegraph were the technological underpinnings of European overseas empire (p. 348).
- New transport was socially coded: the branch line democratised travel, the motor car reversed that for the wealthy, the bicycle was more democratic and a vehicle of female emancipation (pp. 349, 351–352).
- New technologies of flight and communication were quickly turned to military and national-prestige uses (pp. 355–356, 359, 361).
- Uniform linear time underpinned the idea of progress, and the past came to seem both different and vastly longer (pp. 361–362); by the eve of 1914 time had also come to feel hurried and uncertain (pp. 362–363).
- The great epidemic diseases retreated, but mainly through drainage, better diet, hygiene, clean water, sewerage, vaccination and border controls; direct medical intervention did little to prevent and less to cure (pp. 364–365, 369).
- Cholera was a product of European expansion and industrialisation, and its epidemics tracked war and revolution (p. 368).
- Death rates fell and life expectancy rose across most of Europe, with Russia and Spain lagging (p. 371); as death became rarer among the urban middle and upper classes they lost the ability to cope with it and began to deny it (pp. 373, 375).
- Birth rates then fell too, and since contraception was ineffective this was achieved chiefly by abstinence, borne by women and enforced by prudery (pp. 376–377); the motive was the changed economics of children (pp. 377–378).
- The sexual regime rested on a double standard: female sexuality was denied, male lust was assumed, prostitution was regulated to protect men, and the purity campaign against male lust also produced the criminalisation of male homosexuality (pp. 379–382).
- Anaesthesia and antisepsis transformed surgery and the standing of hospitals and doctors, but most diseases stayed incurable until antibiotics (pp. 387–388).
- Asylums grew because of changing social norms and the growing reach of the state more than because of medical advance; class and gender norms shaped diagnosis and treatment (pp. 391–392).
- The reforming asylum and the reforming prison both failed under overcrowding and underfunding, and both gave way to hereditarian theories (Kraepelin, Lombroso) that recast deviance as inborn (pp. 393–396, 402–404).
- What underlay the changes in punishment was the growing power of the state to enforce the law (p. 404); even so, efforts to control or reform the deviant met with only limited success (p. 406).
- The conquest of nature opened possibilities for women, travellers and the military and cut them off for the deviant, the marginal and the mentally disturbed; it also challenged religious belief and artistic representation, which is the bridge to the next chapter (pp. 406–407).

## Arguments

**A. From feared wilderness to exhibited nature (pp. 327–336)**

- **Premises**: (p. 330) Much of Europe was uncultivated and wolves killed people in numbers. (pp. 330–331) Cultivation and hunting pushed large predators to the margins. (pp. 331–332) Urban sympathy for the bear grew as the animal ceased to be a threat, ending in the teddy bear. (pp. 332–333) Menageries became public zoos with scientific, commercial and imperial purposes. (pp. 334–335) Elite hunting became a staged, industrial-scale activity.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 332, 335) Urbanisation removed familiarity with the wild and replaced it with displays whose message was mastery; sentiment about animals and destruction of animals rose together, and conservation was absent (pp. 335–336).
- **Framing device**: Szemere defended Hungarian closeness to nature as a source of freedom while fellow liberals read it as backwardness (p. 328); he ended unable to govern his own nature (p. 329). Evans uses the life to announce that the chapter runs from external to internal nature.

**B. Control of the elements had costs and limits (pp. 336–345)**

- **Premises**: (p. 338) Peasant use, urban building, logging and war consumed forests. (pp. 338–339) Contemporaries linked deforestation to erosion, climate change, landslides, floods and, through silted rivers and marsh, malaria. (pp. 339–340) States and private investors drained, embanked and straightened. (p. 340) Tulla's Rhine correction ended most flooding and gained farmland at the price of the marshland flora and fauna. (pp. 343–344) Fire services professionalised slowly. (p. 345) Smoke abatement failed.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 406) Taming the wild and mastering the elements met with some success but produced unwelcome consequences, from extinction to deforestation and atmospheric pollution.

**C. Railways and telegraphs standardised space and time (pp. 345–363)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 345–346) Local measures reflected local life and peasants ignored official systems. (pp. 346–347) Trade, industry and above all railway companies needed uniform length. (p. 358) Each town kept its own time until railways made that unworkable. (p. 359) In Germany the decisive advocate was Moltke, for military reasons. (pp. 360–361) The global telegraph network made a world standard necessary, and British maritime dominance made Greenwich the reference. (p. 361) France resisted and then sought to recapture the initiative with radio time signals.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 347, 358) Standardisation was a consequence of the communications revolution, pushed by companies and soldiers as much as by governments, and its form recorded the international balance of power.

**D. Technology redistributed access and status (pp. 348–356)**

- **Premises**: (p. 348) Luxury trains were designed for the well-to-do. (p. 349) Branch lines brought cheap travel to millions. (p. 351) Car ownership before 1914 was the preserve of the wealthy and restored private travel. (p. 352) The bicycle reached the working class and freed women's movement and dress. (p. 354) The lift inverted the social hierarchy of tall buildings and weakened neighbourly contact.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 349) Democratisation of mobility was real but uneven and in part reversed. This is Evans's interpretation, built from illustrative cases, not from measured use.

**E. Modern time carried the idea of progress and then unsettled it (pp. 361–363)**

- **Premises**: (p. 361) Newtonian time was uniform, absolute and one-directional. (pp. 361–362) Linear time let people imagine a different past; the French Revolution and geology extended and deepened it. (p. 362) Life felt faster. (p. 363) Painting, film and relativity made time seem malleable.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 362–363) Time became larger, more uniform and more organised, and then more pressing and uncertain. The link between Newtonian time and the idea of progress is asserted, not demonstrated.

**F. Mortality fell for reasons that were mostly not medical (pp. 363–371)**

- **Premises**: (p. 364) Malaria declined as marshes were drained, before its transmission was understood. (p. 365) Tuberculosis mortality fell in the second half of the century although Koch's 1882 discovery had no real effect and sanatoria reached too few. (p. 366) Smallpox was the exception, defeated by compulsory vaccination. (p. 367) Typhus fell with cleanliness and the relative absence of war and famine, before its cause was found in 1909. (p. 369) Cholera was stopped by clean water, sewerage and border controls.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 369) Direct medical intervention did little; (p. 371) the combined result was a dramatic rise in the chance of surviving to adulthood and old age for those born from the 1880s.

**G. Epidemic disease followed power, war and poverty (pp. 367–370)**

- **Premises**: (p. 368) Cholera left Bengal along routes opened by British conquest and spread through industrial transport and insanitary towns. (pp. 368–369) Its epidemics coincided with the suppression of Poland in 1830–1, 1848–9, the Crimean War, and the wars of German unification. (p. 369) In Hamburg in 1892 it killed about 10,000, hitting the poorest hardest. (p. 367) Virchow traced typhus to oppression.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 368–369) Disease exposed the underside of Europe's expansion and prosperity; Koch's remark in the Hamburg slums is used as the verdict.

**H. Falling mortality changed attitudes to death (pp. 371–375)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 371–373) Death was central to nineteenth-century culture, with elaborate funerals, mourning and memorials, and a sanitised representation of the body. (p. 373) The Christian ideal of a conscious good death gave way to the ideal of dying unaware. (pp. 373–374) Rural communities, where death rates stayed high, kept communal rituals. (pp. 374–375) Cremation and spiritualism spread as Christian certainty ebbed.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 375) By 1914 urban society had come to deny death, which made the slaughter of the First World War more traumatic. The last step is a forward-looking assertion with no evidence offered here.

**I. The birth-rate fall was achieved by repression (pp. 375–384)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 375–376) Births per woman fell across Europe. (pp. 376–377) Condoms, diaphragms and withdrawal were unreliable, illicit or little used; abortion was illegal though widespread. (p. 377) Abstinence was the only safe method and women bore the risk. (pp. 377–378) Schooling and child-labour laws made children a cost, first for the middle class and then for workers. (p. 378) Illegitimate births also fell from the 1840s.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 377) Women repressed their sexual feelings, the notion of female sexual incapacity spread, and prudery arose to reinforce it; (p. 384) the continuing fall in the birth rate shows that most people were more concerned to repress their urges than to act on them.
- **Corollary**: (pp. 379–382) Because women were assumed to lack desire and men to be driven by it, the state regulated prostitutes, purity reformers attacked male lust, and the law criminalised male but not female homosexuality.

**J. Pain was brought under partial control (pp. 384–388)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 384–385) Chemical anaesthesia replaced mesmerism in the 1840s against religious and professional objections. (p. 386) Semmelweis cut puerperal deaths but could not explain why and was rejected. (pp. 386–387) Germ theory gave Lister's methods a foundation; adoption was slow. (p. 387) Longer operations became possible and hospitals ceased to be places where the poor went to die.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 388) Medicine had begun to master pain but most diseases resisted cure until after 1945.

**K. The asylum: from cure to custody (pp. 388–396)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 389–390) Esquirol and Griesinger, though disagreeing on causes, shared an optimism that produced state asylums. (pp. 391–392) Diagnoses at Eberbach tracked breaches of bourgeois norms of religion, class and gender. (pp. 393–394) Admissions outran discharges; staff were few and untrained. (pp. 394–395) Urbanisation made home care harder and brought the disturbed to the state's notice. (pp. 395–396) Kraepelin's hereditary psychiatry extended insanity to alcoholism and petty crime, and police and local authorities used it.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 396) After a century of trying to cure insanity little progress had been made, and deviance and insanity had come to seem two sides of one coin.

**L. Punishment: body, mind, body (pp. 396–404)**

- **Premises**: (p. 397) Bourgeois squeamishness and, after 1848, official fear of crowds ended public execution. (pp. 397–399) Capital and corporal punishment contracted under liberal and reforming pressure. (pp. 399–401) Transportation and then the penitentiary replaced them. (pp. 402–403) Budgets, overcrowding and political anxiety defeated the reformers and prisoners kept returning. (pp. 403–404) Lombroso and his followers moved criminality from law to medicine and argued for sterilisation and for execution on eugenic grounds.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 404) Punishment came full circle, from the body to the mind and back to the body.

**M. The growing state behind it all, and its limits (pp. 404–406)**

- **Premises**: (p. 404) States rebuilt order after the Napoleonic Wars through gendarmeries. (pp. 404–405) Urban growth required new uniformed police, initially resented. (p. 405) In Prussia the police held very wide administrative powers and kept a military character. (pp. 405–406) In Italy and Russia numbers and organisation were inadequate.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 404, 406) More state power meant more people arrested and condemned, yet control of crime remained limited.

## Evidence offered

Evans gives no source references anywhere in the chapter (the book has no notes). Named origins below are those he mentions in the text.

- **Opening biography**: Szemere's published travel diary (1840) and later diaries, quoted at length (pp. 327–329). A liberal nobleman's view; the foreign opinions of Hungary reach us only through him.
- **Land-use and forest statistics**: the 1815 Prussian survey; arable growth in Prussia, Romania, Spain, Bohemia and Russia (p. 330); forest cover by country (p. 337); European forest decline of 25 per cent in 1700–1850 and 5 per cent in 1850–1920 (p. 338). Evans gives no source.
- **Wolves and hunting**: Russian official statistics of about two hundred people eaten a year (p. 330); 1,500 wolves killed a year in France in the 1820s (p. 330); Sandringham game book, 7,000 birds a year (p. 334); Franz Ferdinand's reported 300,000 kills, hedged with *is said* (p. 335).
- **Environmental observers**: Passek on climate in Kharkov province, 1836; Blanqui on the Alps, 1843; Italian forestry inspectors' 156 landslides in Cosenza in 1903 (pp. 338–339). Educated official and agronomist voices.
- **Engineering cases**: Fucine Lake, Ferrara reclamation (p. 339); Tulla's Rhine, 51 miles shorter, about 24,700 acres gained (p. 340).
- **Disasters**: Pest flood 1838, Szeged 1879 (pp. 339–340); Messina 1908, 75,000 to 200,000 dead (p. 341); Hamburg fire 1842 with itemised losses (pp. 341–342); press-style reports on Glarus and St Petersburg (p. 342); a study of 114 Bavarian rural arson cases, 1879–1900, author unnamed (p. 343).
- **Fiction and art as evidence of perception**: the Grimms, Dickens, Stevenson, Monet, Dostoyevsky, Chekhov (pp. 337, 342–345); Verne, Lasswitz, Wells (pp. 355, 357); de Chirico, Duchamp, the Lumières (p. 363); Tolstoy on dying (p. 373); Zola, Hugo, Dickens on sex and prostitution (pp. 378, 380). Evans treats these as indicators of attitudes, and once corrects one (Zola's *Germinal*, p. 378).
- **Measures**: a catalogue of ells, rods, miles and journals (pp. 345–346); a French schoolteacher in 1861 and the economist Baudrillart on peasant indifference to the metric system (p. 346).
- **Transport and communications statistics**: railway mileage by country (p. 347); Orient Express fares and timings (p. 348); letters and postcards (pp. 348–349); car numbers and firms (pp. 350–351); bicycle ownership among Dutch taxpayers (p. 352); watch production (p. 358); telegraph ownership (p. 359). Evans gives no source.
- **Contemporary commentators**: Mayreder (p. 352), Highton (p. 359), Nordau (pp. 359–360), Fleming (pp. 360–361), Moltke (p. 359), Greg and Harrison (p. 362). Mostly middle-class, male, western European.
- **Memoir**: Hermynia Folliot de Crenneville on Istanbul fires and a Viennese countess stoning cyclists (pp. 342–343, 352–353).
- **Disease figures**: Young's 1815 estimate of consumption deaths (p. 364); Paris autopsies (p. 364); tuberculosis as about half the mortality decline of 1850–1900 (p. 365); smallpox in the Franco-Prussian War (p. 366); London typhoid rates (p. 366); St Petersburg typhus (p. 367); cholera's 50 per cent case mortality and Hamburg's 10,000 dead (pp. 368–369). The Hamburg material is the densest in the chapter and is Evans's own research field, though he does not say so.
- **Demographic series**: death rates and life expectancy by country (p. 371); Iceland (p. 370); birth rates, births per woman, marital fertility, national populations (pp. 375–376). Unsourced, national aggregates.
- **Death culture**: an 1824 London undertaker's order book (p. 372); the *Lancet*, 1894 (p. 372); the Balfour family letters (p. 375). Upper-class British sources dominate; rural customs are described generally with no witness named (pp. 373–374).
- **Sexuality**: a French survey of doctors, 1899 (p. 377); estimates of 100,000 to 300,000 German abortions a year (p. 377); prostitution counts for Madrid, St Petersburg, Berlin, Munich (p. 378); syphilis mortality as a proxy for men's use of prostitutes (pp. 378–379); Parent-Duchâtelet, Acton, Mantegazza, Butler, Krafft-Ebing quoted (pp. 379–383). The voices are doctors, police and reformers; the prostitutes speak only in the Rouen revolt of 1908 (p. 381).
- **Medicine**: Queen Victoria's journal and Florence Nightingale's letter (p. 385); Semmelweis's ward figures, a third against 10 per cent and then 1 per cent (p. 386); Newcastle Infirmary, 60 per cent to 1 per cent (p. 387); French amputations in 1870–1, 76 per cent mortality (p. 387).
- **Asylums**: Siegburg admissions by social background and Jacobi's 1843 address (p. 391); Eberbach diagnoses and treatments (pp. 391–392), evidently resting on a specialised study that Evans does not name; Lindsay's 1857 visit to a Norwegian asylum (p. 392); Prussian census of 1871 and growth to 1910 (p. 394); Irish admissions and Norman's reports (pp. 393–394); a Hanwell inmate's account (p. 394); Bucknill's report (p. 396). A mix of directors' self-description and a few patient or critic voices.
- **Punishment**: Waldeyer-Hartz's eyewitness account of the 1858 Göttingen beheading (p. 396); Dickens on a hanging (p. 397); execution counts for England and Wales, Prussia, France and Russia (p. 398); transportation totals (pp. 399–400); Cattaneo's 1840 tract (p. 401); Russian prison numbers and mortality (p. 402); Canning's 1851 memorandum and Cardashian's claim, flagged by Evans as sensational (pp. 402–403).
- **Police**: Habsburg gendarmerie strength (p. 404); a 1910 Bukovina document (p. 404); the 1830 *justifiable homicide* verdict (pp. 404–405); Ryazan police numbers (p. 406); Budapest petty-offence figures (p. 406).
- **Whose perspective**: overwhelmingly that of officials, physicians, reformers and literate travellers. The method is accumulation of cases across many countries; causal claims (diet and tuberculosis, abstinence and fertility, norms and asylum growth) are stated with a few supporting figures and no engagement with named historians.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Preface**: the preface lists power over nature among the senses of the book's title; this chapter is where that sense is worked out, and it ends on limits, not triumph (p. 406).
- **ch02 (The Paradoxes of Freedom)**: railways and the telegraph were treated there as economic and political forces; here the same technologies are treated as changing the experience of space and time (pp. 347–349, 358–361). Széchenyi's smuggling of machinery is recalled from the earlier narrative (p. 328).
- **ch03 (The European Spring)**: 1848 recurs as a hinge: hunting rights reformed (p. 334), Virchow's report (p. 367), cholera following the forces of order (p. 368), official fear of crowds ending public executions (p. 397), legislatures gaining power to abolish the death penalty (p. 398), Berlin's new police (p. 405).
- **ch04 (The Social Revolution)**: urbanisation is the standing cause in this chapter, for zoos (p. 332), fog (p. 344), cholera (p. 368), asylum growth (pp. 394–395) and policing (p. 404). The bourgeoisie appears as the bearer of new sensibilities about cruelty, death, sex and deviance (pp. 333, 368, 391, 397).
- **ch06 (The Age of Emotion)**: the closing sentences hand over explicitly: science's challenge to biblical narratives and the new media pose problems for belief and the arts (pp. 406–407).
- **ch07 (The Challenge of Democracy)**: feminism (Butler, Pankhurst, the bicycle), pronatalism, eugenic arguments and the Eulenburg scandal are introduced here from the side of sexuality and science (pp. 352, 376, 380–383, 403).
- **ch08 (The Wages of Empire)**: empire runs through the chapter: Raffles's zoo (p. 333), royal hunting in Ceylon and India (p. 335), communications as tools of empire (p. 348), balloons in colonial wars (p. 355), British cable dominance (p. 359), cholera as the return flow of conquest (p. 368), Koch's comparison of Hamburg with Egypt and India (p. 369), transportation to colonies (pp. 399–400).
- **Internal tension 1**: the chapter's title promises conquest and its conclusion stresses limits and costs (p. 406). The sections on space, time and mortality read as success stories; those on sexuality, madness and crime read as failure.
- **Internal tension 2**: Evans credits the state with growing power as the root cause (p. 404), yet most of his institutional cases show the state underfunding or failing to control what it had built (pp. 402–403, 405–406).
- **Internal tension 3**: the argument that sexual repression produced the fertility decline (pp. 377, 384) sits beside evidence of widespread abortion and of withdrawal as the commonest method in France (p. 377), and an expansion of prostitution (p. 378).
- **Internal tension 4**: medicine is said to have done little against infectious disease (p. 369), yet smallpox vaccination is called its greatest success (p. 366) and bacteriology is credited with informing public health campaigns (p. 366) and antisepsis (p. 386). The claim holds for cure, less clearly for prevention.
- **Unacknowledged debt**: two section titles repeat the English titles of books by Michel Foucault, and the treatment of asylums and prisons as instruments of bourgeois normalisation follows that line; Foucault is not named in the chapter.

## Open questions

- What is the evidence that improved diet was the most important factor in the tuberculosis decline (p. 365)? Evans words it as likely and offers no data.
- Can the fall in marital fertility really be read as a measure of abstinence and repression (p. 384), given the abortion estimates and the French survey on the same pages (p. 377)?
- How much of asylum growth was changed norms, and how much urbanisation, state provision or psychiatric theory? Evans gives all four in turn without weighting them (pp. 391, 394–395).
- The link between Newtonian uniform time and the idea of progress (p. 361) is stated in two sentences; it needs support from elsewhere.
- The claim that denial of death made the First World War more traumatic (p. 375) is left for a later volume; nothing in this chapter tests it.
- Rural and eastern Europe are present mostly as statistics or as a foil; the voices are urban and western. Does the conquest described here apply to the peasant majority before 1914 (pp. 373–374)?
- Apparent slips to check against other sources before reuse: the Wright brothers' first flight is placed at Daytona Beach, North Carolina (p. 356); the Russo-Japanese War is dated 1905–6 (p. 347); Giovanni Lanza is given the dates 1810–82 but quoted as minister in 1891 (p. 377); the engineer credited with draining the Fucine Lake in 1862–75 is given a death date of 1858 (p. 339); Jacques Schneider's dates are misprinted (p. 356).
- The statement that Wilhelm II's readiness to sign death warrants produced a sharp decline in the murder rate because juries became reluctant to convict (p. 399) appears to mean the rate of murder convictions; the wording is ambiguous.
- Iceland's death rate of just under 30 per 1,000 is said to be more than twice that of Hamburg in the cholera year 1892 (p. 370); the comparison seems improbable and should be checked.
