# ch08: The Wages of Empire (pp. 570–647)

## Question this chapter answers

How did Europe come to dominate most of the globe between 1815 and 1914, what did that domination do to the colonized and to Europeans themselves, and how did it end in a general European war? Evans's answer runs in three movements. First, empire before about 1880 grew piecemeal, driven from the periphery by explorers, traders, missionaries, settlers and local officials, not by metropolitan design (pp. 578-585). Second, from the late 1870s a self-conscious imperialism produced a rush of annexations whose rule rested on violence, and which bred a racialized, Social Darwinist view of international relations (pp. 585-624). Third, once the world outside Europe was divided up, the habits of empire turned inward: onto the decaying Ottoman Empire and the Balkans, and finally onto the Great Powers themselves, whose leaders in July 1914 had been taught by a quarter-century of conquest that only force counted (pp. 610, 631, 646). The chapter title names the result: the wage of empire is the war that destroys Europe's global hegemony (p. 647).

The chapter file opens on p. 570 with the last lines of chapter 7 (Kipling's *Recessional* and Alfred Harmsworth's optimistic forecast for the twentieth century on 1 January 1901); that passage frames this chapter ironically but belongs to ch07 (p. 570). The closing paragraph of the book runs over onto p. 648, which the workspace files under the back matter.

## Section guide

- **The Explorers** (pp. 570-578). Opens with the life of Giovanni Belzoni, circus strongman turned Egyptologist, as an emblem of European looting; then Burton and Speke, the Bakers, women explorers, du Chaillu, Stanley, Livingstone, the polar races. Thesis: explorers were the harbingers of imperialism (p. 578).
- **The Revival of Empire** (pp. 578-585). Empire from 1815 to the 1870s: British naval hegemony and free trade, Algeria, west African trading posts, south Africa, India and the 1857 rebellion, Indochina, New Zealand and the Pacific, the Opium Wars, Russian expansion in Central Asia and the sale of Alaska. Thesis: expansion was unplanned and driven from the periphery.
- **The Age of Imperialism** (pp. 585-595; the heading is misprinted *IMPERIALSM* in the text). The cult of empire, the rejection of economic and social-imperialist explanations, Tunisia and Egypt, Bismarck's protectorates, the Berlin Conference, the Scramble, the Pacific, China and the Boxers, Spain's losses in 1898, Adowa, Omdurman. Ends with Evans's own explanation of European global dominance.
- **Exploitation and Resistance** (pp. 595-603). Turning paper colonies into real ones: the Herero and Nama genocide, Maji-Maji, the thinness of German control; then British settler colonies (Canada, Australia, Tasmania), the Xhosa, Zulu and Boer wars, British concentration camps, chartered-company rule in west Africa, Benin and Zanzibar.
- **'The White Man's Burden'** (pp. 603-610). Leopold's Congo and the French Congo; the retreat from the French civilizing mission; British India as the model of indirect rule (civil service, army, famine, indentured labour); Burma; the rise of colonial nationalism and the Indian National Congress.
- **The 'Eastern Question'** (pp. 610-618). Ottoman financial collapse and the Tanzimat, the Bulgarian massacres, the Russo-Turkish War of 1877-8, San Stefano and the Congress of Berlin, Bismarck's alliances, Bulgaria under Stambolov, Romania.
- **Racism and Nationalism** (pp. 618-624). The shift from a relative, Christian-liberal sense of European superiority to biological racism: Disraeli and popular imperialism, Social Darwinism, Haeckel, Galton, Pearson, Gobineau, Houston Stewart Chamberlain; racialized Great Power anxieties; the dissenters (Suttner, the Hague conferences, Angell).
- **The Balkan Wars** (pp. 624-632). Macedonia and IMRO, Abdülhamid's pan-Islamism and the Armenian massacres, the Young Turks, Balkan arms races and state bankruptcies, the 1903 Serbian regicide, the Bosnian annexation, the Italian-Turkish war, the two Balkan Wars and their meaning.
- **'The Great War We All Hope For'** (pp. 632-638). The unravelling of Bismarck's system, the entente cordiale, the Moroccan crises, the Anglo-German naval race, secret British staff talks, the short-war illusion and its few critics (Bloch, Lamszus), and the elite longing for war.
- **Countdown to Catastrophe** (pp. 638-648). Sarajevo, the blank cheque, the ultimatum, Russian and German mobilization, British hesitation, the collapse of socialist opposition, Evans's verdict on responsibility, popular reactions, and the book's closing reflection on what 1914 ended.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Imperialism | p. 579, p. 585 | An explicit ideology asserting the superiority of European civilization and justifying colonies as policy. Evans stresses it did not exist for much of the century; the word entered English in the 1870s. Distinct from the earlier unplanned growth of empire. |
| Age of imperialism / high imperialism | p. 585, p. 596 | The period from the 1870s to 1914, especially 1878-1914, when Europe added over 8.6 million square miles to its territory (p. 586). |
| Free trade (as imperial method) | p. 579 | Britain's reliance on naval and commercial dominance instead of annexation; also a weapon, as when low tariffs forced on Tunisia and the Ottomans flooded them with European goods (pp. 587, 610). |
| Scramble for Africa | p. 589 | A British term coined in 1884 for the rush of annexations set off by Bismarck's protectorates and the Berlin Conference; over by the mid-1890s (p. 591). |
| Effective occupation | p. 589 | The Berlin Conference's test for a valid colonial claim; in Evans's view a dead letter, since it applied only to coasts. |
| Paper colonies | p. 596 | Territories claimed by lines on a map but not controlled; the story of the 1890s and 1900s is their conversion into real ones. |
| Colonies of settlement / colonies of occupation | pp. 597-598 | Settlement: climate allows mass European emigration (Canada, Australia, German South-West Africa). Occupation: a handful of officials over a large indigenous population (Cameroon, Togo). The distinction explains different patterns of violence. |
| Debt Commission | p. 588 | International creditor bodies that took over the finances of Tunis, Egypt, the Ottoman Empire and later Greece and Serbia; Evans treats them as a standard mechanism of informal control (pp. 587, 614, 626). |
| Concentration camps | p. 597 | First official German use in South-West Africa for interned Herero and Nama; also the British camps for Boers and black Africans in the South African war (p. 602). |
| Indirect rule | p. 605 | Governing through existing indigenous institutions and elites; derived from British India and adopted in French thinking as the civilizing mission retreated. |
| Civilizing mission | p. 605 | The earlier French belief in spreading the benefits of the Revolution; partly abandoned on grounds of cost. |
| White man's burden | p. 609 | Kipling's 1899 phrase for the ideology of improvement; Evans inverts it to mean the burden the imperialist imposed on the colonized (p. 610). |
| Indentured labour | p. 608 | A kind of quasi-slavery: paid work without freedom or rights, drawn mainly from India. |
| Eastern Question | p. 610 | The problem of who would benefit from the decline of the Ottoman Empire in south-eastern Europe. |
| Tanzimat | p. 610 | Ottoman reforms of the 1850s and 1860s: civil rights, law codes, a state bank, equal rights for religious groups, an Ottoman national identity. |
| Pan-Slavism | p. 611 | The doctrine, emerging in Russia in the 1870s, that the smaller Slav nations form a family headed by Russia; a pressure on tsarist policy in 1876-7 and 1914 (pp. 612, 643). |
| Great Game | p. 584 | London's name for Anglo-Russian jockeying in Central Asia; the reason Britain saw Russia as its main enemy until the 1900s (pp. 615, 633). |
| Social Darwinism | p. 620 | The application of Darwin's ideas, or a version of them, to human society; it had a progressive welfare variant as well as the harsh competitive one. |
| Scientific racism | p. 622 | The arrangement of racial types on an evolutionary scale, with mixing thought to drag down the higher races. |
| Weltpolitik | p. 634 | Bülow's *world policy*: loud German intervention in world affairs in pursuit of a place in the sun, backed by Tirpitz's battleship fleet. |
| Concert of Europe | p. 615 | Great Power co-operation to manage crises; revived at Berlin in 1878, crumbling after 1890 (p. 633). |
| Blank cheque | p. 640 | Germany's unconditional promise of 6 July 1914 to support whatever Austria did against Serbia; for Evans most probably the crucial moment of the crisis (p. 645). |
| European hegemony | p. 574, p. 647 | Europe's worldwide dominance, real only from the third quarter of the century (p. 595) and ended by the war. |

## Leading propositions

- Exploration, whatever the motives of individual explorers, was a key source of empire: it tied discovery to national prestige and taught Europeans to see other regions as destined for subjugation (pp. 577-578).
- European looting of antiquities and artefacts expressed Europe's hegemony; scruples about plunder developed among Europeans after 1815 but were not applied outside Europe (pp. 573-574).
- For much of the century there was no ideology of imperialism, and Britain, with absolute command of the oceans, had no need to turn economic power into territory (pp. 578-579).
- The revival of empire after 1815 was driven by local situations overseas and by men on the spot, not by metropolitan governments; it was piecemeal and unplanned (pp. 579, 583).
- The economic (Hobson, Lenin, Luxemburg) and social-imperialist explanations of the post-1880 expansion fail on chronology: cartels, mass politics and colonial pressure groups came after most territory had been taken (p. 586).
- The scramble is better explained by intensified international rivalry after the end of free trade, crises produced by European economic penetration, politicians' pursuit of prestige, and Bismarck's use of colonial claims as counters in European diplomacy, which set off a panic (pp. 586-589).
- Europe's global dominance was not inevitable from 1500; it took hold only in the third quarter of the nineteenth century and was a product of European peace under British naval hegemony as well as of technology (p. 595).
- That dominance had limits: China was never partitioned, Spain lost its empire, Italy was beaten at Adowa, and states that acquired European weapons (Ethiopia, Japan) could resist (pp. 593-595).
- Colonial rule in the high imperial period was the attempt to make paper colonies real, and violence lay at the heart of it in the British as in the German empire (pp. 596, 603).
- German South-West Africa saw a deliberate policy of extermination, and some of its vocabulary and measures recurred under the Third Reich (pp. 596-597). The British camps in South Africa were murderous but not a deliberate policy of genocide (p. 602).
- European control depended on indigenous collaborators; small numbers of officials could establish only islands of power (p. 598), and British rule in India rested on the passive co-operation of Indians backed by the Indian Army (pp. 606-607).
- British rule brought disaster to India through famine and created a global system of indentured labour, even as it pursued improvement (pp. 607-608).
- Empire produced its own gravediggers: educating colonial clerks created elites who absorbed European nationalism and liberalism, and unifying India created the basis for a united nationalist movement (pp. 609-610).
- Once colonization reached its limits shortly after 1900, powers could gain territory only from each other; the Ottoman Empire in Europe became the obvious prey (p. 610).
- The Congress of Berlin's division of the Balkans into Austrian and Russian spheres was disastrous in the long run, tying unstable states to Great Power prestige while leaving all of them dissatisfied (p. 615).
- Racist concepts of identity, derived from overseas empire and given scientific standing by Darwinism, replaced an earlier relative and religious sense of superiority, and came to shape how Europeans saw each other (pp. 618, 620, 623).
- The Balkan Wars were imperialism turned in towards Europe, and their massacres prefigured twentieth-century genocide; they were also a military portent that the Great Powers failed to study (pp. 631-632, 636).
- Germany's naval challenge made Britain and Germany regard each other as the main enemy by 1914, although British policy was still guided more by imperial than by European considerations (pp. 634-636, 644).
- War was widely expected by 1910 and positively desired by parts of the political and military elite (pp. 637-638).
- In July 1914 almost nobody sought compromise. The blank cheque was most probably the crucial moment; Austria was set on war from the outset; but there is no evidence that Germany used the crisis as a pretext for war with Russia or Britain (pp. 640, 645-646).
- The statesmen were not swept into war by popular enthusiasm; pro-war crowds were middle class, and most Europeans met the war with incomprehension or dread (pp. 646-647).
- The war ended a century of European hegemony and reversed Europe's march towards democracy (pp. 647-648).

## Arguments

- **Explorers as unwitting agents of empire.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 574-576) Exploration was made possible by steam, rail and the prestige of science, and its heroes (Burton, Baker, Stanley, Livingstone) were publicized at home as national figures. + (p. 576) Stanley's Congo journeys led directly to Leopold's commission and provoked a French counter-expedition under de Brazza. + (p. 577) Even economically worthless goals such as the Poles became national races. + (p. 578) Where explorers met human societies they regarded them as inferior or non-existent; Przhevalsky is offered as typical.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 578) Exploration brought distant regions within reach and marked them as destined to be subjugated; explorers were harbingers of imperialism whether they meant to be or not.

- **The periphery, not the metropole, drove pre-1880 expansion.**
  **Premises**: (p. 579) Algeria began with a debt dispute, an insult to a consul and Charles X's domestic need for popularity. + (p. 581) London vacillated over the Transvaal; in India annexation came from Dalhousie's initiative and Napier exceeded his orders. + (p. 583) In Indochina local French officials pressed for expansion to protect missions; in New Zealand the government declared sovereignty to protect emigrants who had already sailed. + (p. 584) Russian officials in Central Asia concluded treaties on their own initiative.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 583) In almost all cases local officials, merchants, missionaries and above all settlers pressed metropolitan governments, which felt increasingly obliged to intervene when trading interests were threatened.

- **Against the economic and social-imperialist theories.**
  **Premises**: (p. 586) Hobson, Lenin and Luxemburg tied expansion to raw materials, markets and surplus capital under monopoly capitalism; German Social Democrats saw it as a diversion of working-class discontent. + (p. 586) But monopolies and cartels came in the 1890s or later; there is no convincing evidence of falling profit margins in the 1880s, when economies were recovering; popular imperial pressure groups arose after 1900; in the 1880s governments still relied on repression against socialism; Italy had a tiny electorate.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 586-587) These theories misdate their own mechanisms. The better account is a changed international atmosphere after Germany's tariffs of the late 1870s, collisions between expanding possessions, crises caused by economic penetration, and politicians' pursuit of glory. Evans does not deny economic motives altogether: he later says they remained paramount in Britain's tropical African colonies (p. 602).

- **How Bismarck set off the scramble.**
  **Premises**: (p. 587) Bismarck did not want colonies but saw colonial claims as bargaining counters in Europe. + (p. 588) In 1884 he backed France against Britain over Egypt, needed National Liberal votes, and declared protectorates in Africa and the Pacific. + (p. 589) This produced something like a panic; the Berlin Conference, by laying down ground rules, in effect declared the scramble open.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 589-591) A manoeuvre aimed at European alignments produced the partition of Africa by the mid-1890s, most of it on paper only.

- **Why Europe dominated, and why only then.**
  **Premises**: (p. 595) Omdurman shows the imbalance of force; modern weaponry came from Europe's industrial and scientific lead. + (p. 595) Had European states gone on fighting each other and exporting their wars as before 1815, things might have been different. + (p. 595) Peace under British naval hegemony allowed telegraphs, sea lanes, railways, trade and mass migration to build a globalized economy; European states were also better organized to mobilize resources.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 595) Dominance was not inevitable after 1500 but arose in the third quarter of the nineteenth century, and Europe enjoyed it neither before nor after. This is the hinge of the chapter: the same peace that enabled empire is what empire eventually destroys.

- **Violence as structural, varying with type of colony and of indigenous society.**
  **Premises**: (p. 596) Settler land seizures provoked the most violent clashes and drew in reluctant states. + (pp. 597-598) Colonies of occupation had few Europeans, so control was fragile and enforced by routine beatings and penal expeditions. + (p. 598) Loosely organized nomads were easier to crush than structured states such as the Fulbe emirates, with whom Germans ended in effective co-rule. + (pp. 599-600) In British settler colonies violence came more from settlers than troops, and disease did most of the killing. + (p. 603) In British Africa treaty-based company rule gave way under Chamberlain to military assertion (Ashanti, Benin, northern Nigeria, Zanzibar).
  **Conclusion**: (p. 603) Violence lay at the heart of the British as well as the German empire in Africa. The Congo is distinguished not by its conquest but by the way it was run (p. 604).

- **Empire undermines itself.**
  **Premises**: (p. 609) Administration needed educated clerks, so some of the colonized had to be educated. + (p. 609) That education carried European ideas of nationalism, democracy and liberalism. + (pp. 609-610) Britain united India administratively, while leaning on traditional institutions that the new elite saw as obstacles to progress.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 610) Colonial rule created the potential for united nationalist movements, and the white man's burden could be read as a burden laid on the colonized.

- **From overseas empire to the Balkans.**
  **Premises**: (p. 610) Colonization reached its limits just after 1900. + (pp. 610-611, 614) The Ottoman Empire was fiscally ruined, indebted to western banks and under creditor administration. + (p. 615) The Berlin settlement split the Balkans into Austrian and Russian spheres and left every Balkan state resentful. + (pp. 625-626) The Balkan states armed themselves to the point of bankruptcy on credit from the Great Powers. + (p. 618) Nationalist passion was sharpened by racist ideas of identity derived from empire.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 631) The territorial ambitions of the Balkan states went beyond national claims and amounted to imperialism turned in towards Europe, with massacres that prefigured later genocides.

- **From religious-liberal superiority to biological racism.**
  **Premises**: (p. 618) Mid-century textbooks and missionary literature held backwardness to be a matter of progress and religion, not intelligence. + (p. 619) The shocks of the 1850s and 1860s and German unification led Disraeli to make empire central to national identity. + (p. 620) Later textbooks stressed racial inferiority; Darwinism lent hierarchy scientific legitimacy. + (pp. 621-622) Galton, Pearson, Gobineau and H. S. Chamberlain turned this into eugenics, pessimism about improvement, and racialized antisemitism.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 622-623) Conquest or extermination came to seem the only course with supposedly inferior races, and European rivalries themselves were recast as racial struggles (Slav against Teuton, Anglo-Saxon against Latin). Evans notes the view was contested (pp. 623-624).

- **Why the alliance system failed to keep the peace.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 632-633) Bismarck's treaties were meant to isolate France and balance Austria and Russia, whose Balkan interests could not be reconciled. + (p. 633) His successors let the Reinsurance Treaty lapse and brought colonial issues back into European politics. + (pp. 633-634) Franco-British colonial rivalry was settled in 1904, and German pressure over Morocco pushed Britain towards France. + (pp. 634-636) The Tirpitz fleet made Germany Britain's chief potential enemy, and British generals made continental commitments unknown to the Cabinet. + (p. 636) Generals and admirals expected a short, decisive war and ignored the evidence of the Balkan, American and Crimean wars.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 637-638) By 1910 at the latest war was widely expected, and to many in the elites it appeared as a release.

- **Responsibility in July 1914.**
  **Premises**: (p. 639) Franz Ferdinand's death removed a moderate and freed Conrad's war party. + (p. 640) The German leaders gave the blank cheque assuming Russia would not intervene. + (p. 640) Austria delayed for a month and then issued an ultimatum meant as an excuse for war. + (pp. 640, 643) Russia told Serbia to hold firm and began pre-mobilization on 26 July; France backed Russia. + (pp. 641-644) Britain did not take the crisis seriously, gave mixed signals, and the Cabinet refused a deterrent declaration. + (p. 642) German counsels were divided and the Kaiser inconsistent; the Schlieffen Plan meant war with Russia began with an attack on France. + (p. 645) The ultimatum to Belgium was a blunder that gave Britain its reason to intervene.
  **Conclusion**: (pp. 645-646) No one thought of compromise. The blank cheque was most probably the crucial moment, but blame is distributed: Austria and Serbia were both determined, Germany, Russia and France never took mediation seriously, Britain acted too late. The deeper cause is generational and cultural: leaders formed by imperialism believed only force mattered and that the other side was racially inferior and easily beaten, and they behaved as men in a duel.

## Evidence offered

Evans's method is narrative synthesis from secondary literature, studded with numbers and with short quotations from contemporaries. The book has no footnotes, so for nearly every item below Evans gives no source.

- **Biographical vignettes of Europeans.** Belzoni's career and his own published account of crushing mummies and robbing papyri (pp. 570-573); Stanley's losses (356 set out, 114 arrived) and his and Burton's remarks (p. 576); Scott's last journal (p. 578); Carl Peters's hangings and dismissal (pp. 589-590); Stambolov's murder (p. 617); the Serbian regicide of 1903 (p. 627). These are colourful and memorable; they carry the perspective of the European actors, and the more lurid details (Stambolov's pickled hands, Genčić's remark to the king) are of the kind Evans elsewhere flags with *is said to have*.
- **Territorial and demographic statistics.** 8.6 million square miles added 1878-1914 and 57 per cent of world population under European and American colonial rule (p. 586); Royal Navy strength and 80 per cent of world goods in British ships (p. 578); Balkan states' territorial and population gains in 1913 (pp. 630-631). No source given.
- **Death tolls as evidence of colonial violence.** Algeria: about a million dead from the 1868 famine and the 1871 revolt, a third of the population (p. 580). Herero reduced from an estimated 85,000 to 15,000, up to 10,000 of 20,000 Nama killed, half of 17,000 camp inmates dead (p. 597). Maji-Maji: some 80,000 killed and over 200,000 dead of famine (p. 597). Boer War camps: about 28,000 Boers and at least 14,000 black Africans dead (p. 602). Congo: population halved from 20 million to 10 million, based on a 1924 Belgian census compared with late nineteenth-century estimates (pp. 604-605). Indian famines: two million in 1860-1, six million in the 1870s, five million in 1896-7 (p. 607). Aboriginal population from an estimated half to three-quarters of a million in 1788 to 72,000 in 1921 (p. 600). Armenians: 100,000 to 300,000 killed by 1897 (p. 625). Several rest on admitted estimates; the Congo baseline in particular is an estimate against a later census.
- **Administrative statistics.** Public beatings in Cameroon rising from 315 in 1900 to 4,800 in 1913, called an underestimate (p. 597); 415 officials for nearly 10 million people in German East Africa (p. 598); 61 penal expeditions in six years (p. 597); only 5 per cent of Indian Civil Service posts held by Indians in 1915 (p. 606); the Indian Army consuming about a third of Indian tax revenue (p. 607); a French Congo trading post where rubber output correlated with bullets used, from an unnamed study (p. 605). These are official colonial records, so they see violence as the colonizer counted it.
- **Perpetrators' own words.** Trotha on terror as policy (p. 596); D'Urban and Governor Arthur on extermination (pp. 600-601); Wilhelm II's Hun speech (pp. 592-593); a Congo soldier explaining the severed hands (p. 604). These are the chapter's strongest evidence of intent because they are admissions.
- **European critics and witnesses.** A Belgian senator on Congo porters (p. 603); the missionary Threlkeld on a massacre in New South Wales (p. 599); MacGahan's Daily News report from Batak (p. 612); a Serbian socialist soldier and a British vice-consul on massacres of Albanians and Muslims in 1912-13 (pp. 629, 631); Morel and Casement named as sources of the Congo campaign (p. 605). The perspective is that of European observers, often campaigners. Voices of the colonized are almost absent; resistance leaders (Abd el-Kader, Menelik, Orabi) appear as actors but are not quoted.
- **Schoolbooks and popular culture as evidence of attitudes.** British geography textbooks and the Juvenile Missionary Magazine of 1866 for the earlier view (p. 618); later textbooks, Henty, the Daily Express, Empire Day and the Boy Scouts for the racist and jingoist turn (pp. 619-620); Belgian human exhibitions (p. 585). The evidence is overwhelmingly British, although the claim is about Europe.
- **Racial theorists read directly.** Haeckel, Galton, Pearson (quoted at length), Gobineau, H. S. Chamberlain, Bernhardi, Riezler (pp. 620-622). Evans shows what they wrote; that such ideas shaped statesmen's decisions is asserted more than demonstrated.
- **Named theorists engaged as rivals.** Hobson, Lenin and Luxemburg on economic imperialism and German Social Democrats on diversion (p. 586), rebutted by chronology. Unnamed *some historians* on the inevitability of European dominance after 1500 (p. 595) and unnamed *later historians* who allege German premeditation in 1914 (p. 640). Evans names no modern historian anywhere in the chapter.
- **Diplomatic documents and remarks for 1914.** Crowe's 1907 memorandum and his minute of 25 July 1914 (pp. 634, 644); Nicolson in 1912 (p. 644); Delcassé's Cabinet report of 1900 (p. 633); the Kaiser's letter of 6 July and marginal comment of 27 July, Moltke on 13 July, Bethmann Hollweg's circular of 21 July (p. 642); Sazonov's statement (p. 643); Groener's post-war book on the railways (p. 642). This is the perspective of chancelleries and general staffs, and the German and British sides are documented more fully than the Russian, French or Serbian.
- **Popular reactions to war.** Hamburg police reports of workers' pub talk on 29 July 1914 (pp. 646-647); contemporary photographs showing straw boaters and no cloth caps in pro-war crowds (p. 646); a village in south-eastern France and a Cossack settlement recorded by an English traveller (p. 647). The Hamburg material is police surveillance, and (from outside knowledge, not the text) a source Evans has himself edited; it is the chapter's only sustained view from below, and it is German.
- **Prophets of industrial war.** Bloch's Modern Weapons and Modern War (pp. 636-637) and Lamszus's Human Slaughterhouse, which sold 100,000 copies in three months (p. 637), set against Fisher, Moltke, Driant, Vachell, Belloc and Esher on the desirability of war (pp. 637-638). The pro-war voices are officers and writers from the British, French and German elites.

## How the book closes

The last two paragraphs are a coda to the whole book, not only the chapter. Evans says the war ended a century of European hegemony, though not without warning: America was already outstripping Britain and Germany, and independence movements were stirring in India (p. 647). The war destroyed Europe's self-confidence and its economy, which did not recover for more than forty years; the United States became a superpower; the Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires fell (p. 647). Europe's slow and uneven march towards democracy, the subject of ch07, was reversed; Communism, Nazism and Fascism brought extreme violence, and genocide came on a scale that dwarfed the Balkan Wars and the Armenian massacres (pp. 647-648). The final image is Sir Edward Grey at his Foreign Office window on 3 August 1914, saying that the lamps were going out all over Europe and would not be lit again in his lifetime (p. 648). There is no separate conclusion or epilogue: the book that began with the aftermath of one general war stops at the threshold of the next, and its closing judgement is that the century's order rested on a peace its own imperial success had undone.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **ch01 (legacies of revolution, the Vienna settlement).** The chapter closes the arc opened there. Evans restates that after 1815 statesmen chose co-operation to prevent revolution and fought only limited wars (p. 632), and that British hegemony kept colonial conflict out of Europe (pp. 578, 632). The claim that European peace caused European global dominance (p. 595) makes the Vienna order the precondition of empire.
- **ch07 (challenge of democracy).** The chapter file begins with ch07's ending on optimism in 1901 (p. 570). Ch08 supplies the reversal: democracy's march is undone by the war (pp. 647-648). Mass politics, the popular press and franchise extension from ch07 reappear as the vehicles of jingoism (pp. 585, 619) and as the nationalist electorates of Serbia and Greece (pp. 626-627); the Social Democrats' failure to stop the war (pp. 644-645) tests ch07's account of socialist strength.
- **ch05 (conquest of nature) and ch04 (social revolution).** Steamships, railways, the telegraph and industrial weaponry are the means of empire (pp. 574, 595); the rubber boom driven by tyres and electrical insulation links European technology directly to Congo atrocities (p. 604).
- **ch06 (age of emotion).** Religion, science and Darwin return here as missionary universalism giving way to scientific racism (pp. 618-622). The elite longing for war as release from a prosaic age (p. 638) is a cultural argument that depends on ch06's treatment of fin-de-siècle sensibility.
- **Internal tension: economics.** Evans rejects economic explanations for the scramble (p. 586) yet says economic motives remained paramount in British tropical Africa (p. 602), describes debt as the lever of control in Tunis, Egypt and the Ottoman Empire (pp. 587-588, 614), and says China was left intact because lending to it was more profitable than conquering it (p. 593). The rejection applies to the Marxist stage theory and its timing, not to economic interest as such, but he does not spell this out.
- **Internal tension: periphery versus metropole.** Before 1880 expansion is pushed from the periphery (p. 583); after 1880 the trigger is Bismarck's European calculation and Great Power panic (pp. 588-589), though men on the spot such as Peters and Rhodes still force governments' hands (pp. 589-590). Evans presents this as a change of period, not a contradiction.
- **Internal tension: continuity with Nazism.** He draws explicit lines from South-West Africa to the Third Reich (concentration camps, Rassenschande, identification tags, Eugen Fischer) (p. 597), while also showing British camps, exterminationist language from British governors, and general European racism (pp. 600-602, 620-622). Whether German colonial violence was distinctive is left implied by the word *deliberate* (p. 602).
- **Internal tension: war guilt.** He calls the blank cheque the crucial moment (p. 645) but denies German premeditation (p. 640), calls the Russian position aggression in the Balkans (p. 643), and blames British inattention (p. 646). The result is a distributed-responsibility account in which the structural cause is a generation's imperial mentality.
- **Internal tension: war enthusiasm.** The section on hoping for war documents elite belligerence (pp. 637-638); the last pages insist the mass of Europeans were not enthusiastic (pp. 646-647). The two fit only if the elites, not the peoples, made the war, which is what Evans argues.

## Open questions

- The causal step from colonial racism to the decisions of July 1914 (p. 646) is the chapter's central interpretive claim and its least evidenced. Evans shows the ideas (pp. 620-622) and the decisions (pp. 639-645) but quotes no decision-maker in the crisis reasoning in racial terms about a European opponent; Riezler and Bernhardi are the nearest (p. 622). Does the synthesis treat this as shown or as suggestive?
- How far does the peace-enabled-empire thesis (p. 595) depend on British naval hegemony specifically, and is it consistent with the earlier chapters' account of mid-century wars (Crimea, Italian and German unification)?
- Evans does not name the historians he argues against on 1914 (p. 640) or on European dominance since 1500 (p. 595). A syntopical check against a dedicated study of the July Crisis and against a global-history account of the *great divergence* would show how much of this is consensus and how much is his own position.
- The evidence for changing racial attitudes is nearly all British (pp. 618-620). Is the claimed shift from relative to biological racism equally documented for France, Germany or Russia?
- The voices of the colonized are missing; resistance is narrated through European records. This limits what the notes can say about indigenous motives beyond what Evans infers (for example the mixed motives of 1857, p. 582).
- Possible slips to verify against other sources before relying on them (these are flags from outside knowledge, not from the text): the Transvaal annexation is dated 1877 on p. 581 and 1876 on p. 601; Lloyd George's statement is attached to the first Moroccan crisis of 1905-6 (p. 634) though it is usually dated to 1911; the Zanzibar bombardment is placed the year before 1900 (p. 603) though usually dated 1896; Sir Henry Wilson is called Chief of the General Staff (p. 636); Wakefield's dates are given as 1796–1892 (p. 583); the removal of the Rosetta Stone is tied to the Battle of the Nile in 1798 (p. 573).
- The text has several typographical errors that affect searching: *IMPERIALSM* in a section heading (p. 585), *primary* for primacy and *explores* for explorers (p. 596), *millon* (p. 605), *Reheboth* (p. 597).
