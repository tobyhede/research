# ch02: The Paradoxes of Freedom (pp. 87–162)

## Question this chapter answers

What did the legal and economic freedoms of the first half of the nineteenth century (freedom from serfdom, freedom of trade and enterprise, freedom from guild regulation, freedom from the limits of muscle, wind and water) actually do to the lives of the great majority of Europeans, who were peasants and artisans? Evans's answer is that each freedom brought a new dependence. Emancipation ended legal servitude across the Continent and was the century's largest single reform, yet it left most peasants with debt, too little land, and often the status of wage labourers or near-serfs, and it stored up revolts that ran to 1907. The doctrines of political economy that justified free markets also justified the workhouse and the withholding of relief in the Irish famine. Steam power freed production from natural limits while subjecting workers to factory discipline, danger and the destruction of artisan security. By the mid-1840s the result was a recognised *social question*, a nascent working class, and a peasantry whose behaviour would decide the outcome of 1848.

Evans never states what the paradoxes of the title are; the word does not appear in the chapter text. The formulation above is my reading of how the eleven sections fit the title.

## Section guide

- **Lords and Serfs (pp. 87-94)**: opens with the memoir of the Russian serf Savva Purlevsky, then maps where serfdom survived after 1815, defines it (obligations, monopolies, seigneurial courts, sale of serfs) and insists that its worst feature was powerlessness and humiliation, not only cost.
- **The Great Emancipation (pp. 94-99)**: why serfdom ended (serf resistance above all, agrarian inefficiency, war and international pressure, conservative calculation), how the legislation and compensation worked, and the scale of the transfer. Ends with the verdict that this was the century's greatest act of reform.
- **Winners and Losers (pp. 99-102)**: who gained. Lords lost jurisdiction and privilege but mostly did well financially; market-oriented peasants gained; smallholders fell into debt and a landless rural proletariat grew, in places under laws amounting to quasi-serfdom.
- **Peasant Revolts (pp. 102-111)**: the aftershocks: Russia 1861-3 and 1905-7, the great Romanian rising, Portugal's Maria da Fonte revolt, Carlism and southern anarchism in Spain, sharecropping, brigandage and the Sicilian Fasci in Italy, banditry across the south and east, and the slow replacement of sharecropping by wage labour.
- **Feeding the People (pp. 111-119)**: the rural majority, population growth under the demographic ancien régime, agricultural improvement and its limits, pauperism, and the shift in poor relief from Church charity to the deterrent logic of the English New Poor Law of 1834.
- **The Hungry Forties and Beyond (pp. 119-129)**: new crops and potato dependence; the blight of 1845-9 across Europe; why Ireland was a catastrophe when Belgium and Highland Scotland were not; later famines in Finland, Sweden and Russia (1891-2); the end of famine in most of Europe by 1914.
- **The Textile Revolution (pp. 129-136)**: cotton, British lead explained by world trade and empire, the spread of mechanised textiles to the Continent, and the Voortman mill in Ghent as a case study of factory discipline, female and child labour, and family precarity.
- **Coal and Iron (pp. 136-143)**: steam as the decisive breakthrough; British coal and iron dominance; mining disasters; diffusion of British men and machines to Belgium and beyond; the state's role (factory acts in Britain; tariffs, customs union, state enterprise and its retreat in Prussia); industrialisation as a regional, cross-border process.
- **Rail, Steam and Speed (pp. 143-153)**: roads, canals, steamboats, then railways: British capital, equipment and navvies on the Continent, state finance, import substitution, the experience of speed, accidents, and the telegraph as a precondition of 1848.
- **The Making of the European Working Class (pp. 153-160)**: the guild system and its dissolution; impoverished artisans as the recruiting ground of early industry; compagnonnage; the weakness of early British unions; the backward-looking character of protest; the Silesian weavers of 1844 as a transitional case; the words *proletariat* and *working class*.
- **Mapping the 'Social Question' (pp. 160-162)**: how educated contemporaries registered the change (Heine, Riehl, von Mohl, Disraeli, Dickens, Sue and imitators, Bettina von Arnim), the absence of peasants from this literature, and the bridge to 1848.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Serfdom | p. 87 | A customary, locally variable system obliging the peasant to unpaid labour, dues in kind and menial services for a seigneur, with restricted movement and subjection to the lord's justice. Not slavery (serfs had rights as well as duties, pp. 90-91) but a servile status that was always clear (p. 91). Its core, for Evans, is powerlessness (p. 88). |
| Seigneur | p. 87 | The landowning lord holding rights over serfs: labour services, dues, monopolies (milling, liquor, hunting, fishing), courts and police (pp. 91-93). Evans uses the French word for lords everywhere in Europe. |
| Emancipation | p. 94 | The ending of servile status and seigneurial rights by state legislation between the French Revolution and the 1860s (later in Bulgaria, Iceland, Bosnia). Personal freedom was granted free; loss of labour services and dues was compensated (p. 97). |
| Redemption payments | p. 97 | Instalments former serfs paid, usually without state help, to compensate lords for lost services and dues: over 15 years in Romania, 25 in Saxony, 49 in Russia (p. 97). The mechanism by which freedom became debt. |
| Landless rural proletariat | p. 101 | Former peasants bought out or dispossessed after emancipation who live wholly or partly by farm wages; later generalised as people who only had their labour to sell (p. 102). |
| Quasi-serfdom | p. 102 | Evans's label for post-emancipation legal regimes restricting rural labourers (Prussian Statute of Farm Servants 1810, Hungarian Farm Servants Act 1907); also the labour-rent dependency of Romanian peasants (p. 104). |
| Sharecropping | p. 106 | Contract under which a family works a landowner's fields for a share of the crop (half in Tuscany), with controls on marriage, movement and labour that Evans presents as close to servile (pp. 107-108). |
| Jacquerie | p. 103 | Traditional peasant rising: storming and burning manor houses and their records, seizing land. Romania's is called the last great one in European history (p. 104). |
| Demographic ancien régime | p. 112 | Historians' term for a population regime with very high birth and death rates (about 45 births and 30-40 deaths per 1,000). |
| Pauperism | p. 116 | The growing mass of destitute people needing support, seen by middle-class elites as a product of idleness and a political threat (p. 117). |
| Less eligibility | p. 118 | Principle of the 1834 New Poor Law: the pauper's condition must be made worse than the independent labourer's, so relief is given only in a deliberately unpleasant workhouse. |
| Political Economy | pp. 118-119 | The body of liberal economic doctrine (capitalised by Evans) holding that the able-bodied destitute are idle; applied to poor relief and, with calamitous results, to famine relief (p. 119). |
| The hungry forties | p. 119 | The subsistence crisis of 1845-9 (potato blight plus grain failure), called the deepest and most devastating of the century (p. 129). |
| Proto-industry / putting-out | p. 122, p. 129 | Rural domestic manufacture (handloom, spinning wheel) sold through a middleman; seasonal, unreliable, dependent on women's and children's labour (p. 129). Its collapse alongside agriculture deepened the 1840s crisis in Flanders and Silesia (pp. 122, 129). |
| Industrial revolution | p. 136 | A term first used in France in the 1820s. For Evans the decisive element is not the large factory, which existed earlier, but steam replacing natural power (p. 136). |
| Time-discipline | p. 134 | The regime of bells, fixed hours, locked doors and fines that the factory imposed, contrasted with the handloom weaver's control over his own hours. |
| Industrial substitution | p. 152 | Continental countries replacing imported British rails and locomotives with their own production once railways were under way. |
| Guilds | p. 153 | Incorporated bodies regulating craft production, limiting output and entry to secure a decent living and high standards; also sources of welfare, identity and collective action (pp. 153-154). |
| Compagnonnage | p. 156 | French journeymen's secret societies supporting the tour de France; had some trade-union functions but were divided by status rivalry and in decline by the 1830s (pp. 156-157). |
| Proletariat / working class | p. 160 | A new class comprising poor single masters, factory workers, miners and urban wage labourers: all who lack property and live entirely from their own and their families' physical labour. |
| The social question | p. 160 | Contemporaries' name for the anxiety about industrial poverty and class division, dramatised in the social novels of the 1840s. |

## Leading propositions

**Serfdom and emancipation**

- The peasantry was almost wholly absent from the revolutions of 1830, and the rural world was the setting for the lives of the overwhelming majority of Europeans (p. 87).
- What mattered about serfdom was the powerlessness and humiliation of the serf, more than the financial burden (pp. 88, 90, 94).
- By Purlevsky's time most of Europe's serfs lived in European Russia; serfdom had ended in much of the west under the impact of 1789 but survived in Hanover and Saxony to the 1830s, in Prussia in weakened form to 1848, in Austria and Hungary, in Russia and Poland to the 1860s, in Bulgaria to 1880, in Iceland in effect to 1894 and in Bosnia to the First World War (p. 90).
- The burden was heavy: in the Habsburg Monarchy the average serf farmer was estimated to pay over 40 per cent of income to state and seigneur together (p. 92).
- Serfdom had two sides: law and custom obliged the seigneur to support serfs in hard times, and serfs had grazing, gleaning and wood rights (p. 93).
- The first and most important pressure for abolition was the growing discontent of the serfs themselves; economic inefficiency, international factors and conservative political calculation also worked for it (pp. 94-96).
- Conservative governments freed the serfs partly to secure peasant loyalty against liberalism, on a generally inaccurate assumption that peasants were conservative and monarchist (p. 96).
- The key issue in every settlement was the level of compensation to the lords; revolutionary decrees of abolition without compensation acquired compensation by the time they were implemented (p. 97).
- In principle emancipation was the greatest single act of emancipation and reform in nineteenth-century Europe, ending legally prescribed social distinctions (p. 99).
- Landowners, except in Poland, did reasonably well out of emancipation, and large estates grew (pp. 100-101).
- Emancipation benefited market-oriented peasants with substantial farms; smaller peasants fell into debt and many returned to working for the lord (p. 101).
- The integration of agriculture into the capitalist economy created growing numbers of people with only their labour to sell, in places held in quasi-serfdom by law (p. 102).

**Rural protest**

- Emancipation disappointed those it affected from the outset; in Russia the dissatisfaction grew over time (p. 102).
- The main problem in Russia was that emancipation did little to improve agricultural production (p. 102).
- Crises of the state gave peasants their opportunity: Russia in 1905, Sicily in 1860, Spain in 1868 (pp. 103, 107, 109-110).
- The Romanian rising was the largest and most violent peasant uprising in Europe between 1815 and 1914, with an estimated 11,000 killed (p. 105).
- In Spain, because liberals carried out disentailment and the sale of commons, peasant protest was anti-liberal, unlike elsewhere; Carlism aimed above all at reversing liberal agrarian reform (pp. 106-107).
- The targets of peasant fury were everywhere the same: legal documents and their authors, on the belief that a natural right to common land outweighed the law (p. 110).
- Banditry commonly followed the abolition of serfdom and only rarely took a political form (pp. 108-109).
- Sharecropping was the main cause of low Italian grain yields and gave way by 1914 to a rural wage-labour economy (pp. 110-111).

**Subsistence, poor relief, famine**

- The typical European for most of the century was a peasant (p. 112).
- Population rose from about 205 million in 1800 to about 275 million in 1850, chiefly because the great epidemics disappeared (p. 112).
- Agricultural output roughly kept pace with population, largely because more land was cultivated; the profit motive was the most effective driver of improvement and only took hold after emancipation (pp. 113-114).
- The 1834 New Poor Law replaced charity with a calculation of rewards and punishments, and its spirit spread to other countries (pp. 118-119).
- The doctrine that the able-bodied destitute were idle had calamitous consequences in Ireland (p. 119).
- The potato blight hit all of Europe, but outcomes differed with relief arrangements and elite interest: decentralised and energetic relief in Belgium, landlord intervention in Highland Scotland, neither in Ireland (pp. 121-123).
- In Ireland about a million people died, around a fifth of the population (Evans's figure), and uniquely most died of actual starvation, not epidemic disease (p. 124).
- By the end of the 1870s railways and administrative vigilance meant bad harvests seldom led to famine; Russia in 1891-2 was the striking exception (pp. 126-127).
- Witte's blaming of the peasants in 1899 showed a misunderstanding of Russian society that would prove fatal to the regime (pp. 128-129).

**Industry, transport, labour**

- Britain's industrial lead was the product of world trade and global empire, not of ingenuity or domestic factors (p. 130).
- Machinery was introduced in Britain because labour costs were high; low wages elsewhere delayed adoption (pp. 130-131, 146).
- The factory introduced a ferocious new time-discipline, and the factory did nothing to alleviate existing poverty and in some respects worsened it (pp. 134, 135-136).
- Steam was the decisive breakthrough, freeing society from natural limits on power (p. 136).
- British coal expansion was achieved at a high price paid by the workers (p. 138).
- Much Continental innovation was the direct result of importing British men and machinery (p. 139).
- The state supplied the legal, institutional and financial framework; liberal economists gradually pushed it out of direct ownership, except in railways (pp. 140-143).
- A major and often neglected effect of the German Customs Union was to protect German industry from British competition (p. 142).
- Industrialisation followed coal and ore deposits, not state borders (p. 143).
- On the Continent railway-building preceded industrialisation, so capital came from Britain or the state (p. 148).
- The railways' main economic effect was cheap bulk transport, which then drove Continental industry through import substitution (pp. 151-152).
- Railways and the telegraph created the communicative basis without which the revolutions of 1848 would not have broken out in so many countries at once (p. 153).
- The reservoir of labour for early industry was the overstocked, impoverished artisanate, not the peasantry or landless labourers (pp. 155-156).
- Most protest before 1848 was backward-looking and communal; machine-breaking was a form of wage bargaining, and unusual (p. 159).
- The Silesian weavers' rising of 1844 was transitional: a wage demand by workers acting as workers, but aimed at individuals (pp. 159-160).
- The distinction between guildsman and wage labourer was eroding and a new class was being formed, in places a hereditary one (p. 160).
- The social novel registered deep anxiety about the new society but ignored the countryside, whose stance would largely decide 1848-9 (p. 162).

## Arguments

**A. Serfdom as a relation of power (pp. 87-94)**

- **Narrative fact**: Purlevsky's village paid 20,000 roubles rent and was then charged 30,000 a year in mortgage interest by new owners; he became steward, fled, and ended as an Odessa sugar merchant who bought his son's freedom in 1856 (pp. 88-89). Obligations in Lithuania, Russia, Austrian Silesia; monopolies and hunting rights; seigneurial courts (pp. 91-93).
- **Interpretive steps**: (p. 88) the decisive experience is impotence before arbitrary demands. (pp. 91-93) the system is a web of rights and duties, limited by state law and with obligations on the lord. (pp. 93-94) but serfs could still be sold, and the humiliation of belonging to someone was among its worst features.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 90) a prosperous, commercialised serf village was undermining serfdom from within, and the resentment was moral as much as material.
- **Unstated premise**: that Purlevsky, literate and by Evans's own account lucky (pp. 89-90), can stand for serf experience generally.

**B. Why serfdom ended (pp. 94-97)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 94-95) Serf resistance, from go-slows and flight to armed risings, was rising; nearly 2,000 Russian disturbances in 1826-40 and troops used 903 times in 1857-61. (p. 95) Rulers concluded repression would not work. (pp. 95-96) Servile labour blocked agricultural improvement. (p. 96) Great Power interests required a viable Romania. (p. 96) Conservatives saw a loyal peasantry as a bulwark against liberalism. (pp. 96-97) Accessions and revolutions (1830-1, 1848) supplied the occasions.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 96) political forces of many kinds converged; (p. 97) rulers such as Franz Joseph came to see abolition as inevitable and advantageous to the state.
- **Unstated premise**: the ranking of serf discontent as first and most important (p. 94) is asserted, not weighed against the economic and fiscal motives; the evidence shown for each factor is illustrative.

**C. The paradox of emancipation: legal freedom, economic dependence (pp. 97-102)**

- **Premises**: (p. 97) Personal freedom came free, but services and dues had to be bought out, usually by the peasants alone, over decades. (p. 98) Land terms ranged from Romania (lords kept the best land) to Poland (peasants gained). (pp. 98-99) The scale was vast and the legal change real. (pp. 100-101) Compensation capital let large owners buy out weaker ones. (p. 101) Small peasants, unused to money, fell into debt; population growth shrank holdings to garden size. (p. 102) Farm-servant laws kept labourers under corporal punishment and restricted movement.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 99) a great act of reform in principle; (pp. 101-102) in practice the creation of a landless rural proletariat and, in places, quasi-serfdom.
- **Unstated premise**: that the growth of landlessness is attributable to the emancipation terms; Evans also cites population growth (p. 101) and does not apportion the two.

**D. From disappointed emancipation to revolt (pp. 102-111)**

- **Premises**: (p. 102) Russian peasants held that the land was theirs and rejected compensation. (pp. 102-103) Output per head did not improve enough; access to forest and meadow was lost; wood theft doubled as a share of crime. (p. 103) Redemption payments and rising rents weighed on them until a state crisis in 1905 opened the way. (p. 104) In Romania delay, tiny holdings, loss of pasture rights and labour rents reduced peasants to near-serfdom, without the vote. (pp. 106-110) In Portugal, Spain and Italy liberal land registration, disentailment and enclosure of commons provoked revolt.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 110) across Europe the peasants attacked the documents and officials that embodied their dispossession; (p. 104) reform followed revolt (end of redemption payments, Stolypin's measures) but did not dissolve attachment to the commune.
- **Unstated premise**: that risings as different as Carlism, the Sicilian Fasci and the 1905 jacqueries share a cause in the terms on which feudal tenures were liquidated.

**E. Why Ireland starved (pp. 116-124)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 117-119) British elites had adopted a doctrine that relief breeds idleness and must deter. (p. 120) Ireland depended on the potato more than any other country. (p. 121) Belgium, with local relief and elites invested in a new state, spent heavily. (p. 122) In the Highlands the same Treasury doctrine applied, but new commercial landlords with outside wealth paid for relief. (p. 123) Ireland had centralised relief, absentee landlords without national ties or industrial wealth, and the Corn Laws kept food dear while grain was exported. (pp. 123-124) Government measures were patchy and slow, and elite opinion blamed the Irish.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 124) a million deaths, most from starvation; (p. 119) the doctrine of political economy bears responsibility.
- **Unstated premise**: the comparison treats Belgium, Scotland and Ireland as facing the same shock; Evans's own figures show a 1846 Irish yield 88 per cent below normal (p. 121) and far greater dependence on the potato than anywhere else (p. 120), so policy and scale are not separated.

**F. Britain's lead and its diffusion (pp. 129-143)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 129-130) Cotton used slave-grown and Indian raw material; exports exceeded home sales by 1814; India and Egypt were de-industrialised, the latter by force. (p. 130) Naval dominance gave near-monopoly in Latin America. (pp. 130-131) High wages made machinery pay in Britain and not elsewhere. (p. 136) Coal and steam removed natural limits. (pp. 139-140) Export bans were unenforceable; machines, plans and men crossed to the Continent. (pp. 141-143) States supplied tariffs, transport and capital.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 130) the British industrial economy was a product of global empire as well as technology; (p. 143) Continental industrialisation was a cross-border process of borrowing.
- **Unstated premise**: the denial that ingenuity or domestic factors explain the lead (p. 130) sits beside a high-wage explanation (p. 130) and a resource explanation (pp. 136, 138), both domestic.

**G. Railways as leading sector and political precondition (pp. 143-153)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 143-145) Roads were slow and mostly impassable to wheels; waterways carried bulk. (p. 146) Railways proved hugely profitable. (p. 148) Continental countries lacked prior industry and private capital, so the British or the state financed them. (pp. 151-152) Freight costs fell to about a quarter of road rates; demand for rails and engines was met domestically within a decade. (p. 153) Telegraph and rail sped information across borders.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 148) railways became the leading sector; (p. 153) the simultaneous outbreaks of 1848 depended on the new communications.
- **Unstated premise**: the 1848 claim is a counterfactual stated without evidence here; governments, Evans notes on the same page, censored the telegraph and barred private messages.

**H. Where the working class came from (pp. 153-160)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 154-155) States abolished guild privileges and factories bypassed guilds. (p. 155) Masters and journeymen multiplied in Prussia, with journeymen rising faster, so promotion broke down. (p. 156) Three-quarters of Berlin masters were too poor to pay business tax; the choice became pauperism or the factory. (pp. 157-158) Unions were weak, local and repeatedly defeated. (pp. 158-159) Protest was led by literate artisans and was mostly defensive.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 155-156) early industry drew on the artisanate; (p. 160) guildsmen and wage labourers were merging into one class.
- **Unstated premise**: the Prussian guild figures are taken as representative of Europe; the Ghent case earlier in the chapter shows a largely local, family-recruited and heavily female and child workforce (p. 135), which is not obviously an artisan reservoir.

## Evidence offered

Evans gives no footnotes or source references in the text. Where a historian's work is used it is signalled only by phrases such as *historians have counted*, *it has been estimated*, *a study* or *research has uncovered* (pp. 94, 114, 159). The types of evidence are:

- **Memoir, serf's perspective**: Savva Purlevsky's account of Velikoe, his flight and later career (pp. 87-90). A literate, upwardly mobile serf; Evans concedes his life was a lucky one (pp. 89-90).
- **Memoir, noble perspective**: Kropotkin on his father's 1,200 serfs and private piano-tuner (p. 94).
- **Travellers and outside observers**: Haxthausen on the Skoptsy (p. 89); an unnamed traveller told by a Russian landowner about bribing judges (p. 93); Kogălniceanu on Spanish bandits (p. 108); Krasiński on beggars in Messina and English harshness (p. 116); Brayley Hodgetts of Reuters and the Swede Jonas Stadling on the 1891 Russian famine (p. 127). These carry the view of educated outsiders.
- **Rulers and officials in their own words**: Alexander II on abolition from above; a government inspector in 1832; Gorchakov (p. 95); Trevelyan (p. 122); Polovtsov (pp. 127-128); Witte (p. 128); Bismarck on the Frankfurt guilds (p. 154); von Rother of the Seehandlung (p. 143).
- **Lords' complaints**: an Austrian aristocrat, a Russian marshal of the nobility, Windischgrätz (pp. 99-100); a Bologna conservative on sharecroppers in 1883 (p. 111).
- **Peasant voices, mediated**: the Perm villagers and the popular phrase about the land (p. 102); a Russian petition (p. 103); the Moldavian petition (pp. 104-105); the Sardinian villager to the Bishop of Sassari (p. 108); a brigand leader before a military tribunal (p. 108); Princip at his trial (p. 90). All reach us through official or legal records.
- **Official reports**: the prefect's report on the shelling of Baielesti (p. 105); the Russian inquiry after 1905-7 (p. 103); the Poor Law report and an assistant commissioner (p. 118); the Factory Commissions' accident count for 1840 (p. 134); the 1842 mines report (p. 141); an Italian inquiry of 1881 (p. 111); a workers' strike handbill listing the Tyldesley mill rules, 1823 (p. 134).
- **Newspapers**: Sheffield Independent on Clonlolan, the Cork magistrate's letter (p. 124); the Wallsend and Pembrokeshire pit disasters (p. 137); the Huskisson accident and the 1842 Paris crash (p. 151); León in 1897 (p. 126).
- **Statistics, unsourced**: emancipation totals (pp. 98-99); Habsburg serf burden (p. 92); landholding and wage-labour shares (pp. 100-101, 104); urbanisation and population growth rates (pp. 112-113); potato crop losses and excess mortality by country (pp. 121-123); Russian famine deaths by province, 406,643 excess deaths less 103,364 cholera deaths (p. 128); cotton imports, exports and loom counts (pp. 129-131); coal and pig-iron shares (pp. 136, 138); railway investment and locomotive origins (pp. 148-149, 152); Prussian masters and journeymen (p. 155); counts of German protest incidents (p. 159). Much the densest kind of evidence in the chapter.
- **Counts of unrest**: Russian disturbances 1826-40 and 1857-61 (p. 94); categories of rural crime in 1905-7 (p. 103); 186 cases of machine-breaking in the German Confederation 1816-48 (p. 159).
- **Micro-history**: the Voortman mill and the Bauters family in Ghent, with workforce shares, diet survey and the deaths of eleven of twelve children by twenty-five (pp. 133-135). The most detailed single case; no source named.
- **Literary evidence**: Pushkin on roads (p. 144); Carlyle, Burckhardt, Turner, Eichendorff on speed (pp. 150-151); Dickens, Disraeli, Sue, Reynolds, Brass, Willkomm, von Arnim, Balzac, Cladel on the social question (pp. 119, 160-162).
- **Contemporary reference works and theorists**: Brockhaus's encyclopedia in 1839 and 1846 (pp. 156, 160); Blanqui on the industrial revolution (p. 136); von Mohl and Riehl (p. 160); Malthus (pp. 113, 118).
- **Anecdote used for colour**: Gregory XVI and St Peter (p. 147); Stephenson on Spain (p. 147); the single non-British Prussian locomotive that did not work (pp. 148-149); lamb klephtiko (p. 109).

**Whose perspective.** The numbers are the state's and later historians'; the voices are disproportionately those of officials, landlords, travellers and writers. Peasants and workers speak through petitions, trials, strike handbills and one exceptional memoir. Evans notes the bias once, on the 1905-7 inquiry that blamed agitators (p. 103).

**Language outrunning evidence.** The ranking of causes of emancipation (p. 94); *largely a result of the dominance of sharecropping* for Italian yields (p. 110); the flat denial of domestic causes for Britain's lead (p. 130); the 1848 counterfactual (p. 153); Witte's views as fatal for the regime (pp. 128-129); the measures were put into effect *with a minimum of fuss* (pp. 98-99), which the following section on revolts qualifies heavily.

**Apparent slips or oddities in the text** (flag before reuse): Estonian abolition is dated 1817 on p. 90 and 1816 on p. 94. The Saxon declaration is placed in *the following year* directly after an 1856 quotation (p. 95), though Saxon abolition is dated to the early 1830s on p. 90. The Romanian rising is given no year in the text, only 15 March (pp. 104-105). A Seville uprising of June 1857 is listed under the 1860s, and a Spanish republic is dated 1875 (p. 107). The First Carlist War is dated 1832-9 in the text and the map title gives 1833 (pp. 105-106). The Amalgamated Society of Engineers is placed at the start of the 1830s with 12,000 members in 1831 (pp. 157-158), which should be checked against other sources. The 1816-17 deaths are attributed to bubonic plague in the Balkans (p. 124).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Picks up ch01**: ch01 closes by naming the peasantry as the force missing from 1830 (p. 87); this chapter supplies that rural context. The 1816-17 famine and the economic crisis of 1815-16, treated in ch01, are the baseline against which the 1840s crisis and the Old Poor Law's breakdown are measured (pp. 118, 121, 124). Purlevsky's memoir opening mirrors the Jakob Walter opening of ch01.
- **Sets up ch03 (The European Spring)**: the last sentence makes the stance of the peasantry the determinant of 1848-9 (p. 162); the telegraph and railways are named as the communicative basis of 1848 (p. 153); the lesson that unions need political action (p. 158) and the artisan leadership of protest (pp. 158-159) prepare the account of radical politics. The remaining feudal dues abolished in 1848 are already counted here (pp. 96-97).
- **Forward pointers inside the text**: Bosnian serfdom and Princip in 1914 (p. 90); kulaks and Stalin (p. 104); 1905-7 and 1917 (pp. 103, 162); the full impact of railways after mid-century (p. 152); Witte and the fate of tsarism (p. 129). ch04 (The Social Revolution) and ch05 (The Conquest of Nature), not read in this pass, are where the later history of classes, industry and transport should be checked against these claims.
- **Tension (my observation), emancipation verdict**: the measures were implemented with little fuss and constituted the century's greatest reform (pp. 98-99), yet the next two sections describe disappointment from the outset (p. 102), quasi-serfdom (p. 102), and risings into the twentieth century. Evans holds both by the qualifier *in principle* (p. 99); how much weight that qualifier carries is the chapter's central ambiguity.
- **Tension (my observation), Britain's lead**: domestic factors are denied (p. 130) while high wages (p. 130), coal endowment (p. 136) and deforestation driving coke smelting (p. 138) are all given as causes.
- **Tension (my observation), the state**: the state is the indispensable financier, tariff-setter and builder (pp. 141, 148), and its withdrawal from enterprise is presented as freeing the economy (pp. 142-143). Both are asserted; the Customs Union is at once free trade inside and protection outside (p. 142).
- **Tension (my observation), labour supply**: the artisanate, not the peasantry, is the reservoir of early industrial labour (pp. 155-156), while miners are migrants drawn to the coalfields (p. 136), mill hands are women and children of local families (p. 135), and navvies are casual labourers (p. 149).
- **Tension (my observation), peasant politics**: the assumption that peasants were conservative is called rather inaccurate (p. 96), yet Spanish peasant protest is shown as royalist and anti-liberal (pp. 106-107) and Sicilian Fasci carried portraits of the king (p. 110). The apparent rule is that peasants opposed whoever liquidated the commons.
- **Shift in the word freedom**: legal freedom of person (p. 99), freedom of enterprise and trade (pp. 142, 154, 155), freedom from natural limits (p. 136), and the Ghent workers' Freedom Tree, which the mill-owner told them to eat (p. 133). The chapter title depends on running these together.

## Open questions

- What exactly does Evans mean by the paradoxes of freedom? The chapter never says. Check the preface note and the synthesis for whether he states the theme elsewhere, or whether it is left to the reader.
- How much of post-emancipation landlessness is due to the settlement terms and how much to population growth (p. 101)? Evans gives both without weighing them.
- Is the ranking of serf resistance above economic motives in causing abolition (p. 94) a position in a historiographical debate? He names no opposing view.
- The Irish famine comparison (pp. 121-124): does the contrast with Belgium and Scotland isolate policy, given the difference in scale of dependence and crop loss? And is one million, a fifth of the population (p. 124), consistent with other figures for Ireland's pre-famine population?
- The claim that 1848 would not have been simultaneous without telegraph and rail (p. 153): does ch03 supply evidence of news travelling by these means, given that telegraphs were state-controlled and networks thin in 1848?
- The global-empire explanation of British industrialisation (p. 130) is stated as settled. Which historians is Evans following, and does ch08 on empire return to it?
- Do the datings flagged under Evidence (Estonia, Saxony, the Romanian revolt, Seville, the Spanish republic, the Amalgamated Society of Engineers) reflect errors in the book, in this edition's text, or deliberate compression? Verify before citing.
- The Romanian death toll of 11,000 (p. 105) is given as an estimate with no source; treat as contested until checked.
- The social novel ignored the peasants (p. 162). Does Evans's own balance, with six of eleven sections on the countryside, amount to a claim against an urban-centred historiography? Compare with the preface.
