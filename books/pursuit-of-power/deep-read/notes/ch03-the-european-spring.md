# ch03: The European Spring (pp. 162–253)

## Question this chapter answers

Why did the revolutions of 1848 break out across almost the whole Continent at once, why did they collapse within eighteen months, and what did they nonetheless change? Evans's answer has three parts. First, the revolutions came from the meeting of new ideas (socialism, nationalism, moderate liberalism on the British model) with a Continent-wide economic crisis and a set of rulers who expected 1789 to repeat itself and gave way. Second, they failed because the revolutionary camp split three ways (liberals against democrats, nationality against nationality, town against countryside) while the professional armies stayed loyal to the old states. Third, failure was not the end of the story: a new generation of flexible conservatives (Napoleon III, Cavour, Bismarck, Disraeli) took over the revolution's agenda, above all nationalism and mass participation, and carried it out from above through war and plebiscite. Evans therefore proposes that 1848 to 1871 be read as one period of revolutionary change that tore up the Vienna Settlement and produced a political order lasting almost to 1914 (pp. 246-247).

The narrative fact and the interpretation are easy to separate here. The narrative is a country-by-country chronicle of risings, battles and treaties. The interpretation sits in the opening and closing paragraphs of sections and in the two reflective sections, The Limits of Change and Echoes of Revolution.

## Section guide

- **Visions of the Machine Age (pp. 162-170)**: opens with the life of Flora Tristan, then surveys the Utopian socialists (Fourier, Cabet, Saint-Simon and Enfantin, Blanc, Owen, Weitling), Proudhon, Hegel and the Russian and Young Hegelians, Bakunin and the early Marx. Establishes the stock of ideas available in 1848 and the link between women's and workers' emancipation.
- **Nationalism and Liberalism (pp. 170-178)**: defines two types of early nationalism and warns against reading later aggressiveness back into it; Mazzini and Young Italy; Metternich's Austria as the obstacle; moderate liberal reform and its limits in Italy, Hungary, Switzerland, the German states, Britain (Whigs, Peel, Chartism, Corn Laws) and Guizot's France.
- **The Spectre of 1789 (pp. 178-189)**: Galicia 1846 and the economic crisis; the banquet campaign and the fall of Louis-Philippe; why 1848 was European; the spring risings in Italy, the German states, Vienna, Hungary, Milan, Venice, Berlin; the Frankfurt Parliament. Ends by insisting on the scale of the upheaval as it looked in summer 1848.
- **The Revolution Falls Apart (pp. 189-198)**: Schleswig-Holstein and the powerlessness of Frankfurt; the National Workshops and the June Days; rival nationalisms in Hungary, Transylvania, the Danubian principalities, Posen, the Vojvodina, Croatia and Bohemia; the Habsburg counter-revolution from Prague to Vienna to the defeat of Hungary with Russian help.
- **Radicals and Reactionaries (pp. 198-207)**: the Frankfurt Constitution and Friedrich Wilhelm IV's refusal; the last democratic risings in Saxony, the Rhineland, the Palatinate and Baden; Italy from Rossi's murder to Novara, the Roman Republic and the fall of Venice; Louis-Napoleon's election, coup and empire.
- **The Limits of Change (pp. 207-216)**: Evans's explanation of the failure (militias against armies, liberal fear of the crowd, peasant abstention, rival nationalisms); then the variants and exceptions: Spain, Greece, Albania, Bosnia, Scandinavia, Russia, Britain and its colonies; mid-Victorian liberal stability to the 1867 Reform Act.
- **The Crimean War (pp. 216-225)**: post-revolutionary regimes and their turn to economic development; Napoleon III's plebiscitary dictatorship; origins, conduct and incompetence of the war; its results for Russia (the reforms of Alexander II), the Ottomans, Austria and France, and the end of the Concert of Europe.
- **Success and Failure of the National Cause (pp. 225-232)**: Cavour's strategy, Austrian neo-absolutism, Orsini, Plombières, the war of 1859, Garibaldi's Thousand and the Kingdom of Italy; the Europe-wide boost to nationalism; the Polish rising of 1863-4 and its suppression.
- **Steering a Course on the Stream of Time (pp. 232-246)**: the Liberal Empire; the Prussian constitutional conflict; Bismarck's character and aims; the wars of 1864, 1866 and 1870-1; the North German Confederation; the Austro-Hungarian Compromise; the proclamation of the German Empire.
- **Echoes of Revolution (pp. 246-253)**: the balance sheet of 1848-71; abolition of colonial slavery; feminism in 1848; socialism, Marx, the First International, the Paris Commune and the split between Marxists and anarchists. Hands over to ch04.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Evans's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Utopian socialists | p. 164 | Mainly French writers since 1789 who sketched an ideal society and expected model communities to convert the world by example. Evans agrees with Tristan that they lacked realism (p. 165) but credits them with coining the vocabulary: communism (Cabet, p. 165), socialism (Leroux in French, p. 166; Owen in English, p. 167), feminism (Fourier, p. 167). |
| Nationalism (two types) | pp. 170-171 | Type one: freeing a nation from a foreign yoke, which for most meant autonomy or recognition of language and culture, not independence. | Moderate liberals | p. 173 | Propertied constitutional monarchists who took Britain as their model, wanted parliaments, legal equality, free trade and a restricted franchise, and sought reform in order to avoid revolution. | Democrats / radicals | p. 176 | Those who wanted universal male suffrage and usually a republic, inspired by 1789-93 not by Britain (p. 207). Many were former carbonari who now led crowds instead of conspiracies (p. 201). |
| English gradualism / French insurrectionism | p. 179 | The two rival scripts of political change. 1848 is the moment the second temporarily displaced the first on the Continent. |
| March Demands | p. 182 | The items of Struve's Mannheim petition (free press, jury trial, militia with elected officers, constitutions, an all-German parliament) as they circulated through the German states. |
| National Guard / citizens' militia | p. 180, p. 208 | The moderate liberals' instrument for replacing royal armies and keeping order; a double-edged sword because its members wavered between order and the street, and no match for regular troops (pp. 208-209). |
| June Days | p. 204 | The Paris workers' rising of late June 1848 after the closing of the National Workshops (narrated pp. 190-191); the event that pushed liberals and conservatives together across Europe. |
| Magyarization | p. 191 | The Hungarian nationalists' programme of imposing Magyar on Croats, Serbs, Slovaks, Saxons and Romanians; Evans's main example of liberalism and nationalism contradicting each other. |
| Dialectic of radicalization and reaction | p. 201 | The late-revolution mechanism: the more democrats turned to the masses, the more liberals called on the army of the established authority. |
| Plebiscitary dictatorship | p. 217 | Napoleon III's regime: legitimacy drawn from popular votes that were managed by bribery and intimidation, rule through bureaucracy, police and army, with prosperity and foreign glory to keep support. |
| Neo-absolutism | p. 226 | The Austrian system after the New Year's Eve Patent of 1851: centralized, German-speaking bureaucratic rule, an *a-national* not multinational state, combined with railway-building and peasant emancipation. |
| Concert of Europe | p. 225 | Great Power co-operation to maintain the status quo since 1815; Evans dates its effective end, and the end of monarchical solidarity, to the Crimean War. |

## Leading propositions

Ideas before 1848

- The Utopian socialists, Proudhon, the Hegelians and Marx all tried to come to terms with the two new worlds made by the French Revolution and by industrialism, and all fed into 1848 (pp. 168, 170).
- In the 1840s Marx was a minor figure; Proudhon and others were making the running (p. 170).
- Early nationalism should not be read backwards: most nationalists wanted autonomy or cultural recognition, not independence, and nationalism was largely a vehicle for liberal reform against the Metternich order (pp. 170-171).
- Mazzini changed the tactics of nationalism from secret society and isolated rising to programme, formal organization and propaganda aimed at democratic support (p. 172).
- British liberalism impressed Continental liberals because timely concession avoided revolution; in Britain moderate liberals of both parties saw off the Chartist democrats (pp. 176-177). Guizot's France shows the opposite: a closed franchise, refusal of social reform, and corruption (pp. 177-178).

Outbreak

- The Galician jacquerie of 1846 frightened liberals into action and governments into concessions; under everything lay the harvest failure and depression from 1846 (p. 179).
- The crisis was also one of the industrial age: the centres of 1848 lay in areas hit by British competition, and capital cities were centres of industry with a forming working class (p. 179).
- Rulers expected revolution and the expectation was self-fulfilling; governments were paralysed by the memory of 1789 (pp. 179, 207).
- 1848 differed from 1789 in being European from the start, because railways, steamships, literacy and a shared economic crisis spread news and distress simultaneously (p. 181).
- The fall of the July Monarchy, not the earlier Italian risings, was the real start; Metternich's fall signalled the depth of the upheaval (pp. 182-183).
- The revolutions were not half-hearted: by summer 1848 thrones had been shaken, assemblies created, national self-determination asserted and large social reforms begun (pp. 188-189).

Failure

- The revolutions imploded under their own contradictions (p. 189). The Malmö armistice showed that the Frankfurt Parliament had no armed force of its own and so counted for little (pp. 189-190).
- The June Days united moderate liberals and conservatives across Europe in fear of the masses (p. 191).
- Class was not the only rock: rival nationalisms in east-central Europe opened a contradiction between liberalism and nationalism that let conservatives recover the initiative (p. 191).
- The Habsburg court exploited both national divisions and the liberal-democrat gulf (p. 194). Hungary was beaten by Russian numbers, by the minorities' attacks, and most of all by the better-resourced Austrian army (p. 198).
- Four causes of failure, in ascending order of weight: militias were no match for loyal professional armies; liberals, remembering the Terror, moved towards the forces of order; the peasantry mostly did not support the revolution; and, most serious, the revolutionary camp divided, above all between nationalisms (pp. 208-210).
- The crowds were largely the new working class and looked forward to a democratic state, not back to the guilds (p. 208).
- The Habsburg victory contained the seeds of its decay: Germans learned that unity must exclude Austria and be led by Prussia; Italians that Piedmont needed foreign, probably French, help (p. 210).

Aftermath, 1850-71

- What was restored in 1850 was not the Vienna Europe. Post-revolutionary regimes turned to economic development, statistics, urban improvement and press management (p. 216).
- A new generation of conservatives saw that order required co-opting the masses and that nationalism was unstoppable and could be exploited; their readiness to use foreign policy for this destabilized Europe (pp. 216-217).
- Napoleon III has a claim to be the first modern dictator; his need for popularity and Napoleonic glory made France unpredictable (pp. 217-218).
- The Crimean War was limited and old-fashioned in conduct but large in effect: Russia pushed to the margins, France restored, Austria left friendless, the Concert of Europe ended (pp. 222-225).
- Defeat drove Alexander II's reforms, of which serf emancipation was the most significant (p. 223).
- Cavour unified Italy to pre-empt the democrats: he preserved the social and political order by harnessing nationalism to it (pp. 225, 229).
- Italian unification revived the nation-state idea everywhere (p. 230); Poland in 1863 failed because no power had an interest in helping (p. 232).
- Bismarck saw German unity as inevitable and set out to make sure it preserved Prussia's army, monarchy and aristocracy (p. 235); the North German Confederation's constitution did exactly this (p. 240).
- Universal male suffrage was used by Napoleon III, Bismarck and Disraeli to outflank the liberals by appealing to supposedly conservative masses (pp. 216, 240, 246).
- 1848 set the changes of the 1850s and 1860s in motion; 1848-71 is best seen as one period of revolutionary change (pp. 246-247).
- The Commune was the last gasp of Jacobin insurrection and changed socialism by dividing it into Marxist parties and anarchist violence (pp. 252-253).

## Arguments

1. **Why the revolutions happened together**
   - **Premises**: economic distress from 1846 hit the whole Continent through integrated markets (pp. 179, 181); improved communications spread news fast (p. 181); a growing literate urban workforce was a ready audience (p. 181); rulers were personally unfit and paralysed by 1789 (p. 207); governments had conceded too little too late for decades (pp. 207-208).
   - **Conclusion**: revolution no longer needed French armies to carry it; it broke out in parallel, and established governments were overwhelmed by liberalism and democratic republicanism together (pp. 181, 208).

2. **Why they failed (the central argument)**
   - **Premises**: liberals and democrats wanted different things, a reformed monarchy against a democratic republic (pp. 187-188, 207); liberals restricted the franchise and were ambivalent about relief for the poor, so the street turned against them (p. 208); crowd violence revived memories of 1793-4 and drove liberals to the army (pp. 191, 209); the armies stayed loyal and were pushed on by diehard officers and officials working behind liberal ministries (p. 209); peasants had either been emancipated already or were bought off by Habsburg emancipation, and liberal landowners' wish to privatize common rights alienated them (pp. 209-210); nationalities claimed overlapping territory and the larger ones thought themselves superior (p. 210).
   - **Conclusion**: the dialectic of radicalization and reaction (p. 201) ended with the old powers restored by force, in several cases with foreign help: Russia in Hungary, France in Rome (pp. 197, 202, 204).
   - Note the ranking: Evans says the national divisions were most serious of all (p. 210), yet his narrative gives the decisive military weight to disciplined regular armies (pp. 198, 209). The two are joined by the claim that Vienna exploited the divisions (p. 194).

3. **Why failure still transformed Europe**
   - **Premises**: restored regimes accepted that distress caused the revolutions and so promoted development (p. 216); even reactionary regimes adopted deregulation, education and judicial reform (p. 247); politics moved from secret societies to parties, press and pressure groups (p. 246); conservatives learned to appeal openly to mass loyalty (p. 247).
   - **Conclusion**: the lessons of 1848 were applied by conservatives, who delivered national unification and mass suffrage in a form the revolutionaries had not wanted (p. 246).

4. **How the international order came apart**
   - **Premises**: Napoleon III needed glory and Catholic support (p. 218); the Holy Places dispute became a proxy for Franco-Russian rivalry (p. 219); Austria's hesitant siding with the Allies destroyed its partnership with Russia (p. 224); monarchical solidarity ended (p. 225).
   - **Conclusion**: Austria fought alone in 1859 and 1866 because Russia would not help (pp. 227, 237), which made both unifications possible. The Crimean War is thus the hinge between the failed revolutions and the successful wars.

5. **Unification as conservative pre-emption**
   - Italy: Cavour's liberal constitutional monarchy would not survive democratic nationalism, so Piedmont had to lead (p. 225); Orsini's bombs persuaded Napoleon III of the same logic (p. 227); Garibaldi's conquest of the south was absorbed by plebiscite into Piedmontese institutions (p. 229).
   - Germany: liberals concluded from 1848 that they needed control of the army and a Germany without Austria (pp. 233-234); they lost the first struggle to Bismarck and won the second on his terms. The Indemnity Bill and a Reichstag elected by universal male suffrage with limited powers split and outflanked them (pp. 239-240).
   - **Conclusion**: both states were united as conservatively designed constitutional monarchies (p. 246).

6. **Bismarck and the war of 1870**: Evans rejects both the picture of an innocent Bismarck and the picture of a warmonger. Bismarck secretly encouraged the Hohenzollern candidacy as a lever of diplomatic pressure, not to cause war (p. 242); he then edited the Ems telegram for four stated reasons: the anti-Prussian turn in Bavaria, French military reform, the risk of French allies, and the chance to make France look the aggressor (p. 242).

## Evidence offered

Evans gives no footnotes; the kinds of support are as follows.

- **Biographical vignette as opening device**: Flora Tristan's life and writings, quoted at length, stand for the link between industrial misery and women's subordination (pp. 162-165). Evans gives no source; the words come from her own published works and her husband's pamphlets. Hers is the view of an educated radical outsider looking at English workers.
- **Words of rulers and statesmen**, usually a single line: Franz I on his ramshackle empire (p. 172), Carlo Alberto on the coming crisis (p. 173), Palmerston to the Austrian ambassador (p. 173), Guizot (pp. 177-178), Friedrich Wilhelm IV on the imperial crown (p. 199), Napoleon III on plots in the army (p. 218), Bismarck (p. 235), Disraeli (p. 246). These carry the view from above and are used to show that elites expected revolution or understood the new politics.
- **Eyewitness and memoir fragments**: Tocqueville's warning speech (p. 180), the American diplomat Stiles on the news from Paris (p. 182), Wagner on Bakunin's escape (p. 200), Engels on drunken insurgents (p. 201), Garibaldi on peasant indifference (p. 209), Crispi on the Sicilian moderates (p. 203), Austrian spies and officers in 1866 (pp. 238-239). Mostly participants on the revolutionary or military side; almost no peasant or worker speaks in his own words.
- **Numbers of dead, arrested and troops**, given throughout without source: 800 dead in Berlin (p. 187); June Days about 1,500 killed and 11,727 arrested (p. 191); about 40,000 deaths in Transylvania (p. 192); Haynau's reprisals (p. 198); 27,000 arrests after the 1851 coup and the plebiscite figures (pp. 206-207); Crimean deaths (p. 221); Sadowa (p. 239); Franco-Prussian War losses (p. 245).
- **Social composition statistics**, the one place Evans argues from data: of 727 people convicted in Saxony, 19 per cent master artisans, 26 per cent journeymen, 12 per cent artisans, 12 per cent workers, and 97 per cent native Saxons (pp. 200, 208). He infers that almost all were in practice factory workers, an inference he asserts and does not demonstrate (p. 208). Composition of the Frankfurt Parliament (p. 188).
- **Franchise and electorate figures**: 3.2 per cent of the British against 0.5 per cent of the French population before 1832 (p. 178); the 1867 Reform Act raising the electorate by 88 per cent (p. 215); three million French voters disenfranchised in 1850 (p. 206); the 1869 French vote (p. 233).
- **Military and material comparison** for the Crimea: disease deaths by army (16,000 of 21,000 British, 60,000 of 95,000 French, 72,000 of 143,000 Russian), musket range of 200 yards against 1,000 for Allied rifles, no railways south of Moscow, 400,000 untrained recruits (pp. 221-222). This is the strongest evidential passage for a causal claim (Russian backwardness explains defeat and then reform).
- **Archival and recent research**, named only generically: documents from the Sigmaringen archive that surfaced after the Second World War, on Bismarck and the Spanish candidacy (p. 242); a recent investigation from hospital and burial records putting Commune deaths at 5,700 to 7,400 (p. 252). No historian is named in either case.
- **Historiographical foils**, also unnamed: those who have dismissed 1848 as half-hearted or timid (pp. 189, 209). No historian is named anywhere in the chapter.
- **Counter-cases** used to mark the limits of the pattern: Albania as a revolt against Ottoman reform that produced no nationalism (pp. 211-212); Bosnia (p. 212); Sweden, Norway and Russia as barely touched (pp. 212-213); Britain's colonies as the place where the cost of domestic calm was paid (pp. 213-214).

Perspective overall: high-political and military, built from the words of rulers, generals, diplomats and a few famous radicals. The peasantry, whose abstention is one of the four causes of failure, is described from outside.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **ch01 (The Legacies of Revolution)**: this chapter is the undoing of the settlement described there. Metternich's fall is treated as the symbolic end of the Restoration (pp. 183, 216); the carbonari reappear as the school of the 1848 democrats (p. 201); veterans of the Napoleonic Wars command on both sides (pp. 194, 207). The memory of 1789 and the Terror is the single most used explanatory device (pp. 179, 187, 191, 196, 207, 209).
- **ch02 (The Paradoxes of Freedom)**: the closing sentence of ch02 says the peasantry's stance would decide 1848 (p. 162); this chapter cashes that in (pp. 209-210). The Habsburg emancipation of 1848-53 (pp. 209, 226) and Alexander II's of 1861 (p. 223) are here explained politically, as counter-revolutionary and military measures, complementing the social account in ch02. The crop failures and potato blight are the underlying cause of the crisis (p. 179).
- **ch04 (The Social Revolution)**: explicitly announced. To see why socialism and anarchism won millions of adherents, Evans says he must turn to social and economic development between 1850 and 1914 (p. 253).
- **Forward to the end of the book**: the peace of 1871 stored up resentments that found their outlet in 1914 (p. 245); the borders of the major states then stayed stable for over four decades (p. 246); the Ottoman Empire was soon the sick man again and open to Russian aggression two decades later (p. 223); Garibaldi's red shirts are linked to fascist coloured shirts after the First World War (p. 228); the cult of Garibaldi is the first high point of later hero-worship (p. 229).
- **Tension: failure or success?** Evans says the revolutions should not be called half-hearted failures (p. 189) and also that they were defeated everywhere by September 1849 (p. 206). He resolves it by shifting the unit of analysis to 1848-71 (p. 247), but that makes the achievements those of the revolution's enemies.
- **Tension: peasants.** They are said to have failed the revolution (p. 209), yet peasants stormed offices in Sicily (p. 181), were among the insurgent forces on the Lower Rhine in May 1849 (p. 201), and joined the Polish rising of 1863 in numbers (p. 231). Evans covers this with the qualifier that revolts occurred where grievances remained (p. 210).
- **Tension: working class or artisans?** The crowds are called the new working class (pp. 187, 208), but the Luxembourg Commission's demands are called typically artisanal (p. 190) and the Saxon figures are almost all artisan categories (p. 208).
- **Tension: nationalism benign or aggressive?** The warning against reading aggressiveness back into the 1830s and 1840s (p. 171) sits beside Magyarization debated in 1843-4 (pp. 191-192), Jordan's remark on Posen (p. 193) and the German deputies' threat of force over Bohemia (p. 194).
- **Tension: socialism marginal or influential?** Street demonstrations were influenced by Utopian socialist ideas (p. 179), but socialism was in the end marginal to 1848 (p. 249).

## Open questions

- How far does the single-period thesis (p. 247) depend on hindsight? Evans himself notes that few in 1849 foresaw Habsburg decline (p. 206).
- The claim that all the centres of 1848 lay in areas affected by British industrial competition (p. 179) is asserted with two examples (Borsig, Bohemian textiles) and no general evidence. Does it hold for Palermo, Naples or Budapest?
- Is *first modern dictator* (p. 217) an analytical category or a flourish? The criteria offered are plebiscitary legitimacy, managed elections, bureaucratic rule and police expansion. Check how ch07 or ch08 use the idea.
- What made the Prussian and Austrian armies stay loyal when National Guards wavered? Evans notes the rural origin of Prussian troops (p. 209) but does not develop it.
- Britain's role is double: the model for Continental liberals (pp. 176, 208, 214) and a power that exported the costs of calm to its colonies (pp. 213-214). Evans juxtaposes these without drawing the conclusion.
- Women appear at the start (Tristan) and the end (the clubs of 1848) but not in the narrative of the revolutions; feminism is called marginal (p. 249). Later chapters should show whether the re-emergence promised on p. 249 is followed up.
- Apparent slips to check against a second history before quoting: the flight to Varennes is attributed to Louis XIV (p. 186) though Louis XVI is named correctly on p. 187; Cavour is called a Protestant (p. 173); Lamartine is said to have been appointed President in February 1848 (p. 190); the Bavarian armoury is stormed on 4 February 1848, before the Paris revolution that supposedly triggered it (p. 182); the pre-parliament is placed at Mannheim (pp. 188, 194) after being organized from Heidelberg (p. 182); the King of the Two Sicilies is spelled both Ferdinando Carlo and Fernando Carlo (pp. 181, 228). These do not affect the argument but matter for citation.
- As the history-genre guidance suggests, this is one witness. A second account of 1848 with a social-history or peasant focus would test the weight Evans puts on liberal fear and military force.
