# ch07: The Challenge of Democracy (pp. 489–570)

## Question this chapter answers

How did the entry of the masses into politics between about 1870 and 1914 (wider franchises, organised labour and socialism, peasant mobilisation, feminism, national minorities) change the political systems of Europe, and why did the apparently irresistible advance of democracy leave those systems less liberal and less stable instead of more?

Evans's answer: the extension of the vote was conceded mostly out of fear of revolution or from conservative miscalculation, and it did draw the masses into politics. But the voters it brought in did not support the middle-class liberals who had built parliamentary government. They went to socialists, Catholic parties, peasant movements and nationalists. Liberalism declined almost everywhere; national minorities used the new mass politics to paralyse multinational states; and in the central and eastern empires real decisions stayed with, or moved back to, monarchs, courts and armies. The title has two senses, and Evans uses both: democracy as a challenge to the existing order, and democracy as a thing that was itself in difficulty by 1914.

## Section guide

- **The Final Frontier (pp. 489-499)**: opens with Emmeline Pankhurst's arrest in May 1914, then surveys women's suffrage movements in Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Hungary, Bohemia, Norway, Finland, Denmark and Sweden. Argues that nationalism decided where the cause succeeded, and that the movements were bourgeois and liberal.
- **The Rise of the Welfare State (pp. 499-504)**: strikes and trade union growth before 1914; Bismarck's insurance laws and their imitators; the shift of poor relief from Churches to secular bodies. Presents welfare as a response to the socialist threat that partly worked.
- **The Second International and its Rivals (pp. 504-517)**: the SPD and its ideology, revisionists, pragmatists and the left; socialism in Austria, Sweden, Britain and France; anarchist terrorism in France, Italy, Spain and Germany; socialist women's movements. Ends by ranking organised socialism as perhaps the strongest democratising pressure.
- **The Age of the Masses (pp. 517-521)**: defines democracy, tabulates franchise extension country by country, explains why it was conceded, and describes the politicisation of the peasantry (Germany, France, Ireland).
- **The Crisis of Liberalism (pp. 521-530)**: Britain from the Whigs through Gladstone, Disraeli, Chamberlain, Salisbury and Asquith. Ireland is the point where democracy breaks liberalism; ends with the pre-war crisis of 1910-14.
- **Nationalism and Democracy (pp. 530-536)**: Austria-Hungary. Franchise reform in Austria fragments the Reichsrat by nationality; Magyar liberals in Hungary refuse reform and Magyarize; in both halves government ends up ruling by decree.
- **The Illusion of Instability (pp. 536-547)**: Italy and France. Rapid turnover of governments, corruption, the would-be dictators Pelloux and Boulanger, the Dreyfus Affair; then the argument that both systems were stable underneath.
- **'Full Steam Ahead!' (pp. 547-555)**: Imperial Germany. Bismarck's gamble on universal male suffrage, the Reichstag's slow gain in weight, the turn to the right in 1878-9, the decline of the liberal parties, Prussia's dominance, Wilhelm II and his Chancellors, the nationalist opposition.
- **The Politics of Extremes (pp. 555-566)**: closes the German account (Zabern), then Russia: intelligentsia, Populists, terrorism, the assassination of Alexander II, reaction under Alexander III, Nicholas II, the Marxists and Lenin, war with Japan, 1905, the Dumas, Rasputin.
- **'The Americanization of the World' (pp. 566-570)**: the chapter's conclusion (democracy carrying the seeds of its own destruction), followed by European images of America and the turn of domestic politics towards empire, which leads into ch08.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Democracy | p. 517 | Defined conditionally as resting above all on universal adult suffrage plus the responsibility of government to parliament and electorate. Both halves matter: Germany had the first (for men) without the second (pp. 548, 556). |
| Challenge of democracy | p. 499 | Pressure from below on existing political systems: organised working-class interests (p. 499), feminism, and in Russia the intelligentsia (p. 556). Not a settled condition but a contest. |
| Suffragettes / suffragists | p. 490 | *Suffragettes* is the Daily Mail's label for the militant Women's Social and Political Union (p. 490); the moderate National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies was far larger (p. 493). |
| Equal suffrage | p. 499 | Votes for women on the same terms as men, which under property franchises meant only a small propertied minority of women; contrasted with universal adult suffrage (pp. 495, 499). |
| Welfare state | p. 499 | State insurance against sickness, accident and old age, plus regulation of work, introduced from the 1880s. For Evans in essence a response to left-wing politics (p. 504). |
| State socialism | p. 502 | Bismarck's own name for his welfare measures, linked in his mind to aristocratic paternalism. |
| Anarcho-syndicalism | p. 500 | The doctrine of overthrowing the capitalist state through a universal general strike (Spanish CNT, French CGT). |
| Revisionism / pragmatists | p. 506 | Revisionists (Bernstein) argued Marx's predictions had failed and the SPD should convert to liberal reformism; they lost. Pragmatists, to whom ideology was irrelevant, were more numerous and influential. |
| Propaganda by the deed | p. 512 | Anarchist direct action: destabilising the state by individual violence and terrorism. |
| Age of the masses | p. 517 | The condition, reached in almost every country by 1900, in which parties of every kind competed for mass allegiance. |
| Nationalization of the masses | p. 521 | The drawing of newly enfranchised and literate populations into national (and minority-national) identity; Evans puts the phrase in inverted commas and gives no source. Reused at pp. 536 and 567. |
| Liberal hegemony | p. 521 | Dominance of parliamentary politics by middle-class or aristocratic liberals under restricted franchises; its decay is the most obvious consequence of franchise extension. |
| Tory Democracy | p. 524 | Disraeli's remaking of the Conservatives as a modern party in organisation and ideology, appealing to patriotism. |
| Home Rule | p. 525 | A separate Irish parliament within the United Kingdom; the issue that split the Liberals in 1886 and returned in 1912 (pp. 526, 529). |
| Curiae | p. 531 | The class-based electoral colleges (landowners, towns, chambers of commerce, rural communities, later a fifth general one) that chose the Austrian Reichsrat until 1907. |
| Magyarization | p. 535 | Imposition of Magyar as the language of administration, justice, place names and secondary schooling in Hungary. |
| Trasformismo | p. 539 | The art of converting a failing coalition into a winning one by changing its composition. Evans attaches it to Giolitti. |
| Solidarism | p. 543 | The centrist Republican alliance of Radicals and Opportunists after Boulanger, which passed France's social legislation. |
| Camarilla | p. 553 | Wilhelm II's court circle, credited by critics with more power than it probably had. |
| Intelligentsia | p. 556 | A group defined by civic activism, especially in literature, not by education alone; in Russia the carrier of the democratic challenge in the absence of a large middle class. |
| Narodniki (Populists) | p. 559 | Students who went to the people in 1874 to convert peasants to revolution. |
| Zemstva | p. 562 | Elected local government bodies created under Alexander II; their professional employees became the base of Russian liberalism. |
| Democratic centralism | p. 563 | The Bolshevik doctrine that, in Evans's words, legitimised an autocratic system of party administration. |
| Americanization | p. 568 | Term coined by W. T. Stead (1901); used for the growing American influence on European economy, culture and political imagination. |

## Leading propositions

- Female suffrage was the last frontier of democracy; by 1914 feminists had put it on the agenda almost everywhere but won it in very few places (pp. 496, 498-499).
- Nationalism was the most important factor in the fate of women's suffrage: where nationalism was authoritarian and masculinist (Germany, Hungary) it blocked the cause; where a subordinate nation sought legitimacy against a foreign ruler (Bohemia, Norway, Finland) it carried it (pp. 496-498).
- Feminist movements were overwhelmingly bourgeois and liberal; equal suffrage meant votes for a propertied few, and the argument shifted from women's equality with men to women's difference from them (p. 499).
- Suffragette militancy brought publicity but may have made it harder for government to concede; the comparison with fascism fails because the violence was aimed at property, not people (p. 492).
- The welfare state was a response to working-class politics, intended to draw workers away from socialism (pp. 499, 503, 504).
- It partly succeeded: it did not stop workers voting socialist, but it gave them a stake in existing society and helped make socialist practice more moderate than socialist programmes (pp. 502, 504).
- The SPD, the largest party in the world by 1914, combined revolutionary doctrine with law-abiding, organisation-building practice, and was increasingly paralysed (pp. 505-506).
- Within the SPD the pragmatists and the trade unions, not Bernstein's revisionists, were what moved the party towards reform (pp. 506-507).
- State discrimination against Social Democrats fed the party's left wing (p. 507).
- Anarchist terrorists were few; their main effect was to frighten bourgeois society and provoke repression (p. 512).
- Socialist parties' commitment to women's suffrage was never more than token (p. 516).
- Organised socialism was perhaps the most powerful of the pressures for democratisation in the quarter-century before 1914 (p. 516).
- By the second half of the century the tide seemed to flow inexorably towards democracy; 1848's defeat was not liberalism's defeat (p. 517).
- Franchise extension owed much to fear of revolution, to defeat in war or revolution, and to conservatives who wrongly expected the new voters to outflank the liberals (p. 519).
- Mass politics reached the peasantry around the turn of the century (pp. 519-520).
- In Britain and Austria-Hungary the extension of the franchise empowered national minorities, who fastened on the decay of liberal hegemony and threatened the state itself (p. 521).
- The Irish Question was the point at which democracy came into conflict with liberalism; it ended Liberal hegemony in 1886 and threatened civil war in 1914 (pp. 525, 526, 530).
- The Parliament Act of 1911 was a major step forward for democracy, but the advance was running into difficulties by 1914 (pp. 529, 530).
- In Austria universal male suffrage (1907) fragmented the Reichsrat and so reduced the influence of elected bodies, moving power to court, ministers and military (pp. 530, 533-534).
- In Hungary the Liberals refused to widen the vote for fear of the minorities, Magyarized instead, and also ended up ruling by decree (pp. 534-536).
- Italy and France only seemed unstable: continuity of ministers, bureaucracy, a stable electorate and (in France) slow social change sustained them (pp. 540, 545-547).
- Both nevertheless showed how vulnerable parliamentarism was to an authoritarian saviour in a crisis (pp. 539, 542).
- In Italy, the higher the turnout rose, the more seats the liberals lost to the extremes (p. 540).
- Bismarck's gamble on universal male suffrage failed spectacularly, because he did not grasp the scale of industrialisation (pp. 548, 551).
- German liberalism of all kinds declined steadily, to the gain of socialism on the left and political Catholicism on the right (p. 551).
- The Reichstag gained weight over time, but the German system kept decision-making at the top beyond parliamentary reach, above all over the army (pp. 549-550, 552, 555-556).
- Russian opposition tactics were extreme because autocracy left no legal outlet; the zemstvo liberals were potentially a greater threat to the regime than the terrorists (pp. 556, 563).
- After 1905 full autocracy was not restored, but the tsar's repression hollowed out the centre and left democracy squeezed between a violent right and a terrorist left (pp. 565-566).
- Overall: the tide of democracy carried the seeds of its own destruction; liberalism was outflanked by mass movements, and nationalism was no longer an unambiguously liberal force (pp. 566-567).
- Imperial issues became a route to domestic popularity, and national pride fed by mass politics increasingly shaped policy (p. 569).

## Arguments

**A. Why women's suffrage succeeded where it did (pp. 489-499)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 493-494) In France the natural allies, the Republicans, opposed votes for women because they expected women to vote for the Church and monarchy. (pp. 494-495) In Germany the allies were weak, the movement split over equal versus universal suffrage and was pushed onto domestic and Social Darwinist ground by nationalist attack. (p. 496) In Russia it fell with the general roll-back of representative institutions. (pp. 496-497) In Hungary a socially conservative gentry nationalism distrusted a movement led by Jewish pacifists. (pp. 497-498) In Bohemia, Norway and Finland nationalists needed women as transmitters of the national language and as extra legitimacy against an outside ruler.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 496) nationalism was the decisive variable; (p. 499) the movements' class base and the turn to arguments from difference limited what they asked for.
- **Unstated premise**: that the variation across countries is better explained by the character of nationalism than by, for example, the strength of liberal parties or of Catholicism, both of which his own French and German cases also point to.

**B. Welfare as counter-socialism (pp. 499-504)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 499-500) Strikes and union membership rose sharply and became political. (p. 501) This drove governments to social legislation. (pp. 502-503) Bismarck, then other states, then the British Liberals introduced insurance schemes with the stated or evident aim of keeping workers from socialism. (p. 502) Workers kept voting socialist, but may have been kept from the socialist left. (p. 504) Socialist practice proved more pragmatic than socialist programmes.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 504) the welfare state gave workers a stake in the society socialism meant to destroy, so its architects' political aims were to that extent fulfilled.
- **Hedging**: the causal link is stated cautiously (may well have played a part, p. 502; could be said to have been fulfilled, p. 504).

**C. Why socialism democratised without revolutionising (pp. 504-517)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 505-506) Kautsky's evolutionism taught that revolution would come by itself, so the party had only to survive, stay legal and grow. (pp. 506-507) Mass organisation required paid staff, who cared about running the party, and unions, who cared about immediate benefits. (p. 507) Exclusion and harassment kept the party outside the state. (p. 508) The left's reliance on spontaneous mass action had no following.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 516-517) socialism was a democratising force, building a separate world for its members and frightening elites into reform, more than a revolutionary one.

**D. How the franchise was widened and what it did to liberalism (pp. 517-521, 566-567)**

- **Premises**: (pp. 517-519) Male suffrage was widened in nearly every state between 1848 and 1914. (p. 519) The motives were fear of revolution, defeat, and conservative calculation; moderate liberals wanted the vote tied to property or literacy and were forced to give way. (p. 521) Literacy, schooling, standard languages and communications drew the masses into national politics; turnout rose. (pp. 566-567) Workers went to socialists, peasants to Catholic and rural parties, and nationalism gave politics a new vehemence.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 567) middle-class liberalism was outflanked and in decline by 1914 in many polities.

**E. Democracy against liberalism in multinational states (pp. 521-536)**

- **Premises**: (p. 525) The secret ballot freed Irish voters from landlords and returned Home Rulers; (p. 526) the 1884 Act raised them to eighty-six and gave them the balance of power. (p. 526) Home Rule split Whigs and Radicals from Gladstone. (pp. 529-530) After 1910 the Liberals again depended on Irish votes, and Ulster armed. (pp. 533-534) In Austria the 1907 franchise produced some thirty parties and obstruction by language groups. (pp. 534-535) In Hungary a 10 per cent franchise was kept precisely to shut out the minorities.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 536) the nationalization of the masses damaged parliamentarism and state integrity, in Austria-Hungary far more than in Britain.
- **Note**: the Hungarian case shows damage from refusing the franchise, the Austrian from granting it. Evans draws the same conclusion from both.

**F. Apparent instability, real continuity (pp. 536-547)**

- **Premises**: (p. 536) France had forty-nine governments in 1871-1914, Italy thirty-two in 1861-1914. (p. 540) In Italy the same ministers recurred and dominant figures (Depretis, Crispi, Giolitti) governed for years. (pp. 545-546) In France prefects and civil servants, fixed regional loyalties, a large independent peasantry, a tamed Paris and a long-serving Chamber gave continuity; (p. 546) ministers were recycled.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 547) the Third Republic, like Italy, only seemed chronically unstable.
- **Qualification he adds himself**: (p. 540) Italy's system was becoming less manageable as industrial society outgrew a politics built for a small liberal elite.

**G. Germany: participation without responsibility (pp. 547-556)**

- **Premises**: (p. 548) Universal male suffrage was meant to produce monarchical majorities. (pp. 548-551) It produced first National Liberal dominance, then Centre and SPD growth as turnout rose. (pp. 549-550) New Reich offices and legislation shifted weight to the Reichstag. (pp. 551-552) But the SPD was ostracised, the legislature's competence was limited, the states (above all three-class Prussia) kept wide powers and the army escaped budgetary control. (p. 556) A no-confidence vote in 1913 changed nothing.
- **Conclusion**: (pp. 555-556) a vibrant political culture coexisted with a system in which the legislature had limited effect on decisions at the top, as August 1914 would show.

**H. Russia: no legal outlet, therefore extremes (pp. 556-566)**

- **Premises**: (p. 556) No legislature, no parties, restricted discussion, no large middle class. (pp. 557-560) Each clampdown radicalised students further, from reading circles to Populism to assassination. (pp. 560-561) The assassination of 1881 cancelled a planned step towards representation and brought police rule. (p. 564) Defeat by Japan broke the regime's legitimacy and forced the October Manifesto. (p. 565) None of the concessions was sincere; the Dumas were dissolved or rigged.
- **Conclusion**: (p. 566) politics polarised and the creation of democracy looked increasingly unlikely.

## Evidence offered

Evans gives no source for almost everything in the chapter; the book has no footnotes. What can be said about the kinds of evidence:

- **Opening vignette from a photograph and memoir**: Pankhurst's arrest and career, with quotations from her and a daughter (pp. 489-491). Perspective: the militants' own account.
- **Membership and attendance figures** as the measure of movements: suffragists 50,000 against suffragettes 2,000 in 1913 (p. 493); the German women's federation at a quarter of a million (p. 494); French feminist demonstration of 6,000 (p. 494); Russian Union 12,000 (p. 495); SPD over a million members and 110 seats (p. 506); French socialists 90,000 and 102 of 601 seats (p. 511); German socialist women 175,000 (p. 515). No source.
- **Welfare coverage statistics**: 4.3 million in German sickness insurance by 1885; by 1914 over 15 million insured for sickness, 28 million for accidents, a million pensions; only 27 per cent of male workers lived past seventy (p. 502). Dutch state share of poor relief 40 per cent in 1855, 57 per cent in 1913 (p. 503). French limits: nearly nine million people in communes without the administering bodies (p. 543). No source. This is the strongest quantitative support for a thesis in the chapter, because the same paragraph gives both the scale and the limits.
- **Strike and union counts**: France 400,000 strikers in 1906; Spain 35,000 to 84,000; British unions from about one and a half million to over four million (pp. 499-500); Russia 1,450,000 strikers in the first half of 1912 (p. 566).
- **Franchise statistics**: Britain 1,365,000 to 2,446,000 voters in 1867; Belgium 4.4 per cent of males in 1892; Netherlands 14 to 31 per cent (pp. 518-519); Austria 6 per cent in 1873 (p. 532); Hungary about 10 per cent (p. 534); Italy half a million, then two million, then 8.5 million (pp. 536, 537, 540); Third Duma weighting of one elector per 230 landowners, 60,000 peasants or 125,000 workers (p. 565).
- **Election results as evidence of liberal decline**: German seats in 1871 and 1912 by party (p. 551); turnout from 50 to over 80 per cent (p. 551); Austrian Reichsrat of 516 deputies in some thirty parties (p. 533); Duma compositions (p. 565); Italian Socialist gains (pp. 539-540).
- **Government-turnover counts**: forty-nine French, thirty-two Italian, fourteen British, twenty-four Austrian, seventeen Hungarian governments (p. 536); French ministers' repeat service, 217 of 561 serving once and 122 more than four times (p. 546); a quarter of deputies with twenty years' service (p. 546). These carry the argument of the section.
- **Statesmen's own words**: Bismarck on suffrage (p. 548) and on the army (p. 552); Taaffe (p. 533); Hartington on the Whigs (p. 521); Gladstone and Disraeli on each other (p. 522); Salisbury (p. 527); Wilhelm II (pp. 553-554); Nicholas II (pp. 561, 565); Plehve (p. 566). Perspective: rulers and ministers. Used both as evidence of intention and for colour.
- **An official's forecast**: Posadowsky-Wehner in 1896 on industrialisation eroding the monarchy's support (p. 551), used to show the elite understood the failure of Bismarck's gamble.
- **Revolutionaries' words**: Luxemburg (pp. 507-508), Émile Henry and Lucheni (pp. 512, 513), the Catechism of a Revolutionary (p. 558), Karakozov (p. 557). The police chief's eyewitness account of Alexander II's death (p. 560).
- **Novels as markers of opinion**: Zola, James, Conrad on anarchists (p. 512); Turgenev, Chernyshevsky, Dostoyevsky (pp. 557-558).
- **One named historian, unnamed**: the phrase about the strange death of liberal England is credited only to a later historian (p. 530). Evans does not name George Dangerfield.
- **Anecdote**: a large share of the chapter. Forty cows registered to vote at Predappio (p. 536); the Kaiser's practical jokes (p. 553); Chambord's shoelaces (p. 541); the handkerchief vote (p. 535). These illustrate character and corruption; they do not bear the structural claims.
- **Language outrunning evidence**: that nationalism was the most important factor for suffrage (p. 496) is asserted, then illustrated; that welfare kept workers from the socialist left is hedged (p. 502); that Crispi set a precedent for Mussolini (p. 538) and that suffrage ran turnout against liberals everywhere (p. 540) are stated without comparison data.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Back to ch01 and ch03**: the claim that nationalism up to 1848 was an unambiguously liberal force (p. 567) and that the roll-back of 1848 did not defeat liberalism (p. 517) depend on the earlier chapters' account of liberal nationalism and of the revolutions. Check whether ch03 presents 1848 nationalism as that unambiguous.
- **Back to ch02 and ch04**: peasant politicisation (pp. 519-520) and the Russian peasant rising of 1905 (p. 564) continue ch02's peasant revolts; the labour movement here presupposes the making of the working class in ch02 and the social history of ch04. The Poor Law of 1834 is said to be undermined by Liberal welfare (p. 503), which links to ch02's account of it.
- **Back to ch06**: the chapter opens directly after the Rite of Spring riot (p. 489); antisemitism, Social Darwinism and the retreat from universal principles to Volk and race (pp. 495, 567) are treated here only as political effects.
- **Forward to ch08**: the last section says imperial questions were becoming central to domestic politics and that the imperial card bought popularity (p. 569); Giolitti's colonial ventures are flagged as disastrous (p. 540); Plehve's remark on a small victorious war (p. 566) is the domestic-politics theory of war in miniature. August 1914 is anticipated at pp. 530, 534 and 556.
- **Tension (my observation)**: the franchise is blamed both ways. In Austria granting it paralysed parliament (pp. 533-534); in Hungary refusing it did (pp. 534-536). The common cause in Evans's own account is national conflict, not the franchise.
- **Tension (my observation)**: Evans calls socialism perhaps the most powerful pressure for democratisation (p. 516) and also lists working-class support for socialism among the things that outflanked liberalism and so helped democracy undo itself (pp. 566-567). The two fit only if democracy and liberal parliamentarism are kept apart, which the chapter does at p. 525 but not consistently.
- **Tension (my observation)**: the section on Italy and France argues stability (p. 547), yet says Italy was becoming progressively less manageable (p. 540) and twice reaches forward to Mussolini (pp. 538, 539). The thesis holds better for France.
- **Tension (my observation)**: Germany is described as moving towards reliance on parliamentary majorities (p. 555) and also as a system where parliament had limited effect (p. 556). Evans holds both; he does not say which tendency was winning in 1914, beyond noting polarisation (p. 555).
- **Teleology**: the chapter repeatedly reads forward to fascism, Weimar, the Russian Revolution and the post-1918 roll-back of democracy (pp. 492, 538, 539, 547, 555, 566, 567). These are assertions of connection, not arguments.
- **Possible slips to check against other sources (my observation)**: the Russo-Japanese peace is said to have been brokered in London by the United States (p. 564); the Benevento rising is dated both two years after 1874 and 1877 (p. 513); Parnell's imprisonment, the Phoenix Park murders and the forged letters are compressed into 1882 (p. 526); Austria's 1859 defeat is said to be by Italy and France (p. 531); the autocracy is called unaccountable to no one but itself (p. 556); trasformismo is credited to Giolitti alone (p. 539).

## Open questions

- Is nationalism really a separate cause of suffrage outcomes (p. 496), or a proxy for whether a national movement needed mass legitimacy against a foreign ruler? Evans's successes are all subordinate nations.
- How much did welfare actually moderate socialism (pp. 502, 504)? Evans offers the intention and the outcome but no test separating welfare from rising wages, union growth or party bureaucracy, which he also cites (pp. 506-507).
- The chapter defines democracy by suffrage plus responsible government (p. 517). By that test which states were democracies in 1914? Evans names France as unusual in having both (p. 540) but gives no list.
- What is the source of the phrase nationalization of the masses (p. 521)? Evans uses it as a term of art without attribution.
- Does the claim that rising turnout cost liberals seats to the extremes (p. 540) hold outside Italy and Germany (p. 551)? Britain 1906 and 1910 (pp. 528-529) look like counter-cases.
- Is the stability argument for France and Italy (pp. 545-547) an argument about democracy or about oligarchy? Evans himself calls the Republic an oligarchy of professional politicians (p. 546).
- Southern and south-eastern Europe get a few lines each (Spain, Portugal, Serbia, Romania, Greece: pp. 517-519). The synthesis should not treat the chapter's conclusions as tested there.
- Why does the conclusion sit under the heading of Americanization (pp. 566-570)? The America material is brief, impressionistic and only loosely tied to the argument; it mainly serves as a bridge to empire.
