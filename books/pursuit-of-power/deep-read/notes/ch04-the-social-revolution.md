# ch04: The Social Revolution (pp. 253-327)

## Question this chapter answers

How did the structure of European society change between about 1850 and 1914, and why did socialism and anarchism find millions of adherents in those decades (the question is set at the end of chapter 3, p. 253)? Evans's answer: industrialization, urbanization and the growth of the state dissolved the legally defined society of orders and replaced it with a society of classes defined by wealth and occupation. The landed aristocracy lost its power and merged with rich commoners into a new elite; a large and varied bourgeoisie set the values of the age; a lower middle class was remade rather than destroyed; and a permanent, hereditary proletariat formed at the bottom. This was a real social revolution, but a gradual one driven by economic change and state-building, not the violent overturning that Marx or Bakunin imagined (p. 326).

## Section guide

- **The Decline of the Aristocracy** (pp. 253-260). Opens with the memoir of Hermynia von Zur Mühlen and her marriage into the Baltic German nobility; then surveys noble numbers, ennoblement, marriage with new money, and the loss of noble hold over armies, bureaucracies, parliaments and local rule. Ends by rejecting the thesis of a persisting old regime.
- **The New Elite** (pp. 260-270). Large estates, the grain-price fall of the 1870s, mechanization, fertilizers, tariffs, the sale of noble land to commoners, noble investment in industry, and the fusion of titled and untitled wealth into one upper class. Closes with Fontane and Lampedusa.
- **The Workshop of the World** (pp. 270-277). British dominance in manufacture and shipping at mid-century, then the uneven spread of industry: Germany's rapid rise, France's interrupted growth, the weak southern periphery, Austria-Hungary, and Russia under Witte.
- **The Second Industrial Revolution** (pp. 277-282). Britain's relative decline; German lead in chemicals and electricity; cartels and banks; hydroelectric industrialization in Italy, Sweden and Norway; science and the state; summary statistics on the fall of agricultural employment.
- **Building the New Jerusalem** (pp. 282-293). Urban growth, slums, foul water and filth; then sanitation, sewers, water supply (Chadwick, Bazalgette, the Lindleys), Haussmann's Paris, housing, lighting, trams, underground railways, monumental capitals; limits of improvement.
- **The Triumph of the Bourgeoisie** (pp. 293-301). Formation of the urban middle class through associations; economic and educated bourgeoisie; professionalization of law, civil service, medicine, engineering, teaching; the Russian exception; what it meant to be bourgeois; fin-de-siècle doubts.
- **The Petty Bourgeoisie** (pp. 301-305). Clerks, teachers, shopkeepers, department stores, small masters; the *Mittelstand* and its politics; women's work as the line between lower and established middle class.
- **'Nothing to Lose but their Chains'** (pp. 305-313). Size of the working class, inequality in income, height, health and death; food and adulteration; unstable employment, hours, accidents; women's work and domestic service; rural labour and flight from the land; formation of a hereditary proletariat.
- **The 'Dangerous Classes'** (pp. 313-319). Fear of an urban criminal class; pilfering and professional crime; rural crime, wood theft, charivari, vendetta; the statistical decline of crime; crime in the press and fiction.
- **The Great Exodus** (pp. 319-327). Political and above all economic emigration, country by country; population growth; the first age of globalization; the chapter's summary conclusion and the bridge to chapter 5.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decline of the aristocracy | p. 253 (heading), p. 260 | Loss of political power and legal distinctness by the titled landowning nobility, caused mainly by the growing state taking over the seigneur's functions, plus franchise extension and economic change. A loss of power and economic identity, not of prestige. |
| Persistence of the old regime | p. 260 | A thesis of *some historians* (Evans names none) that aristocratic dominance lasted to 1914. Evans rejects it flatly. |
| Embourgeoisement | p. 259 | The steady replacement of nobles by commoners in officer corps and bureaucracies. |
| New elite / hybrid social elite | p. 267, p. 326 | An upper class based above all on wealth, mixing large landowners, bankers, industrialists and investors, titled and untitled, sharing one style of life; its values are bourgeois (thrift, hard work, sobriety, responsibility). |
| Order, class, status group | pp. 268-269 | An order is fixed by legal privilege; a class is defined economically. Evans holds the aristocracy was always also a class (wealth from land), and in the nineteenth century became ever more a status group: prestige without a distinct economic base. |
| Workshop of the world | p. 270 | Britain's mid-century position, producing over 40 per cent of traded manufactures and dominating a global division of labour; by 1914 no longer alone (p. 326). |
| Vertical combination / integration | p. 273, p. 280 | Firms uniting coal, ore, smelting and engineering to cut costs; typical of the Ruhr and of Russian metallurgy (p. 276). |
| Second industrial revolution | p. 277 (heading), p. 280 | The late-century wave based on chemicals, electricity and science-trained staff, in which Germany and others outpaced Britain. Evans qualifies it: Britain lagged *in parts of it* (p. 280). |
| Organized capitalism | p. 280 | A term of *some historians* for the German interweaving of banks, cartels and large firms, often close to government, which controlled open-market competition. Evans reports it and supports it with cartel figures. |
| Hygienic revolution | p. 308 | The late-century provision of clean water, sewers and food control in towns; the phrase appears in passing, the substance is at pp. 285-288. |
| Bourgeoisie (economic and educated) | p. 293, p. 295 | Literally city-dwellers; in many ways a new class created by industry. Divided into an entrepreneurial stratum living off the market and a professional stratum living off qualifications, increasingly joined by clubs and marriage. |
| Respectability | p. 300 | The outward sign of bourgeois status, won by work, probity and lifestyle rather than title or descent. |
| Intelligentsia | p. 299 | The small educated group, many of them noble, that stood in for a middle class in Russia for most of the century. |
| Petty bourgeoisie / Mittelstand | p. 301, p. 304 | Lower middle class of clerks, teachers, shopkeepers, small masters. Marx thought it doomed; Evans says change both threatened and renewed it. *Mittelstand* is the German self-description as a middle order between workers and the bourgeoisie. |
| Proletariat (permanent, hereditary) | p. 305, p. 312 | Wage-earning manual workers and their families, the largest class in industrial countries; becomes a class in the full sense only when workers stop returning to the land and a second generation is born into it. |
| Landflucht | p. 312 | Prussian term for the flight from the land of young men to better-paid urban work. |
| Dangerous classes / Lumpenproletariat | p. 313 | Contemporaries' image of a separate, irredeemable criminal underclass. Evans treats the separate class as mostly a literary invention. |
| Charivari (rough music, Haberfeldtreiben) | p. 316 | Communal shaming rituals by which villages policed their own rules; evidence that rural society administered its own justice until the state suppressed it. |
| First age of globalization | p. 325 | The peak, just before 1914, of flows of capital, goods, people and ideas between continents, with Europe dominant; mass emigration is its social dimension. |

## Leading propositions

- The aristocracy declined across Europe in the nineteenth century, and the main cause was the state: it abolished feudal corporations and serfdom, imposed equality before the law, professionalized local administration and created elected parliaments (p. 260). The thesis that an old regime persisted to 1914 is wrong (p. 260).
- Nobility was neither ancient nor closed: titles were multiplied by inheritance rules in Russia and Hungary, stripped by rulers, sold, confirmed for fees, and granted to businessmen; old families married new money (pp. 257-259).
- Armies, bureaucracies, cabinets and lower chambers passed increasingly to commoners, and nobles who wanted political power had to campaign for votes (pp. 259-260).
- The fall in grain prices from the 1870s and cheap American and Russian grain broke the economic base of many estates. Mechanization, fertilizers and tariffs helped large market-oriented estates but did not rescue the traditional aristocracy; land passed on a large scale to middle-class buyers (pp. 261-264).
- Nobles who survived did so by becoming capitalists, investing in industry or exploiting minerals, yet they were often outpaced by entrepreneurs of humbler origin (pp. 265-267).
- The result was a new upper class based on wealth, in which titled and untitled shared a lifestyle and even the code of honour; the aristocracy survived as a status group merging into the upper bourgeoisie (pp. 267-269).
- Britain dominated the world economy at mid-century through manufacturing and command of shipping, but industrialization spread unevenly and fed national economic and military power, above all in Germany (pp. 270-273).
- France industrialized slowly, the southern periphery hardly at all, Austria-Hungary and Russia only in patches; Russian heavy industry depended on the state and on foreign capital and was held back by rural poverty (pp. 273-277).
- From about 1880 Britain fell behind, especially in chemicals and electricity, because latecomers adopted the newest methods, employed scientists and organized through banks and cartels. Hydroelectricity let Italy, Sweden and Norway skip the coal stage (pp. 277-282).
- Cities grew faster than they could be housed or cleaned, but from mid-century middle-class pressure and municipal engineering produced clean water, sewers, lighting and transport, with sharp falls in deaths from waterborne disease. Housing lagged, and improvement was incomplete in 1900 (pp. 283-293).
- Urban improvement also served political ends: clearing sites of insurrection in Paris and displaying princely or national majesty in capital cities (pp. 288, 291-292).
- The bourgeoisie was in many ways a new class, formed socially through voluntary associations and consolidated by professional training, self-regulation and intermarriage between business and professional families (pp. 293-298). It developed unevenly and was blocked in Russia by state controls (pp. 299-300).
- Bourgeois status rested on servants, education, public engagement, a suitable home, a non-earning wife and respectability (p. 300). By 1900 the middle classes were still dominant but had to share power with those below them (p. 301).
- Against Marx's forecast, the lower middle class was renewed by clerks, teachers and shopkeepers; small shops multiplied despite department stores, though many were short-lived (pp. 301-304). Its organizations moved to the right, with antisemitism that Evans thinks historians have sometimes overstated (p. 304).
- The working class was the largest class in industrial countries and was separated from those above by gross differences in income, stature, health and life expectancy (pp. 305-306). Nutrition began to improve only in the last two decades of the century (pp. 307-308).
- Shared poverty, oppression and lack of rights bound workers into one class despite internal gradations, as Marx had predicted, once a permanent and hereditary proletariat replaced the first generation of worker-peasants (pp. 312-313).
- The idea of a separate criminal class was mostly a literary conceit; the casual poor moved in and out of illegality (p. 314). Rural society was not peaceful, and crime rates fell as areas urbanized and industrialized (pp. 315-318).
- Some 60 million people left Europe between 1815 and 1914, chiefly for economic reasons, driven by population growth; this migration was the social side of a first age of globalization in which Europe remained dominant (pp. 320, 324-325).
- Taken together these changes amount to a social revolution of major dimensions, different in kind from that imagined by Marx or Bakunin, and one in which bourgeois values came to dominate and filtered down to the lower middle and respectable working classes (p. 326).

## Arguments

- **The aristocracy declined; there was no persistent old regime.**
  **Premises**: (p. 259) commoners took over officer corps, bureaucracies and cabinets; (pp. 259-260) franchise extension and secret ballots ended deference voting; (p. 260) the state abrogated noble self-government, serfdom and manorial justice; (p. 260) noble status had derived its meaning from dominion over serfs.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 260) nobles were left legally little different from other subjects, so the persistence thesis fails. Evans adds that contemporaries all over Europe discussed aristocratic decline before 1900 (p. 260).
- **Economic pressure completed what the state began.**
  **Premises**: (p. 261) grain boom of the 1850s-60s, then a sharp downturn, with British wheat prices halving 1871-1901; (pp. 261-262) modernization needed capital only large estates had; (p. 263) tariffs came partly from landed pressure; (p. 264) these measures shored up large estates as such, not their noble owners.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 264) middle-class purchase of noble land was one of the most widespread social phenomena of post-feudal Europe.
- **Fusion, not replacement, at the top.**
  **Premises**: (p. 264) businessmen bought estates; (p. 265) nobles joined company boards and wealth shifted from land to shares; (pp. 258-259) intermarriage; (p. 268) middle-class men took up duelling and so claimed honour.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 267) a new elite based on wealth; (p. 269) the titled became a status group merging into the upper bourgeoisie. Evans corrects the formula that the aristocracy changed from an order to a class: it had always been a class as well, and what it lost was its economic identity (pp. 268-269).
- **Why Germany overtook Britain.**
  **Premises**: (p. 277) Britain's early investment tied it to older industries and plant; (p. 277) latecomers adopted the latest methods; (p. 279) German firms employed trained scientists from state-funded universities; (pp. 279-280) banks brokered mergers and cartels; (p. 282) the state supported industry through orders, education and law.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 282) Britain was being overtaken, especially in the newer industries, and British awareness of this fed anxieties that turned political and military.
- **Backward agriculture holds industry back.**
  **Premises**: (p. 273) the peasant sector retarded French investment; (p. 274) weak home markets in Spain; (p. 275) rural poverty held down demand in Austria-Hungary; (p. 276) the same in Russia.
  **Conclusion**: outside the north-west, industry was patchy and depended on the state, foreign capital or export of raw produce (pp. 274-276). Evans also argues that German strength itself inhibited neighbours, keeping Austria-Hungary a supplier of raw materials (p. 281).
- **Urban reform as bourgeois self-interest and moral project.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 285-286) the middle classes moved out but still worked in city centres and demanded a healthy environment; (p. 286, p. 287) Chadwick and Lindley linked dirt to crime and rebellion; (pp. 287-288) the Lindley systems cut typhoid deaths, except in unfiltered Hamburg.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 326) sanitation and urban renewal are expressions of the bourgeois values that came to dominate.
- **Class formation at the bottom.**
  **Premises**: (pp. 305-306) the gulf in income and health between workers and the rest; (p. 312) internal gradations were small beside that gulf; (pp. 312-313) workers at first kept ties to the land (Esslingen, Wales, Asturias, St Petersburg), then stayed at their posts and raised a second generation.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 312) one class, much as Marx predicted; (p. 313) with political consequences already visible before 1900. This is the chapter's answer to the question posed at p. 253.
- **Urbanization did not breed crime.**
  **Premises**: (p. 314) the criminal class was a literary construction; (p. 315) official figures understated rural crime and ignored its roots in a clash of values; (p. 316) wood theft was by far the most common offence in Prussia; (pp. 316-317) villages and clans ran their own violent justice; (p. 318) recorded crime fell in Britain, Germany and Wales as they urbanized.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 318) conservatives who idealized the countryside were wide of the mark; (p. 319) the alarmists were wrong.
- **Emigration as safety valve and globalization.**
  **Premises**: (p. 325) Europe's population rose from 188 million in 1800 to 458 million in 1914; (pp. 320-324) land hunger, artisan crisis, famine and persecution pushed, cheap land, kin and shipping lines pulled; (p. 321) German emigration fell once industry absorbed labour in the 1890s; (p. 322) Denmark's co-operative farming kept people at home.
  **Conclusion**: (p. 324) emigration absorbed over a quarter of western Europe's natural increase; (p. 325) it was the social dimension of globalization.

## Evidence offered

What happened (narrative fact, mostly statistics Evans gives without source):

- Baltic nobility: 58 per cent of 2,060 noble marriages in Estonia 1860-1914 were within the corporate nobility; nobles owned 58 per cent of the land in 1914; 79 of 401 manors had passed to commoners by 1902 (p. 255). In 1905, 184 manors were burned and 90 landowners killed (p. 257).
- Noble numbers and ennoblement: about 890,000 legal nobles in Russia; some 64,000 Polish nobles reduced to commoners; 1,129 Prussian ennoblements 1871-1918; 210 Spanish titles 1886-1914; over a hundred marriages of English peers' sons to American heiresses 1870-1914 (pp. 257-259).
- Loss of office: Habsburg bourgeois generals 2 of 37 in 1804, 20 of 39 in 1908; non-noble Prussian officers under 10 per cent in 1806, 70 per cent in 1913; landowners in the Commons 209 in 1874, 78 in 1885 (p. 259).
- Land: 363 titled individuals owned almost a quarter of England in 1873 (p. 260); Esterházy estates of 750,000 acres (p. 261); only a third of East-Elbian noble estates still noble-owned by 1900; up to a third of Spain's surface sold 1836-95 (p. 264); Hungarian gentry estates down from 30,000 to 10,000 between 1867 and 1900 (p. 265).
- Wealth: French fortunes at death held 5 per cent in shares in 1848 and 31 per cent in 1900, while land and houses fell from 58 to 45 per cent (p. 265). Prussian duellists were 44 per cent noble in 1800-69 and 19 per cent in 1870-1914 (p. 268).
- Industry: Britain made over 40 per cent of traded manufactures in 1850 (p. 270); Ruhr coal rose from 2 million tons in 1850 to 114 million in 1913 (pp. 272-273); Austria-Hungary had 6 per cent of European industrial output in 1913 (p. 274); ten of the fifteen firms digging three-quarters of Russian coal in 1899 were foreign-owned (p. 276); British growth slowed from 3 per cent to under 2 per cent after 1880 (p. 277); Germany had 90 per cent of world dyestuff exports against Britain's 2 per cent (p. 279); 275 German cartels by 1900 (p. 280); German steel output per man-year 77 tons against 48 (p. 282). Agricultural employment fell in France from 52 to 42 per cent, in Germany from about 60 to 35, in Britain from 22 to 9 (1850-1900) (p. 282).
- Cities and health: Berlin grew from 172,000 in 1800 to over two million by 1914 (p. 283); Naples slums at 208,000 people per square mile (p. 284); typhoid deaths in Frankfurt fell from 80 to 10 per 100,000 between 1868 and 1883 (p. 287); only 42 per cent of Prussian towns had mains water in 1895 (p. 293).
- Classes: professional and public-service sector in England and Wales 200,000 in 1851, 560,000 in 1891 (p. 296); clerks 129,000 in 1871, 461,000 in 1901 (p. 301); new retail forms under 15 per cent of turnover (p. 303); British incomes in 1867 (p. 305); Sandhurst cadets almost 9 inches taller than Marine Society boys by 1840 (p. 305); life expectancy 50 in Hampstead, 36 in Southwark (p. 306); Hamburg cholera deaths in 1892 at 62, 37 and 5 per thousand by income band (p. 306); 22,771 reported British industrial accidents in 1899 (p. 309); 2.5 million servants in Germany (p. 310).
- Crime: Prussian wood-theft convictions 120,000 in 1836, 373,000 in 1865 (p. 316); 4,300 vendetta murders in Corsica 1821-52 (p. 317); Welsh indictments one per 845 in 1851, one in 2,994 in 1899 (p. 318).
- Migration: Ireland from 8.5 to 6 million in 1848-55 (p. 320); over five million Germans to the USA 1820-1914 (p. 321); 873,000 Italians left in 1913 (p. 324); about 60 million Europeans left 1815-1914 (p. 324).

Kinds of source and whose view they carry:

- **Memoir and autobiography.** Hermynia von Zur Mühlen's memoirs, written in the mid-1930s by a noblewoman turned Communist, carry the opening case (pp. 253-256); her hostility to her husband's caste shapes the picture, and Evans concedes the Baltic nobles were an extreme case (p. 256). Franz Rehbein's 1911 autobiography gives the farm labourer's view (p. 311); Arthur Harding's recollections in old age the criminal's (p. 315); Haussmann's own memoirs the planner's (p. 288).
- **Middle-class observers of the poor.** Engels on Salford (p. 283), Chadwick (p. 286), Mayhew and Booth (pp. 313-314), Munthe on Naples (p. 284), factory inspectors (pp. 283, 309), travel guides (pp. 285, 292-293). The poor mostly appear through reformers', officials' and employers' eyes; Evans notes that such writing often titillated bourgeois readers (p. 314).
- **Official statistics and inquiries.** Censuses, criminal registers, tax and mortality tables, mostly British and German. Evans himself warns that the Russian crime figures were deceptive (p. 315). The Hamburg material (water, cholera, servants' tribunal, dock pilfering, the 1906 riot) is unusually dense and local (pp. 284-288, 306, 310-311, 314-315).
- **Novels as social evidence.** Fontane and Lampedusa for aristocratic decline (pp. 269-270), Dickens, Balzac, Zola, Mann, Gogol, Trollope, Galdós, Serao for slums, clerks, shops, bureaucrats and bourgeois decay (pp. 284, 297, 300-303, 305). These illustrate perceptions; they do not prove the trends.
- **Named historians: none.** Positions are attributed to *some historians* on the old regime (p. 260), order and class (p. 268), organized capitalism (p. 280) and lower-middle-class antisemitism (p. 304). Evans gives no source for any statistic; several are hedged as estimates or as what was said.

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- **Back to ch03.** The chapter is framed as the explanation for why Marxist socialism and Bakuninist anarchism won mass followings after 1870 (p. 253). It supplies the social conditions (class formation, inequality, pp. 305-313) and says the political consequences were already appearing (p. 313), but the politics themselves are left to later chapters.
- **Back to ch01 and ch02.** Serf emancipation and its redemption payments are assumed here as the starting point for both noble decline and noble investment (pp. 260, 264-265). The runaway serf Savva Purlevsky is recalled at p. 299.
- **Forward to ch05.** The last sentence says this new social world had a major and not always positive effect on the natural environment (pp. 326-327). The polluted Ruhr and the Welsh slag heaps already point that way (pp. 272, 285).
- **Decline versus survival.** Evans denies any persistence of the old regime (p. 260), yet also reports that the aristocracy remained a force to be reckoned with (p. 260), held vast estates to 1914 (pp. 260-261), won tariffs and tax favours (p. 263), kept control of local tax assessment in Prussia (p. 263) and mostly weathered the transition (p. 265). The reconciliation offered is that power passed to a mixed elite of wealth; whether that counts as decline or successful adaptation is partly a matter of definition. Lampedusa's line at p. 269 fits the adaptation reading as well as the decline one.
- **Whose values won.** The new elite is said to be based on bourgeois values (p. 326), but the same pages show the bourgeois adopting aristocratic forms: country houses, hunting, duelling, titles (pp. 258, 267-268, 282). Evans calls this a merging of mores (p. 268) and elsewhere reports the British complaint that industrialists turned landowner rather than reinvest (p. 282).
- **Marx right and wrong.** Marx is wrong about the petty bourgeoisie (p. 301) and about the form of the social revolution (p. 326), right about the formation of a single working class (p. 312), and his Lumpenproletariat belongs with the literary images Evans discounts (pp. 313-314).
- **Triumph and unease.** The section title claims a bourgeois triumph, but it closes on decadence novels and on the need to share power (pp. 300-301).
- **State as destroyer and builder.** The state dismantles noble privilege (p. 260), forces industrialization in Russia (p. 275), blocks a Russian middle class (p. 299) and suppresses village justice (p. 318). The growth of state capacity is the thread tying this social chapter to the book's political narrative.
- **Britain as model and laggard.** Britain is the pattern for sanitation, professional self-regulation and civil-service reform (pp. 286, 296-297) while losing industrial leadership (pp. 277-282).

## Open questions

- Which historians is Evans answering on the persistence of the old regime, on order and class, and on organized capitalism (pp. 260, 268, 280)? The text gives no names, so the debates cannot be reconstructed from the chapter alone.
- The fall in recorded crime rests on conviction and indictment figures (p. 318). Evans has just shown that such figures missed much rural crime (p. 315); how far do changes in policing and recording, rather than behaviour, explain the trend? He lists religion, temperance, living standards and education as causes without weighing them (p. 318).
- Several figures look doubtful or are unclear as printed and should be checked before reuse: a British population of roughly 45 million dated to 1868 (p. 305); the unit of the German crimes-against-the-person figures (p. 318); the prize for 23 per cent monthly attendance at Carmaux (p. 308); a 42 per cent rise in German shops in 1905-7 (p. 303); the St Petersburg second-generation percentages, where a word seems to be missing (p. 313); the phylloxera cost stated as 37 per cent of annual output (p. 274). The 1862 American land law appears as the Homeland Act (p. 321) and the curbing of the Lords is dated 1910 (p. 259).
- The chapter shows a working class forming and says this had political consequences (p. 313), but it also says bourgeois values percolated down to the respectable working class (p. 326). How these two claims fit, and whether shared values blunted or sharpened class politics, is left for later chapters.
- Evidence is weighted towards Britain and Germany, with Hamburg prominent. How representative are these for southern and eastern Europe, which Evans mostly characterizes by what they lacked (pp. 274-276, 292-293, 323-324)?
- Women appear as excluded from clubs (p. 295), as household managers (p. 300), as clerical and shop staff (pp. 303-304) and as factory and domestic workers (pp. 309-311), but the chapter draws no overall conclusion about how the social revolution changed women's position.
