# fm01: Preface (pp. 1–13)

Scope note: p. 1 and the top of p. 2 hold the series list, the dedication to Eric Hobsbawm, and the lists of illustrations and maps. The Preface proper runs from p. 2 to p. 13 and is signed Cambridge, May 2016. It is the only place in the book where Evans states his argument in general terms, so this note is the reference point for every chapter note.

## Question this chapter answers

Why treat 1815 to 1914 as one period of European history, how should a history of it be written now that master-narratives are out of favour, and what does the title mean?

## Section guide

The Preface has no headings. Its argument moves in seven steps.

- pp. 2–3: periodisation. Dates are arbitrary; social and economic time runs differently from political time; Mayer's persistence thesis is set aside.
- pp. 3–4: predecessors. Hobsbawm's trilogy and the long nineteenth century; the nation-state framework and how globalisation changed historians' view.
- pp. 4–5: global history. Osterhammel as model and foil; Bayly and Darwin on rough parity around 1700; European hegemony as the justification for the period.
- pp. 5–7: transnational history. Against country-by-country histories; Europe as a region with common characteristics; Acton's plan.
- pp. 7–8: lines of development taken over from Blanning (state, public sphere, communications, economy), and the warning against reading the century as linear progress.
- pp. 8–10: famine, plague and war; inequality and emancipation; the pursuit of power as the common thread, and its reconception in racial terms.
- pp. 10–13: plan of the book, the opening biographies, the absence of notes, sources for long quotations, acknowledgements, conventions for names.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| long nineteenth century | p. 3 | Hobsbawm's name for 1789-1914. Evans grants its influence but calls it broken-backed, split by 1848 into two unlike halves, and does not adopt it. |
| ancien régime économique et social | p. 3 | The Annales idea that the old social and economic order (serfdom, high birth and death rates, marginal industry) lasted into the second half of the century although the political old regime had fallen. |
| persistence of the ancien régime | p. 3 | Arno Mayer's thesis that aristocratic elites stayed dominant to 1914. Evans reports that historians have not widely accepted it. |
| global history | p. 4 | History that links parts of the world instead of telling their separate stories; here, bringing other continents in to explain what happened in Europe (p. 5). |
| global hegemony | p. 5 | Europe's lead over, and dominion in, other parts of the world between 1815 and 1914, gained through specific historical circumstances and not intrinsic superiority. It is the main justification for the period. |
| transnational history | pp. 5–6 | History of what European countries had in common and what wider processes affected them, as against parallel national narratives. |
| Europe | p. 6 | Not a geographical area with fixed edges but a social, economic, political and cultural region with common characteristics, from Britain and Ireland to Russia and the Balkans. |
| master-narrative | p. 7 | An overarching story that binds all themes, such as Hobsbawm's placing of capitalism at the core. Evans says historians of his time can no longer use one. |
| lines of development | p. 7 | Blanning's substitute for a master-narrative: long processes that can be traced without claiming they determine everything. Evans names the march of the state, the public sphere, communications and economic growth. |
| public sphere | p. 7 | The cultural space of periodicals, coffee houses and reading clubs, confined in 1815 to a small educated stratum and widened by 1914 into mass participation. |
| progress | p. 8 | How most contemporaries saw the century: linear and open-ended. Evans treats this as a view to be warned against, because progress had a price. |
| inequality | p. 8 | A running theme: older forms such as serfdom give way to newer ones such as wage labour in the factory. |
| emancipation | p. 8 | The grant of greater equality of status to the rural majority, women and religious minorities, notably Jews; always partial and conditional. |
| power | pp. 9–10 | The book's organising word, used in many senses: state, imperial, military, revolutionary, party, economic, the power of lords over serfs and men over women, power over nature, and physical power sources. It may be formal or informal, violent or persuasive. |
| glory | p. 9 | The priority of the elites of 1648-1815 in Blanning's volume; the value that power displaced. |

## Leading propositions

- Any start and end date is arbitrary, and different kinds of history have different chronologies (p. 2).
- The social and economic old regime outlasted the political one: serfdom ended in most of Europe only in the second half of the century, the demographic transition came after 1850 except in France, and until then industrialisation was confined to small pockets (p. 3).
- Change was nevertheless real in politics and other spheres; Mayer's claim that little of significance changed has not been widely accepted (p. 3).
- The long nineteenth century is split in two by the 1848 Revolutions, which is why historians have struggled to find a unifying theme for it (p. 3).
- Twentieth-century historians put the rise of nation states at the centre; globalisation since the Cold War has moved historians to a global perspective (p. 4).
- Around 1700 the Mughal, Qing, Ottoman and African empires were roughly on a par with Europe; by 1815 Europe had forged ahead because of specific historical circumstances (p. 5).
- Europe kept and extended that advantage until the early twentieth century; the First World War called it into question and the Second destroyed it (p. 5).
- European global hegemony is the main justification for treating 1815-1914 as a distinct period (p. 5).
- National-history formats lose sight of what bound Europe together; Europe had a definable existence as a collective entity (p. 6).
- A real European history became possible again only with the fall of communism, EU enlargement and renewed globalisation (p. 6).
- The history of Europe can no longer be equated with national politics and international relations; the field now covers almost every human activity, most recently landscape and environment (p. 7).
- Without a master-narrative the most a historian can do is trace lines of development (p. 7).
- Two of Blanning's lines, the march of the state and the growth of the public sphere, continued to an extent unthinkable in the eighteenth century (p. 7).
- By 1914 the state had been transformed in two directions at once: mass participation through suffrage and parties, and far greater control over citizens in education, health, military service and social work (p. 7).
- Communications and the economy accelerated beyond eighteenth-century imagining: railway, telegraph, steamship and photograph were barely in view in 1815; telephone, motor car, radio and cinema had arrived by 1914 (p. 7).
- The same years brought the machine gun, tank, submarine and fighter aircraft, the first aerial bombardment (Libya, 1911) and the first European concentration camps in South Africa and South-West Africa (p. 8).
- The century should not be read as its inhabitants read it, as linear progress; progress had its price, paid between 1914 and 1949 (p. 8).
- The century was relatively free of major European famines, plagues and wars, and the changed relationship with the rest of the world was an important cause (p. 8).
- Inequality did not disappear; its forms shifted (p. 8).
- The century was the age of emancipation above others, but equality and emancipation were only partial and conditional (p. 8).
- Disputes about inequality were at the centre of politics, and every doctrine, conservative ones included, joined the debate (pp. 8–9).
- What all these strands shared was a desire to acquire and wield power in order to act on their ideas; hence the title (p. 9).
- The central social process was the emancipation of the oppressed from their oppressors, most widely the emancipation of women (p. 9).
- Society also increased its power over nature (p. 9).
- People increasingly put power before glory and honour, and by the end of the century power was being reconceived in racial terms (pp. 9–10).
- Most Europeans still lived in and off the countryside in 1914, and histories built around industry wrongly push them to the margin (pp. 10–11).
- The book's originality lies in its arguments, interpretations and the range and juxtaposition of themes, not in new research (p. 11).

## Arguments

- **Premises**: [p. 2] dates that matter in political history may mean little in social or economic history + [p. 3] serfdom, the old demographic pattern and pre-industrial economy lasted past mid-century + [p. 3] yet political and other change was real.
  **Conclusion**: [p. 3] no single date divides the century cleanly, and neither "nothing changed" (Mayer) nor an age of revolution ending in 1848 captures the whole.
- **Premises**: [p. 5] major world civilisations were roughly equal around 1700 + [p. 5] by 1815 Europe led and it held the lead until the early twentieth century + [p. 5] the two world wars ended it.
  **Conclusion**: [p. 5] 1815-1914 is a meaningful unit because it is the century of European global hegemony, and events on other continents must be brought in to explain Europe.
- **Premises**: [p. 6] country-by-country histories hide common processes + [p. 6] Europe shared social, economic, political and cultural characteristics + [p. 7] historical research now covers nearly every human activity.
  **Conclusion**: [pp. 6–7] the book should be transnational and thematic, and cannot be a history of high politics alone.
- **Premises**: [p. 7] Hobsbawm unified his themes through capitalism + [p. 7] grand master-narratives have fallen into disrepute.
  **Conclusion**: [p. 7] the historian traces lines of development instead. Evans then supplies a common thread all the same, in the next argument.
- **Premises**: [pp. 8–9] every political and religious doctrine of the century proposed a remedy for inequality or a defence of hierarchy + [p. 9] each needed power to put its ideas into action + [p. 9] states, armies, parties, businessmen, unions, feminists and artists all sought power in their own fields + [p. 9] society extended its power over nature.
  **Conclusion**: [pp. 9–10] the pursuit of power permeated the century and is the theme at the centre of the book.
- **Premises**: [p. 7] technical and political advance was real + [p. 8] the same advance produced new weapons, aerial bombing and colonial concentration camps.
  **Conclusion**: [p. 8] the century must not be treated as linear progress.

## Evidence offered

The Preface argues from historiography, not from primary evidence.

- Named historians as positions to accept, adapt or reject: Blanning (framework and title, pp. 2, 7, 8, 9), the Annales school (p. 2), Mayer (rejected, p. 3), Hobsbawm (admired, not followed, pp. 3, 7), Sperber and Alexander (examples of the missing unifying theme, p. 3), Osterhammel (the stated main influence, with a criticism, pp. 4–5), Bayly and Darwin (the parity thesis, p. 5), Ferguson (rejected, p. 5), Acton (precedent for transnational history, p. 6).
- The parity of civilisations around 1700 is asserted on Bayly's and Darwin's authority, with no data given here (p. 5).
- Contrasts of 1815 and 1914 serve as illustration: inventions, Einstein, Picasso, Schoenberg (p. 7); weapons, Libya 1911, the camps in southern Africa (p. 8).
- Sources: the book has no footnotes or endnotes, by series format (p. 11). Evans names the sources of the eight opening biographies and of a handful of long quotations taken from secondary works; other long quotations are said to come from original sources (p. 11). He states that he relies principally on the work of others (p. 11).
- Whose perspective: the opening biographies are chosen to balance four men and four women from eight countries (p. 10).

## Tensions / links to other chapters

- Evans says master-narratives are no longer available (p. 7) and then offers power as what every strand had in common (p. 9). The word is used so broadly, from steam power to patriarchy (p. 9), that it works as a title and a recurring motif more than as an explanation. Whether any chapter uses it to explain something is a question for the synthesis.
- The claim that the century was relatively free of famines, plagues and wars, and the promise to explain why (p. 8), is taken up in ch02 (hunger), ch05 (disease) and ch01 and ch03 (the international order).
- Emancipation and its limits (p. 8): serfs in ch02, women in ch06 and ch07, Jews in ch06, ch07 and ch08.
- The transformed state (p. 7): control over citizens in ch05, mass participation in ch07.
- European hegemony and racial thinking (pp. 5, 10): ch08.
- The countryside (pp. 10–11): ch02 and ch04.
- The plan on p. 10 says each chapter has ten sections. In this edition ch02 has eleven headings.

## Open questions

- The specific historical circumstances that put Europe ahead by 1815 are asserted but not named in the Preface (p. 5). They belong to the previous volume's period; does Evans return to them?
- If power displaced glory and honour (pp. 9–10), what is the evidence that people ranked values this way, as distinct from historians naming volumes?
- With no notes, which interpretations are Evans's own and which are borrowed cannot be told from the text. He says so himself (p. 11).
