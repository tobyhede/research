# bm01: Illustration captions and Further Reading (pp. 647–657)

Scope note: the last lines of ch08 fall on p. 647. The plate captions run pp. 648–651, Further Reading pp. 651–657, and the copyright page closes p. 657. The plates are images and were not extracted; only their captions are in the text. This unit is reference material and carries no argument of its own, so the note is short.

## Question this chapter answers

What visual evidence did Evans choose to accompany each chapter, and which secondary literature does he point the reader to in place of notes?

## Section guide

- pp. 648–651: captions to 32 plates, in chapter order as the Preface says (p. 12). Each caption is a small piece of interpretation, not a bare label.
- pp. 651–657: Further Reading in seven groups: General Histories, National Histories, War and Peace, Biography, Politics, Economy and Society, and Nature, Culture, Gender and Religion.
- p. 657: publisher's end matter and copyright notice (2016); maps by Andras Bereznay.

## Key terms

| Term | First used | Author's meaning |

| year without a summer | p. 648 | 1816, when emissions from the eruption of Mount Tambora in April 1815 brought widespread famine in Europe (caption 1). |

## Leading propositions

- The plates follow the order of the chapters and were chosen to add to the topics discussed (p. 12).
- Caption 1 ties the post-1815 hunger to a volcanic eruption on the other side of the world, the book's first instance of a global cause for a European event (p. 648).
- Caption 4 reads Turner's painting of a sailing warship towed by a steam tug as a symbol of the arrival of a new age (p. 648).
- Captions 29 to 32 close the sequence with colonial war in South-West Africa, the Balkan Wars and the Sarajevo assassination, the same order in which ch08 ends (p. 651).
- Caption 30 points to trenches, an aeroplane, heavy artillery and khaki in 1913 as a preview of 1914 (p. 651).
- The reading list is weighted towards national histories and biography: on a rough count about 60 entries under National Histories and about 50 under Biography, against about 10 General Histories (pp. 651–657). For a book that argues for transnational history (p. 6), the literature it rests on is still largely national.
- The general histories listed include the works the Preface argues with: Bayly, Hobsbawm's trilogy, Osterhammel and Sperber (pp. 651–652).

## Arguments

None. The captions assert; the list recommends.

## Evidence offered

- Paintings, lithographs, cartoons, a hydrographic map, photographs and a newspaper sketch, each dated and attributed in its caption (pp. 648–651).
- Perspective: mostly the work of professional artists and the illustrated press, so the plates show how events were pictured for the educated and newspaper-reading public. Caption 32 says the Sarajevo sketch derives from an eyewitness report (p. 651).
- One slip to be aware of: caption 5 attributes the 1861 emancipation proclamation to Tsar Alexander I (p. 648). The tsar in 1861 was Alexander II. This is the edition's text, not an extraction fault.

## Quotes

- "showing the skies turned yellow by the sulphur emissions from Mount Tambora" (p. 648) Evans, caption 1.
- "Note the trenches, the Ottoman aeroplane, the use of heavy artillery" (p. 651) Evans, caption 30.
